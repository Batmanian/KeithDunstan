Timeline

Every transcribed article in the archive, alongside Keith Dunstan's published books and key life events, in the order they appeared — from his birth through to his final columns in The Age.

03 Feb 1925 Born in East Malvern Keith's Life

Born on the kitchen table at 13 Coppin Street, East Malvern, to journalist and Victoria Cross recipient William Dunstan and his wife Marjorie.

1929 Family moves to Toorak Keith's Life

Aged four, moves with his family to 20 Wallace Avenue, Toorak, the home he would describe as a cosy womb and leave only reluctantly for boarding school a decade later.

1932 Enters Wadhurst, Melbourne Grammar's prep school Keith's Life

Aged seven, begins at Wadhurst, the preparatory school of Melbourne Grammar, an Anglican school founded in 1849. A left-hander, he is beaten into writing right-handed by his form teacher Miss Baker.

1937 Moves to Melbourne Grammar senior school Keith's Life

Transfers from Wadhurst into the senior school; consistently near the bottom of the class in mathematics, placed on a daily report card system by headmaster Mr Caffin, and caned regularly.

13 Jan 1939 Black Friday bushfires at Lorne Keith's Life

Victoria's worst bushfire disaster burns through Airey's Inlet toward Lorne while the family is staying at Erskine House. The family shelters on the beach; his father drives through flames at high speed to reach them.

1939 Begins boarding at Geelong Grammar School Keith's Life

Begins four years as a boarder in Francis Brown House at Geelong Grammar, Corio. Assigned number 62 in a house hierarchy of 68 boys. Discovers Beethoven, studies under Manning Clark, and learns to endure cold showers at 6.30 a.m.

03 Sep 1939 Australia enters the Second World War Keith's Life

Aged 14, listens to Prime Minister Menzies announce war and immediately begins a scrapbook of clippings tracking the conflict, with a particular focus on Royal Air Force aircraft and Battle of Britain kill tallies.

1942 First flight in an aeroplane Keith's Life

Takes his first flight as a cadet in the Air Training Corps, from a flying field near Geelong Grammar, and joins Bob Dalziel in the ATC to secure priority in the RAAF call-up.

1942 Sits Matriculation examinations Keith's Life

Passes Matric in four mathematics subjects, physics and chemistry, after science teacher Charles Cameron provides private tuition from 5 a.m. Cameron's verdict: 'You have no brains at all. But you do have guts.'

03 Feb 1943 Enlists in the Royal Australian Air Force Keith's Life

Joins the RAAF with school friend Bob Dalziel on his 18th birthday. Posted to No. 2 Initial Training School, Bradfield Park, Sydney, where they are immediately patronised as rookies by the trainees who arrived three days earlier.

1943 Pilot training begins at Benalla Keith's Life

Transferred by his father via Air Commodore F. R. W. Scherger to No. 11 Elementary Flying Training School, Benalla, to train as a pilot on Tiger Moths rather than as a wireless air gunner.

1943 Goes solo for the first time Keith's Life

Flies solo at Benalla after nine hours and ten minutes of instruction under Flight Sergeant Bill Dale. One of the happiest moments of his life: 'I'M FREE! I'M FREE! I'M GOING SOLO.'

1943 Advanced training at Deniliquin Keith's Life

Posted to No. 7 Senior Flying Training School, Deniliquin, to fly Wirraways; chooses single-engine training, hoping to be a fighter pilot rather than a bomber crew.

1945 Posted to Morotai Keith's Life

Posted to No. 9 Transport and Movement Office on the Indonesian island of Morotai, a staging point for strikes into Borneo. Works round the clock as shipping officer unloading Liberty ships. The island is the base for Mustang squadrons of 81 Wing.

10 Jun 1945 Lands at Labuan with the assault force Keith's Life

Participates in the amphibious assault on Labuan, Borneo, aboard United States LSTs. Serves as RAAF representative with the Australian Beach Group, Ninth Division. Reaches the rank of Flight Lieutenant.

1946 Discharged from the Royal Australian Air Force Keith's Life

Discharged at No. 1 Personnel Depot at the Melbourne Cricket Ground after three years of service as Flight Lieutenant. Issued with an austerity Victory suit; his father promptly arranges a handmade replacement from family tailor J. Dunstan Lewis.

1946 Joins the Herald and Weekly Times as a cadet journalist Keith's Life

Against his father's advice, approaches Archer Thomas, editor of The Herald, and is hired without telling anyone. His father discovers three days later, but never again calls him a potential hack. Cadet training is four years, rotating between the Herald and the Sun News-Pictorial.

1949 Marries Marie Rose McFadyen Keith's Life

Marries Marie Rose McFadyen, niece of his mother's closest friend Doris McFadyen, whom he had first contacted during the war by posing as an identity card inspector to reach her at Presbyterian Ladies' College.

1949 Foreign Correspondent, New York Keith's Life

Appointed US correspondent for the Herald and Weekly Times. The honeymoon is the posting: a flight across the Pacific in a Boeing Stratocruiser. Sets up home at Forest Hills, Long Island, from where he covers the United Nations, interviews Frank Sinatra, and meets a 'shy, diffident' young Rupert Murdoch.

01 Dec 1950 First child born in New York Keith's Life

First child born at Cornell Hospital, New York, in one of the earliest public experiments in natural childbirth.

1952 Foreign Correspondent, London Keith's Life

Moves from New York to London as the Herald and Weekly Times's British correspondent; the family lives in Wallington, Surrey. Covers the death of Sir Keith Murdoch in October 1952.

1953 Returns to Australia Keith's Life

Sails home from London aboard the P&O liner Strathmore with Marie and two children. Arrives to find that the Herald and Weekly Times has taken up the option to buy the Courier-Mail and editor Colin Bednall has resigned.

1954 Joins the Courier-Mail, Brisbane: 'Day by Day' column Keith's Life

Begins writing a daily column, 'Day by Day', for The Courier-Mail in Brisbane; the family lives in Queensland for approximately four years before returning to Melbourne.

1958 'A Place in the Sun' column begins in the Sun News-Pictorial Keith's Life

Returns to Melbourne and begins a daily column in The Sun News-Pictorial, Melbourne's largest-circulation newspaper. The column runs for twenty years and establishes him as a Melbourne institution.

1962 The paddock that grew Book

Keith's first book, about the origins and history of the Melbourne Cricket Ground, tracing its transformation from a swampy paddock beside the Yarra into Australia's premier sporting arena.

The staid ladies'-only department store Georges opens a men's-wear department for the first time in 82 years, complete with a private-club atmosphere and £250 vicuna coats.

Melbourne wins bragging rights as Australia's "Conference City," hosting the World Power Conference's 1000-plus delegates through a whirlwind of interpreters, transistor earphones, and two 1600-guest banquets in the Exhibition Building.

A tongue-in-cheek peace plan for the Sydney-Melbourne rivalry, sparked by Melbourne's jetport frustrations and a Senate row over an anti-Melbourne pamphlet, complete with a proposed Bolte-Heffron summit in a railway carriage at Albury.

01 Jan 1963 Freedom of Beach Walkabout Magazine

A survey of Australia's unusually open beaches, contrasting the free run of the Australian coastline with the private, fenced-off beaches of the Italian Riviera, Hawaii and California, and tracing the colonial land law and the Bondi bikini scandals that shaped the public's right to walk the sand.

05 Jan 1963 Gather Ye Rosebuds While Ye May The Bulletin

A satirical guide to how Victorians spend their summer by the water, from Portsea's status-symbol boat sheds and Harold Holt's earnest spearfishing hobby to Lake Eildon's flotilla of cabin cruisers and Rosebud's vast camping ground.

12 Jan 1963 Henry Bolte's Brilliant Duck The Bulletin

Premier Henry Bolte stands in as a replacement batsman in a Centenary of International Cricket replay match at the MCG, kitted out in a garish spotted shirt and a learner's L-plate pinned to his back.

19 Jan 1963 Senator Kennelly and the Monster The Bulletin

Albert Park Lake's decade-long infestation of Canadian waterweed defeats every remedy the Albert Park Trust's chairman, Senator Pat Kennelly, throws at it, from an imported weed-cutting boat to a fleet of speedboats dumping tons of copper sulphate.

26 Jan 1963 Under the King Street Bridge The Bulletin

Profiles Whelan the Wrecker, the Melbourne demolition firm founded in 1891 by a bowler-hatted Irish immigrant, now engaged to dismantle three cracked spans of King's Bridge and busily expanding into wrecking Sydney as well.

09 Feb 1963 Mayhem, Rapine and Sin The Bulletin

A gleeful account of radio station 3UZ's Sunday-morning "Newsbeat" bulletin, which thrusts a microphone in front of car-crash victims and brawl survivors moments after the police and ambulance arrive, delivering Melbourne its weekly dose of actionable gossip that the once-lurid Truth newspaper no longer supplies.

23 Feb 1963 About Cigarettes and Gondolas The Bulletin

Surveys Melbourne's booming cigarette trade and Nobel laureate Macfarlane Burnet's lonely campaign against smoking, then reports the mishap befalling Moomba's imported 16th-century Venetian gondola, which shrank so badly out of water that it now refuses to float.

09 Mar 1963 All the Queen's Men The Bulletin

On the exhausted vocabulary of Royal Tour reporting nine years after "obviously" and "radiant" were banned in 1954, and on Melbourne's noticeably cooler, less crowd-mobbed reception for the Queen's 1963 visit compared with her first.

16 Mar 1963 In the Depths of SE 2 The Bulletin

Follows up on used-car dealer Emmanuel Margolin's spectacular financial collapse with a report from the auction of his Toorak household effects, from leopard-skin rugs and a matador suit tailored in Madrid to the Ford Thunderbird he had already bought twice over.

30 Mar 1963 Bunnies in Black Silk Stockings The Bulletin

A dispatch from Melbourne's new Playboy Restaurant, the local answer to Hugh Hefner's American bunny clubs, where five waitresses in bunny tails and 18-inch stockings serve drinks only, chosen from a hundred applicants and forbidden from dating the customers.

06 Apr 1963 Those Lucky OYO Men The Bulletin

A tour of Domain Park, Robin Boyd's new 20-storey "own-your-own-flat" tower opposite the Botanic Gardens, from its Georges furniture and £37,500 penthouses to the view over Government House, the MCG and St Patrick's from its balcony.

Wangaratta throws everything at the Australian Wool Bureau's national publicity contest, from a 500-guest "Wild Colonial" banquet with Sir Robert Menzies as guest speaker to a week-long bullock-wagon re-enactment of Major Mitchell's 1836 expedition down the Hume Highway.

Farewells Melbourne's Glaciarium after fire destroyed the pioneering ice rink, recalling its genteel opening in 1906, orchestras, speed skaters and eventual defeat by St Moritz and changing fashions.

Tours the condemned cells and gallows yard of the old Melbourne Gaol, recalling the 1929 demolition works that turned up bones from Ned Kelly's unmarked grave and the scramble of souvenir hunters that followed, before dropping in on a talkative old lag at the City Watchhouse next door.

A tour of Victoria's ornate Legislative Council chamber and a survey of its obstructive power over Henry Bolte's government, from a killed Liquor Reform Bill to the enduring mystery of the Legislative Assembly's mace, stolen in 1891 and never recovered.

Surveys Melbourne's early-1960s building boom around the corner of Flinders and Swanston Streets, from the Flinders Street Station redevelopment to a new cultural centre, and worries aloud that the one thing left untouched, the century-old Young and Jackson's pub and its nude painting of Chloe, might finally be swallowed up.

A visit to the Queensland oil boomtowns of Tara and Moonie, tracing the district's turn from a prickly-pear-choked backwater once known as 'The Lost Province' into the birthplace of Australia's oil industry, and meeting the selection farmers whose land the oil was found under.

06 Jul 1963 Beastliness — Dyed in the Wool The Bulletin

Covers the Wool Board's annual fashion showcase at Her Majesty's Theatre, where 1964's advertised colour palette, deliberately 'sulky', 'moody' and 'beastly', is played for laughs against the previous year's wholesome wild-colonial theme.

20 Jul 1963 Vengeance on a Black Mud Altar The Bulletin

Recounts the fevered build-up and mud-soaked revenge match after South Australia handed Victoria its first Melbourne Cricket Ground interstate football loss in 37 years, centred on John Peck's on-field haymaker at Adelaide Oval and the lenient VFL tribunal verdict that followed.

Marks Hoyts' golden jubilee against the demolition of Melbourne's old Savoy Theatre, tracing the cinema chain's rise from a Bourke Street dentist's magic-lantern hobby to the picture-palace rivalry between the Hoyts Regent and its rival, the State, and the wave of name changes that followed as the old chains contracted under television.

Marks Hoyts' 50th anniversary, from dentist-turned-showman Arthur Russell's 1913 start to the neck-and-neck 1929 opening race between the Regent and the State, alongside the wreckers' demolition of the old Savoy (once the temperance-movement Temperance Hall).

Tours Melbourne's calorie-conscious boom, from Albert Park's new circular-bar restaurant and Collins Street's steak house Capers, through to health guru Lelord Kordel's free Myer lectures pitched, rather transparently, at selling his own diet books.

19 Oct 1963 Aussie Rules — In and Out of Season The Bulletin

Recounts Geelong's first VFL premiership in 11 years, the frantic logistics that got federal minister Hubert Opperman to the Grand Final on time, and the mock-solemn burial of a sparrowhawk (standing in for the vanquished Hawthorn) staged the next day at Kardinia Park by ruckman John Watts, plus MCC secretary Ian Johnson's plans to lift the MCG to a 150,000 capacity.

Goes behind the scenes at Myer's Santa Claus School, a 28-subject course modelled on America's original Santa Claus Inc. that trains a whole football team's worth of department-store Fathers Christmas each November, complete with reindeer consultants, hidden microphones and a graduation degree in Bachelor of Santa Claus.

Defends Melbourne's civic pride against a Sydney magazine's charge of drab mediocrity, then traces the theft of a display-only Melbourne Gold Cup replica lent to New Zealand's Tourist Bureau, a farce compounded when the genuine trophy had to travel there in its place for a building opening days later.
