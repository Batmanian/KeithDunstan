The Sun News-Pictorial

Keith joined The Herald and Weekly Times in 1946 and spent more than three decades at its flagship tabloid, The Sun News-Pictorial, where from 1958 he wrote the daily 'A Place in the Sun' column — a Melbourne institution that had run since the paper's first edition in 1922. The column was a direct line into the texture of Australian suburban life: wine culture finding its feet, the demolition of old Melbourne, social fashions from Toorak to the outer suburbs, and the thousand small peculiarities that made the city what it was. At a time when most Australians got their picture of the world from a single morning paper, 'A Place in the Sun' was where a generation of Melburnians read about themselves.

Walkabout Magazine

Walkabout Magazine was Australia's premier travel and geography monthly from 1934, a publication that took seriously the proposition that Australians should know their own continent before looking elsewhere. Keith contributed between 1962 and 1969, filing long-form pieces from regional towns, outback stations and coastal cities that rarely made the metropolitan press — portraits of a country still in the process of understanding its own scale and diversity. The magazine's combination of lavish photography and literary reportage gave Keith room to write at a length and pace that daily journalism never allowed, and several of his best pieces from this period read as miniature books in their own right.

The Bulletin

The Bulletin was Australia's oldest and most influential opinion weekly, founded in 1880 and a cradle of the national literary identity through Henry Lawson, Banjo Paterson and generations of satirists and commentators who followed them. Keith wrote for it from 1962 to 1984, initially under the pseudonym 'Batman' — a nod to the magazine's tradition of anonymous contributors — filing a column called 'Around Melbourne' that became one of its most-read regular features. Where The Sun gave him daily breadth, The Bulletin gave him the space to be caustic, digressive and funny at greater length, and the column's subjects — football tribalism, pub culture, the sociology of Melbourne's suburbs, the slow vanishing of the old city — add up to an unusually candid social record of the era.

The Age

Keith joined The Age in 1984 after nearly four decades at The Herald and Weekly Times, a move that felt to him — and to observers — like a defection of the order of Ron Barassi leaving Melbourne for Carlton. His columns ran until 2013, were regularly reprinted in the Sydney Morning Herald and Canberra Times, and two collections drawn from them became books: 'No Brains on Tuesday' and 'A Day in the Life of Australia', the latter commissioned for the bicentennial. The Age gave his writing a slightly more reflective register than The Sun had demanded — the columns from this period are more essayistic, more willing to sit with a subject and turn it over — and together they form the most complete record we have of his final three decades as a working journalist.

Reader's Digest

The Australian edition of Reader's Digest was, through the 1960s and 1970s, one of the most widely read magazines in the country — a publication that reached households well beyond the capital-city readership of the dailies and weeklies. Keith contributed several pieces during the 1970s, writing in a register more expansive than his newspaper columns allowed: the articles tend toward the reflective and the panoramic, taking subjects like the Melbourne-versus-Sydney rivalry or the peculiarities of an Australian Christmas and treating them as windows into something larger about how Australians understood themselves. The Digest's enormous circulation meant these pieces were read by more Australians than almost anything else he published.

The Courier-Mail

The Courier-Mail has been Queensland's newspaper of record since 1933, and for most of that time it was the dominant daily voice of a state that considered itself culturally distinct from the southern capitals — more outdoor, more provincial, less encumbered by the old-money conventions that Keith spent much of his career gently lampooning. His contributions were occasional rather than regular, pieces that came when a story or a visit took him north, and they tend to read as a Melburnian's affectionate but clear-eyed dispatches from another country: the Gold Coast's breakneck reinvention of itself, Queensland's particular relationship with heat, leisure and the public beach, and the gap between the two cities' understanding of what constituted the good life.
