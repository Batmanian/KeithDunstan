Once, footy dominated our lives. Now, things are much worse

5 min read.

First published 22 Aug 2011

Surveys the exponential growth of Australian Football League saturation since Keith Dunstan and Douglas Wilkie founded the Anti-Football League in 1967, from a single Saturday afternoon competition to year-round coverage, gambling and coaching sackings.

The Anti-Football League needs you. Its work is not done yet.

YOU will have noticed another footy coach has been executed. By my reckoning, this is the third in the past month.

Remember the news coverage when the Demons performed last rites on Dean Bailey? On the scale of news importance it was ahead of the humiliation of Rupert, and even the London riots.

Of course, it is the season for killing coaches. You can't have coaches who don't win games. By the grand final the carnage will be awful. Only one proud coach will be left standing.

It is an ugly thing when one has to admit total failure.

When we started the Anti-Football League in 1967, it seemed we were suffocating under the barrage of football. Melbourne was besotted with the game. It seeped out of every pore. It was mandatory to announce your allegiance to a VFL team. How else could you carry on a conversation on a Monday morning? The results on Saturday were the only topic a human could discuss in the office, in a shop, in a taxi, down a mine, up in the air.

Every outlet received the full smother, radio, television and the newspapers. My colleague Douglas Wilkie and I thought it might be possible to provide a little relief, succour to those who wanted to talk about something else.

If we thought we were suffocating in 1967, we are totally breathless now. In 2011 it is worse, infinitely worse. I picked up the sporting pages at the weekend and the first 17 were devoted to football before any other sport was mentioned. In 1967 the football matches were all nicely played, finished and got rid of during the one Saturday afternoon. Now they are played on Fridays, Saturdays, Sundays and Mondays. Tuesdays, Wednesdays and Thursdays are not far off. TV will demand it.

In 1967 no footy was played until cricket was finished. Now they seem to have a pre-season competition, and a pre-preseason practice competition.

You can even see them playing football in January, the poor creatures covering themselves with zinc cream like Shane Warne staving off sunburn.

In 1967 the competition, most correctly, was restricted to Melbourne. There were only 12 teams, with a final four. Look at it now. There are 17, a final eight, and there will be 18 next year. In the early days we didn't have to watch live footy on the telly, now not a kick, bounce, shove or clout, will be missed. And as for money, the Australian Football League (I refuse to call it the AFL, as they stole that from us) has sold the television rights for $1.2 billion.

That's real money. You could do something useful with that, like getting Tasmania out of debt.

One of the league's customers is Telstra. That means those fans who are not at the game, or not watching it on TV, can sit there looking at their mobiles. They can watch it in class, watch it at work, watch it walking, probably watch it during sermons in church, or even worse, while driving their cars.

Then there's the gambling. How innocent we were back in 1967. We liked to gamble, mainly on horses and dogs. Now we have switched to humans. We are betting $300 million a year on footy and it is soaring.

You can do absurd things like betting on who will kick the first goal. You can probably bet on who will be the first to be reported.

Perhaps you can begin to understand my depression. We have tried countless ways to give relief. The best has always been the red badge, square, symbolic of the football that won't bounce. Badge wearers look out for others wearing this AFL crest, and when they see it they are happy because they know at last there is hope of an intelligent conversation.

Also we have had posters announcing THIS IS A FOOTBALL-FREE ZONE. Housewives, particularly, found it helpful to paste one up in the kitchen.

We have sold these items in favour of various charities. That, too, has not been easy. It is dangerous to be anti-football. Many consider this anti-Australian, an insult to the flag.

There was one letter, typical of many, which said: "You'd have to be a poofter or a Commo to be anti-footy. Why don't you get out of the country and go to Afghanistan." So, understandably, some of our best-known charities thought it might be wise if they didn't associate with the true AFL. Being anti-football was like being a terrorist. That's not fair.

Actually, Australian Rules is quite a good game. It would simply be better if it received equivalent space in the media as, say, royal tennis, squash and lawn bowls. Furthermore, the season is too long. Look how many of the players get injured.

It would be better for all concerned if they finished off the season quickly. Six to eight weeks, surely, would be long enough.

However, be warned. We want to breathe again. We need to rally our forces. If you would like to join a magnificent cause go to our website, just type in Anti-Football League.
