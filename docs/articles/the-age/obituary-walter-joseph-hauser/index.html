Obituary: Walter Joseph Hauser

5 min read.

First published 11 Feb 2012

An obituary for Walter Hauser, one of the touring Vienna Mozart Boys' Choir singers stranded in Melbourne by the outbreak of war in 1939, who was taken in by Archbishop Daniel Mannix and went on to a career as an industrial chemist and Keith Dunstan's regular bridge partner.

WALTER Hauser, a leading member of Melbourne's Austrian community and famed as one of the Vienna Mozart Choir boys, has died at Cabrini Hospital in Malvern after a long illness. He was 84.

Hauser, the fourth in a family of five children, the first boy after three sisters, sang in the choir of the Church of the Holy Ghost in Vienna.

Dr Georg Gruber was conductor of the Vienna Boys Choir from 1933 to 1937, but he wanted to make some money and thought a choir on a world tour was just the way to do it, so he founded the Vienna Mozart Boys choir. He hunted all over Austria for the best choristers and when he called at the Church of the Holy Ghost, Hauser had just the voice he was looking for.

In 1938, Gruber took a team of 20 boys, aged nine to 14, on a tour that was to last nine months. It must have been a nervous choice for Hauser's parents because Hitler was already drumming Germany's might and the march into Austria was near.

The choir sang all over Europe, the United States, Canada and across the Pacific to Hawaii, New Zealand and Australia. They sang their way around the Australian capitals and many provincial cities. Wearing their traditional sailor suits they were an immense success. In Melbourne, Catholic archbishop Daniel Mannix could see the threatening storm and told the choristers: "If ever you get stuck, I would be happy to have you."

The boys gave their final concert in Perth Town Hall on September 2, 1939. Already the Wehrmacht's tanks were rolling and bombs were being dropped in Europe. The ship that was to take the choir home never left Perth.

Mannix stuck to his word and St Patrick's Cathedral won a choir almost beyond its dreams. He billeted the boys with families close to the cathedral.

Hauser lived with Hubert Cooney, the sacristan at St Patrick's. The Cooneys had no children of their own and they were very good to him. But, of course, the shock was tremendous and for a time every night the young Hauser cried himself to sleep.

Mannix was strong willed and many found him a controversial figure, but Hauser wouldn't hear any of this. He said: "I really loved him and he was very good to us."

All their lives the members of the Vienna choir were referred to as "the boys". First they went to Christian Brothers School Parade College, then St Kevin's.

It wasn't easy. Many of them had fathers who were fighting for Hitler, but as Hauser pointed out, they had one advantage, there were 20 of them. It wasn't long before they were accepted, but it was a different story for their musical director, Dr Gruber, and their teacher, Dr Otto Sternberg. They were interned at the prison camp in Tatura until the end of the war.

When the war ended in 1945, it seemed logical they would return home, but Vienna was in such a state that only one of the 20 did go back.

There were three boys whose voices broke just before the tour started; they were heartbroken when left behind. All three were drafted into the German army and died on the Russian front.

As for the others, they realised being stranded in Australia was the luckiest thing that ever happened to them.

Hauser went to Melbourne University and graduated with a BSc in chemistry engineering. He was good at sport, played in the ruck for Melbourne Football Club reserves and was destined for a great future until he destroyed his knee.

As an industrial chemist, he held positions with various employers and in 1954 he started work with the Victorian branch of the Australian Government Analytical Laboratories, where he became the director.

In 1952, he married Hanna, a Berlin-born daughter of a Jewish mother and Christian father. She had a story of an escape from the Nazis that could more than match his tale as a refugee choir boy.

Hauser, who had a lovely "everything happens to me" understated sense of humour, was well liked. Extraordinary for an Austrian, he adored cricket. He would watch it long into the night on Foxtel, no matter who was playing, sometimes to Hanna's despair. Also he loved bridge; if ever a slam came his way, even a minor slam, it was the happiest day of his life.

He was secretary of the Austrian Club for 10 years, and president for another two. Recently the Austrian government awarded him the Silver Cross for his services to Austria.

Just 18 months ago he underwent an operation for an aneurism in the aorta; he was in surgery for more than eight hours. Three weeks later his grandson, Matthew, graduated in medicine in Adelaide. Hauser said: "I'll be there even if it kills me." He was there indeed, but his health deteriorated.

Hauser is survived by his wife, Hanna; sons Conrad and Simon, five grandchildren, and his brother, Herbert.

Keith Dunstan enjoyed bridge with Walter Hauser for 14 years.
