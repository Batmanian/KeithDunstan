Frank Little's Sunday

4 min read.

First published 12 Dec 1986

A Good Weekend "My Sunday" profile of Frank Little, Catholic Archbishop of Melbourne, whose Sundays run to three or four different homilies, an Italian Mass he preaches in Italian, and a Monday golf game at Metropolitan that recently ended when he stepped off the sixth tee.

THE CATHOLIC Archbishop of Melbourne, the Most Reverend Frank Little, does not inspire a feeling of awe or even terror as did some of his predecessors. One thinks of the formidable Daniel Mannix.

No, there is a simplicity about Frank Little coupled with a sense of humour that can be even impish.

For example, he knows that it is almost compulsory to be interested in football if one is an archbishop in Melbourne. He pulled an Essendon card from his top pocket. 'I carry round my holy picture to show St Kevin and St Terry.' (Your correspondent is not an expert in these matters but one presumed that those concerned were Essendon holies St Kevin Sheedy and St Terry Daniher.)

'When I was made Archbishop,' he continued, 'lots of journalists came round. After an hour or so of questions, they lapsed into silence. I said, "There's one good thing about being an archbishop." "What's that?" they said eagerly. "You wear red and black."' (Need we add, these are the Bombers' colours?)

Yes, football was very useful around the parishes, always a swift entry into a conversation. But the truth is that he doesn't get to the football that much — maybe twice a year. 'Essendon club is kind enough to invite me annually and I did go more often but they seemed to lose every time. So I go through the fixtures and pick a match they are sure to win.

'My prayers, I find, don't work in that area.' More laughter.

Saturday is not a free day. He must prepare — he doesn't call them sermons — his homilies for the next day. Preaching, he believes, is the most important part of his office.

On grand feast days and holy days, he always celebrates at the cathedral. On other Sundays, 'if they care to invite me, I go to a parish church.'

If they care to invite him! They care a great deal. His record is five in the one day. It is nearly always two or three.

But can an archbishop cheat a little and keep on giving the same sermon? Well, you can at confirmations — the message is the same thing. Otherwise, you cannot get away with it. 'I usually have to prepare three or four different homilies a week.'

And, when it is all over, what does he do on Sunday nights? 'I'm pretty pooped out. But there's always correspondence to be done, a lot of that. But I watch a bit of television and I thank God for MBS radio — you know the one in Cotham Road, Kew. They play classical music. I love that in the background.'

As for time off, he uses the old-style clerical Monday. He plays with the same friend at Metropolitan golf course, teeing off at 8 am. This leaves the afternoons free for more work.

'You will notice I am nursing a bit of an ankle,' he said. 'Two Mondays ago, I was on the sixth tee at Metropolitan, did a good drive, outdrove my companion, stepped back to look and I went down the side of the elevated tee. Over I went. Haven't played since.'

The best Sundays? He loves the Italians. He spent six years in Italy and used to say he was one-fifth Italian (but now he only claims to be one-tenth). The cathedral has an Italian Mass every June. 'It has a beautiful spirit to it. More responsive. The choir that gathers that day is different from the cathedral choir.' The Archbishop preaches in Italian. But he actually comes from Essendon and, as a boy, paid his sixpence every Saturday to get into the outer for the football.

He loves, too, the multi-ethnic Mass in October — lots of vim there. The whole multiracial character of the Church fascinates him.

He said: 'You know, overseas people ask me what Australia is like. I tell them about St Aloysius in North Melbourne where they say they have only 58 nationalities. I say 76. They have special services there where they highlight this. Two representatives from each nationality wear ribbons and they bring them forward to the stage. Wonderful.'
