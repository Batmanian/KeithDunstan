Once a vision, now a site for sore eyes

5 min read.

First published 14 Nov 1998

The gutted, empty Southern Cross Hotel four years after the Nauru Government's redevelopment plans collapsed, and a look back at the lively Eastern Market it replaced in the 1960s, the market where C.J. Dennis's Sentimental Bloke first met Doreen.

The Southern Cross Hotel at the corner of Exhibition and Bourke Streets is gutted and forlorn. There is a sign inviting you into the foyer to look at a "demonstration suite". But you can't get in. Everything is locked.

The hotel has been closed since 1 April 1995 and for four years it has been "that bomb site" at the corner of Exhibition and Bourke Streets.

The Nauru Government bought the site in 1994 for $42 million. There were splendid plans for a 85-metre tower, 220 apartments, a 3000-guest ballroom and the nation's largest hotel lobby. It was to be reopened as the Grand Southern Cross. But it was not to be; the Nauru Government ran out of money.

There are rumors this week that something is happening and a deal for a new hotel may be under way. That would be good; Bourke Street needs help.

The Southern Cross opened in 1962. This was the new American-style hotel, glitzy blue tiles on the outside, every bedroom with an ensuite, a splendid array of shops, and instant access to Bourke Street. It was the choice of all the big stars: Judy Garland, Frank Sinatra, Danny Kaye, Rock Hudson, John Wayne, Marlene Dietrich, Sammy Davis junior, and, of course, the Beatles.

Who could forget Beatles Day, 14 June 1964 when 15,000 fans gathered out in Exhibition Street and 350 teenagers were treated for fainting, hysteria and minor injuries? We knew about the Southern Cross, no question.

But then came the Melbourne Hilton, the Regent, the Hyatt and Rockman's Regency, and the Southern Cross was outdated. Yet those who know their history feel sad. In the 1960s, we were too eager to rid ourselves of old buildings. Many historians have never got over the fact this failed hotel took away perhaps our most colorful and active building, the Eastern Market.

As a market it began in the early 1850s. It had long covered stalls and Melburnians called it Paddy's Market. But in the hearty days of the boom, gold-rich Melbourne decided to do something grand and spent 100,000, a huge sum, on a market that had few equals.

The Marquess of Normanby opened the building on 23 December 1879. There was a luncheon for 1000 people and music was composed for the occasion. Apart from fruit and vegetables, it was a great entertainment centre. There were phrenologists, jugglers, a Punch and Judy show, Aunt Sally stalls, hooplas, shooting galleries, and slot machines that depicted pantalooned bathing beauties "at play on the beach". A band played on Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday nights.

The star turn, perhaps, was Madame Zinga Lee, who sat in regal robes on a great white throne. She had a thrilling, almost terrifying handshake. This was because an assistant secretly passed a light electric current through her body.

There were jewellery shops and the Eastern Market went down in history through C.J. Dennis. This is where the Sentimental Bloke met Doreen:

"I seen 'er in the markit first uv all,

Inspectin' brums at Steeny Isaacs' stall.

...You could 'a' knocked me down wiv 'arf a brick!"

E.W. Cole started his enormous book arcade there. When the market closed in 1960, it still housed a lovely browsable bookshop. There was a licensed grocer, who had an equally browsable range of wine. Indeed, the cellars at the Eastern Market were huge and in the 1880s Dr L.L. Smith used half an acre of cellar space as a "champagne manufactory", an Australian first.

Yes when the wrecker moved in, the market still had a music store and a hardware store. It wasn't your normal hardware shop, but the marvellous, rambling, old-fashioned kind. If you wanted a G-clamp, a peculiar two-inch brass screw or bolt "that big", they would go out and find it for you. Never again was there a good hardware store on that side of the city.

The east end of Bourke Street had the reputation of being lively, the show business and entertainment centre of Melbourne. Ever since, Bourke Street has been slowly dying, with the life shifting down towards Southbank and the coming Federation Square.

If negotiations over the Grand Southern Cross Hotel, or whatever it is to be, fail, maybe we could rebuild the Eastern Market as it was. Have Madame Zinga Lee back with her electric shocks, return a decent hardware store and maybe we could have some updated penny slot pictures of bathing beauties. Bourke Street needs something.

There are rumors, too, concerning the National Mutual Centre in Collins Street. Before 1960 this was the Western Market. In the early days of its history it housed stocks used for sobering up drunks. If they pull down the National Mutual Centre, we could also rebuild the Western Market and make that end of Collins Street a rival to the casino. And how about the Fish Market? Could we have that back? It used to be at the corner of Spencer and Flinders.

Yes there are many ways to improve this city.
