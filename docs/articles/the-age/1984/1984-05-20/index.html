Know him? We've just kissed.

4 min read.

First published 20 May 1984

A 1984 photograph of Prince Charles kissing Prince Edward on the cheek prompts a survey of the social kissing boom, from Brezhnev lifting Kissinger bodily off the ground to the art of the cocktail party fake-kiss.

There is a fascinating photograph doing the rounds. It shows Prince Charles greeting Prince Edward. He embraces him, and then he kisses him on the cheek. 'Has Prince Charles unwittingly cut a curious taboo?' asked the Daily Express. 'Has he sparked a new fashion for men?'

English footballers have been kissing each other for years, of course. The Scottish Football Association became so concerned about this that it wrote to its players asking them to stop. Kissing and hugging, the SFA felt, didn't do anything for the image of the game. It was hard to see why, but that was the official position.

Soccer has an understandable logic to it. A goal is such a rare and wonderful event that the players who score them tend to throw their arms around each other and kiss. Hugging has now come to Australian Rules football as well, though we are perhaps not yet at the kissing stage.

Cricket has been embracing for six or seven years. Dennis Lillee is a fantastic embracer. Geoff Lawson, upon taking a difficult wicket — particularly the wicket of a Viv Richards or a Desmond Haynes — will exchange embraces all around. 'I wouldn't say that they actually kiss, but we are poised and ready for it.'

Kissing is booming as never before. My father-in-law didn't approve of holding hands in public. He insisted that kissing was something done discreetly and never in the streets. That era has gone. Now kissing goes on everywhere. People kiss each other in the midst of church services to show warm fellowship. You ought to see the passion that goes on at our railway stations, even at eight in the morning.

Mr Brezhnev was, in his time, a great kisser of both males and females. In his heyday he not only kissed Kissinger on the lips, he embraced him in such a bear hug that he lifted him clear off his feet. This was witnessed by several hundred people and caused considerable press interest.

President Carter was an indefatigable kisser. He kissed almost everyone in sight. The Queen Mother was startled by his kiss. She is quoted: 'He is the only man, since my dear husband died, to have had the effrontery to kiss me on the lips.'

Kissing is particularly terrible on talk shows. When Parkinson or Bert Newton kisses a guest it reveals the wonderful intimacy they have with those guests. You feel you are watching old friends meet. Time magazine recently quoted Charles Murray Davis, an eminent sociologist in California: 'Increased kissing is part of the general inflation of intimate signals. We kiss people we used to hug, hug people we used to shake hands with, and shake hands with people we used to nod to. Not to kiss or hug means one is not relating. Everyone is expected to kiss everyone else.'

Prince Charles may have started a new male kissing habit, and everyone will eventually have to follow. Wasn't it Edward VII who started putting creases in his trousers? He left the bottom waistcoat button undone because there was too much under the waistcoat, and everybody had to follow that too.

The cocktail party problem is now a genuine social anxiety. Should you or shouldn't you? The wife is watching with a critical eye. Some hostesses are remarkably kissable, with a warm pair of lips — an excellent night by zones. A press slightly to the left or right of the lips: pretty good. Further and further into frigidity, you encounter the 'Bass Strait in the winter look', offering an area somewhere north of the ear hole. The deadly fake kiss: the whole act performed so carefully that lips and cheek remain ten millimetres apart throughout.

Hand kissing is still considered very gallant. But for real success at a dinner party, kiss the hostess on both cheeks. It sends a frisson, though you may have to explain why when you get home.

As for me, I'm damned if I'm going to kiss any chap who is sporting a beard.
