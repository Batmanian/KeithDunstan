Where Modern Design Lives

4 min read.

First published 01 Jun 1952

Reports on the Museum of Modern Art's annual "Good Design" exhibition in New York, whose accompanying booklet argues that modern design should fulfil practical needs, never disguise mass production, and reject imitation of the past, with practical advice on choosing furniture from Herman Miller and Knoll, tableware by Russel Wright, and cutlery by Hermann Gretsch.

On 53rd Street, Manhattan, stands a large, modern building, "The Museum of Modern Art." The Museum is a progressive body which is always doing something. One week there will be a showing of Matisse, the next a collection of modern automobiles, a display of colour transparencies, a screening of rare films, or perhaps an exhibition devoted to some architect like Frank Lloyd Wright. It is an exciting place.

But every year there is an exhibition of "Good Design." It is an exhibition of everything that goes into the house, no matter what — chairs, stoves, radios, refrigerators, knives, forks, pots — everything. The museum does this to try to raise the level of the public's taste, to show them there can be both beauty and utility in the simple things we have around the house. Then, perhaps, the day will come when people will refuse to buy any ugly, shoddy, poorly designed article.

This year the Museum brought out a booklet, "What is Modern Design," to show what it is driving at.

The booklet says: "Modern design should fulfil our practical needs and express the spirit of our times. For this reason it should make use of every modern technique and the advances in fine arts and sciences.

"It should never make an article seem what it is not. If it was made by mass production, there should be no attempt to disguise this fact, but rather the makers should be proud of it. It should make use of new materials and techniques and make no attempt to imitate others.

"Designs made now in mimicry of past periods or remote ways of life cannot be considered as anything more than embarrassing indications of a lack of faith in our own values.

"A well-established home has many requirements which were never envisaged in the designs of the past or of other civilisations. Our requirements cannot be fitted into their designs without inconvenience to ourselves and disregard for the fine accomplishments of other peoples.

"Modern life demands modern design. Not because it is cheaper to acquire or less work for housekeepers than period styles, but because modern design is made to suit our own special needs and expresses our own spirit.

"Now, as in the past, mere novelty is no key to good design. Constantly we use certain articles whose forms have scarcely changed for 200 years. Most of them have to do with eating. Until modern designers develop fundamental improvements on them we shall pay tribute to the competence of the past in this field."

Of course, not all modern design is good; some of it is deplorable. A good example is this crazy desire to streamline everything as if we were all tearing through space with Buck Rogers. Here, form escapes function because streamlining is only necessary for high velocity objects like jet aircraft and bullets.

The booklet, as an example, gives instructions on how to choose a wooden dining room table. Look at the top of the table and make sure there is no elaborate inlay of veneer to hide the real grain. A good plywood top is all right because most table tops would be too expensive and too heavy in solid wood. The test is to look at the edge. Cheaper plywoods have less regular stripings at the edge, so good, clear stripings along the ends and sides are proof of good material.

How are the legs joined to the top? A good design will make a beautiful feature of the process. A poor one will depend on behind-the-scene strengtheners, sturdy, but ignored in the appearance of the table. As for leg spacings, a good design will allow people to sit at the table comfortably, and at the same time the legs will be finely proportioned.

These things are very much a matter of practice. If you are constantly looking for the good points in the design of a tea pot, a cup or a saucer, and every article you use, you can become skilled. Perhaps it might even take a little courage; particularly when the people around you prefer to use products of another culture and another generation.
