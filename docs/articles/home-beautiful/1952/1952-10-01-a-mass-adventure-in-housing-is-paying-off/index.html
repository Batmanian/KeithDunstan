A Mass Adventure in Housing is Paying Off

5 min read.

First published 01 Oct 1952

Reports on Park Forest, 30 miles south of Chicago, where American Community Builders Inc. raised £12 million to build a town of 17,000 from scratch on disused farmland, with 4,000 houses, its own schools, churches, kindergartens, the finest shopping centre in Illinois, and carefully planned roads so no child has to cross a major road to reach school.

The model communities becoming more and more popular in the United States are still, to some eyes, a depressing sight — with their freshly painted, neat houses and their bare flat gardens, still too new to boast even a decent plot of grass.

The houses are attractive with a suburban kind of cosiness about them. They don't set any new standards in design. When you are building by the thousand you cannot afford to take any risks and you must never rise above the common denominator of public taste. They are comfortable and well-designed, but they all look alike. For mile after mile they look as alike as though they were all stamped from a mould.

Here and there are a few pathetic variations. An extra chimney added to one house, a second house with a different style of porch. But these planned towns are a sign of the times, a sign of civilisation in transition.

If they are the big mistake of the 1950s they are certainly a better mistake than the mistakes of the twenties and the thirties. A drive through the suburbs of New York is all you need to find out what congestion and no planning can do to a city. In Brooklyn, the Bronx and even the newer borough of Queens there are great areas of lofty apartment houses, or "filing cabinets." They shut out the light and create the true "asphalt jungle." The only areas for the children to play are the streets and an occasional asphalt playground. There are houses, but these are mostly semi-detached, with no gardens and so little space housewives have to string their washing on wires from house to house.

After the war, men who had survived two years in the mud and the heat were faced with the same problem, here as in Australia and Europe. They wanted somewhere to live, not a cramped little flat somewhere on the fifth floor, but a house of their own. But, in the big cities like New York, the rents were so high only the rich could pay them. As for building a house, that was out of the question. Housing communities gave them a solution.

One of the most successful of these planned communities is Park Forest, 30 miles south of Chicago. Six years ago there was nothing there but a disused golf course and the corn lands of Illinois. There is now a town of 17,000 people.

American Community Builders Inc. raised £12 million from insurance companies and bought 2,400 acres of land. It was pure private enterprise and they went about it in a shrewd and business-like manner.

They didn't just buy a stretch of land, build a few houses and clear out after a quick profit, leaving a hopelessly isolated community. They knew that in creating a town they were also creating a buying power that would buy and buy year in and year out.

So even before they built the first house they had plans ready for super markets, drug stores, bowling alleys, theatres, bus lines and organisations to supply light, heat, power and water. All these they knew would one day have a heavy turnover in cash, and would make Park Forest a worthwhile proposition regardless of the houses they proposed to build.

Now there are 4,000 houses in Park Forest. It has its own schools, churches, kindergartens, playgrounds and the shopping centre is one of the finest and most modern in the State of Illinois. The shops are grouped around a closed courtyard and on the outside there are two large parking areas so that no one has to walk for more than 50 yards from the car to shop.

As one journalist said, Park Forest is a sharp contrast to Chicago's jam-packed grid of mixed industry and housing. The buildings occupy only 11 per cent. of the ground area with 10 families to an acre.

Mass production has made this difference — the two and three bedroom houses sell for between £5,000 and £6,000, at least 25 per cent. cheaper than the average house building cost. Apartment buildings with two to eight units a building rent for £32 to £52 a month, well below the regular prices for the Chicago area.

American Community Builders Inc. went about the planning with remarkable foresight. They knew beforehand that 40 per cent. of the residents would be children, so they designed the road network for safety. The children don't have to cross one major road to get to school. They also studied the prevailing winds and built the town so it would be free of factory smoke. Before they put in the first bulldozer they set aside an area for gas fired light industry, so that the people of Park Forest would have jobs and the houses would always be filled. As a piece of planning it is a gem.

They advanced cash for schools, police and fire departments and gave the town to the inhabitants to organise. It may be a little like living in a seaside guest house, but now there are probably more clubs, committees and ex-servicemen's organisations in Park Forest than in any other town of its size in America.

But these committees do achieve things. Representatives of 22 Protestant denominations got together and organised a United Protestant Church, something which Protestants the world over have failed to do for centuries. This church, worth £70,000, made mostly of glass and redwood, opened last April.

Park Forest is expanding. Eventually it will be a £60 million town with a population of 30,000. It is not a perfect answer to all the problems of housing. In some ways it is dull and mediocre, but it is a break away from the wretchedness of industrial towns. Park Forest gives light and air with space for children, three of the greatest needs of our time.
