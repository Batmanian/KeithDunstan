Eero Saarinen — Architect

5 min read.

First published 01 Mar 1952

Profiles Eero Saarinen, 41, Finnish-born architect who won the Jefferson National Expansion Memorial competition in St Louis with a 590-foot stainless steel parabolic arch, and whose General Motors Technical Centre north of Detroit, with its soft green heat-resisting glass, brilliant orange-glazed brick, and exhaust stacks that rise like Egyptian columns, has been hailed by Architectural Forum as architecture's most appetising post-war plum.

A great new movement in architecture which one day may rival Gothic, Romanesque and Renaissance is beginning to show itself in buildings scattered throughout the United States.

These buildings have a classic beauty, a beauty derived from the elimination of all superficial effects. There are universities, museums, banks and the like, which are so simple, so geometrically clean they could easily have been made from fogs of cards. Architects appear to be striving after flat, unbroken wall areas contrasted with great sheets of glass, rectangles, straight lines and clear cut edges. It is a new emphasis on structural clarity.

The rigidity of this modern design is controversial and often unpopular with city councils. They refer to it as "the ice box design," saying it lacks warmth and feeling. Yet these buildings are expressive of our modern art and age. They give a fresh and clean appearance to a city, they are more comfortable to work in and best of all they give an appearance of light and space.

Architects from the world over have contributed to the new design that is evolving itself now — such men as Le Corbusier, Mies van der Rohe, Frank Lloyd Wright and Gropius. But in the United States, one of the most promising and successful of the young architects is Eero Saarinen. At 41 he is carrying on the work of his great predecessors.

Saarinen is the son of the famous Finnish architect Eliel Saarinen who died here in 1950. Eero Saarinen went to the United States from Finland in 1923. Son of a famous architect, he could do perspective drawing when 8 years old.

Few people realised there was another Saarinen until in 1939 Eero won three competitions in a row for work on a school, playground and a music hall. After that the winning of competitions became a habit. He quickly became known as one of the country's leading designers of modern furniture and he won several prizes from the Museum of Modern Art. In this way, he played a major part in convincing Americans that there was a new style of furniture more suited to their lives than ponderous stuffed armchairs. He showed what could be done with rubber, plastics, and steel, and he designed original furniture which was ideal for cramped apartments in New York and Chicago.

In 1947 he won his biggest competition, a design for the Jefferson National Expansion Memorial at St Louis. He won 40,000 dollars, an unbelievable sum for an architectural competition.

The idea was to commemorate Thomas Jefferson, the Louisiana purchase and the opening of the West. Saarinen designed a park, two museums, an open-air theatre and a number of sculptural monuments, but the most striking feature was a slim, beautiful stainless steel parabolic arch 590 feet high.

The judges said: "The entire concept, full of exciting possibilities for actual achievement, is a work of genius, and the memorial structure is of that high order which will rank it among the nation's greatest monuments."

Saarinen has just finished work on the General Motors Technical Centre at Detroit, a project which Architectural Forum described as architecture's most appetising post-war plum. Here is a perfect example of what 20th century architecture can do.

Saarinen has designed a factory of extraordinary beauty. It is unlike any other factory that ever existed. It looks more like a youth centre, club or futuristic art gallery. Forum said: "They don't look like buildings as we know them. The group looks more like an exalted industrial product. Saarinen has blazed an industrial trail into the no-man's land of architecture which today lies between modern romanticism and the new classicism."

This building has the rigid rectangles, the unbroken wall areas of brick but at the same time it is alive and interesting. He gets this life by using soft green, heat-resisting glass and brick with a brilliant orange glaze.

Saarinen overcame the problem of the exhaust stacks in an ingenious fashion. They rise straight out of the ground, broad and black, like a series of Greek columns. Forum described them as monumental as the stone pylons in Egyptian hypostyle halls of 1800 B.C. A far cry from the ugly factory chimneys which often do much to destroy a city's skyline.

It is not easy to pin down Saarinen to a discussion on trends in modern architecture. He says: "Trends? I know! Is the flat roof passé? Will more natural wood be used? Should all windows go to the floor? It sounds too much like a discussion on women's fashions."

Saarinen believes we are at the beginning of a great period in architecture; that a new style is developing which won't reach its final form for some time. He points towards larger and larger shop fabrication units — that is, larger building blocks to play with.

In a recent lecture Saarinen said: "How well have we covered the whole field of architecture? As my father once said it covers everything from the ash tray to the city plan. We have done well in some departments. The house has received a great deal of attention and so have specialised building types like schools and hospitals.

"There are, however, other fields we have not conquered. The first and most obvious is city planning. If architecture is man's physical development, we have to be far more effective in this field. Another example is the design of outdoor spaces, the planning, the relation of buildings to each other and to nature. A third example is the relation of architecture to the other arts. Architecture has a responsibility toward the other arts it hasn't yet grown up to face.

"I was talking to a sculptor about this and he put it in a nice way. He said he believed architecture was the greatest of all the plastic arts — but only after it had incorporated sculpture and painting."
