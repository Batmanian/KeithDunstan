These Won Prizes

5 min read.

First published 01 Jun 1950

Covers two New York furniture design competitions, one organised by the Museum of Modern Art and one by the American Institute of Decorators, where designs by Clive Latimer, Robin Day, Charles Eames and others proved that inexpensive, practical furniture could also be genuinely beautiful.

Two international furniture design competitions that have just gone on display in New York attracted entries which were original to the modernists and downright startling to many a man-in-the-street. The entries were startling because the designers used materials which a decade ago most people would have thought impossible for furniture. Yet the entries did not win prizes for the sake of originality alone. All were practical and mostly inexpensive.

One competition was run by the Museum of Modern Art and the Museum Design Project Inc., which represents leading retail stores in 160 cities throughout the United States. The other was run by the American Institute of Decorators.

The Museum of Modern Art's show took three years to organise, and there were 3000 entries from 33 countries, including Australia. The winners were chosen early last year, but part of the programme was to put the winning designs on the market through the sponsoring retail stores. It took a year to do this, and because it was feared that the designs could be pirated by unscrupulous manufacturers, the show did not appear until May of this year.

The most beautiful furniture out of the 3000 entries was a set of storage units designed by two young Englishmen, Clive Latimer and Robin Day, both 34, of London. They won 5000 dollars with a passage-paid trip to America. They came over here and had the time of their lives. The first week they spent on a hell-for-leather tour around the United States and in seven days they covered about 7000 miles. The remaining three weeks they spent recuperating.

Robin Day is well-known in England where he planned and designed furniture for the "Britain Can Make It" exhibition. Clive Latimer teaches furniture design at the Central School of Arts and Crafts in London.

The storage units they designed for the exhibition took the only prize for that section. They are mounted on unusual brass supports which make the drawers easy to get at. Cleaning is a simple problem. The whole idea could have been massive and ugly, but they overcome this by breaking the unit into sections and with smooth horizontal lines. They also submitted book shelves, desk and bed with side tables to match.

There were two equal first prizes for seating units. One was taken by a 26-year-old, a navy ex-serviceman from San Francisco, who produced what the Museum described as one of the most ingenious ideas for modern furniture ever seen in this country. Working at his home at weekends he worked out a flat sheet of metal, designed to meet the complicated curves of the human form. He bent the metal round to meet itself in the seat of the chair. He attached it to four metal legs and there is nothing that could be simpler. The chair is comfortable because all the metal gives under weight.

The other first prize was won by Professor George Muche from Berlin-Fronau in Germany.

There were also two second prizes. One was taken by Charles Eames, a furniture designer who has already won a great number of prizes in America. His chair is made from moulded plywood and has an extraordinary lustre and soft, smooth finish. The photograph hardly does justice to his entry.

The other competition run by the American Institute of Decorators is an annual affair, and next year they are hoping for some entries from Australia. The only qualification for designers is that their entries must have been on sale through retail stores during the preceding year. The prizes have no monetary value. They are just citations of merit, but in this country their value is great because of the business they bring. Pictures of the awards are published in the newspapers and entries are shown on exhibition throughout the country.

The most attractive entry was an unusual coffee table designed by three young men from Long Island. It has tubular steel legs and handles with a top of slim birch strung together and framed in gumwood. It is much smaller than it looks in the picture and is a good example of the trend here to keep living room furniture low off the floor. The trio also won an honourable mention for a nest of three tables.

First prize for furniture accessories was a three-piece fire set of polished brass and steel designed by James Teague, of Chicago. The design is really beautiful but the price at £29 is a little staggering.

America is doing a great deal for her young designers and architects. Competitions such as these put their names on the map, but more than that they have a great educational value to the public. They point out that while the 17th and 18th centuries produced some very lovely things, the 20th century can do the same. Organisations such as the Museum of Modern Art and the American Institute of Decorators all the time are holding exhibitions to show the people the good things around them.

I don't mean just furniture, but pottery, sewing machines, radios, cutlery and almost everything we use. Naturally manufacturers try to design their goods better to get in these competitions. It would give more life to some of our stately old buildings, and industry, if our museums in Australia could hold a few such exhibitions.
