An Australian Looks at American Housing

7 min read.

First published 01 Jan 1950

Examines New York's post-war housing crisis and two contrasting responses, the government-subsidised towers of Stuyvesant Town and the mass-produced suburban sameness of Levittown on Long Island, asking what Australia might learn from each.

While the United States pours out billions of dollars to nations all over the world, New York has slums that beat anything Australia has to offer.

In Manhattan's "Hell's Kitchen," jammed incongruously alongside shiny skyscrapers are filthy tenements, packed with vermin and humanity. In New York, with high cost of living and bloated cosmopolitan population, it is almost impossible to avoid having slums. For example, there are 350,000 Puerto Ricans here and they're coming in at the rate of 500 a week. The authorities cannot keep them out because they are American citizens. They have no money and most of them live under disgusting conditions.

Yet Americans are self-conscious about their cities and they have slum clearance projects which would be ideal for Australia. We could well copy their ideas, because the schemes are not grandiose. American cities are not nearly as wealthy as many people imagine.

Baltimore, which is about as big as Melbourne, has some of the worst slums in America and not a great deal of money. However, in Baltimore they have a plan. Housing inspectors go block by block through the slum area and they give the landlords 70 days to clean up. If nothing is done the landlord is fined — in Australian terms, £99/10/- for a first offence and in which no work has then started, another £22/10/- fine that follows. This policy of "cleaning up," the authorities mean the removal of dilapidated fences, the repair of all broken plumbing and the spreading of sunshine from the rear — that is, the removal of rear porches and the repair of outside walls and leaking roofs. They also demand that slum owners remove outside toilets and install modern equipment inside.

The plan has been in operation for some time now. The difference it has been able to make in that time is staggering. Where there had been open broken-down fencing, rotting timber and broken concrete slabs, there are now strips of shining lawn, plenty of sunshine, a weapon against disease.

This is a stop-gap measure until the big slum clearance projects can be erected. At the state level, the New York State Housing is pouring money into slum clearance. The Department has a £195 million fund which will be used to build 31,000 apartments. More than 14,000 of these have been built already. This is another typical New York situation: the city provides many amenities and 150 apartments to those persons first eligible. The department is fighting to keep the rents down.

Let us look at a few of these projects. The first post-war housing settlement in New York City was the Elliott project, which cost £35 million. The buildings, made of fireproof steel and concrete, are 12 storeys high with automatic elevators. They cover five acres and between the buildings there is light and air with large areas of playing space for children. A few years ago it was the dirtiest part of "Hell's Kitchen" and now it can cater for twice as many people.

The rents are held down to an average of 43/- a week for the large modern apartments and this includes gas, electricity and heat. The average Manhattan rent for a similar apartment is £18/7/- a week. The state subsidises each apartment at the rate of 38/- a week. Only poor people can live in Elliott Town and once their annual income goes above £1134 a year (and that is by no means a high salary in America) they have to move elsewhere.

Another way the Government tackles slum clearance is to tie in with private companies. New York City can condemn slum areas for companies interested in putting up new housing. For 25 years it charges them taxes based only on the old slum values. In return, the companies promise to limit profits to 6 per cent.

Stuyvesant Town — a £36 million city built by the Metropolitan Life Insurance Company — is one of these projects. The area on lower East Side by the river was once a wretched slum known as the "Gas House District," a smutty collection of 19th century tenements that ruined the approach to Manhattan. Now 85 fire-proof buildings (12 and 13 storeys high) house 8755 people. It has a three-acre park, lawns, gardens, trees and 10 large playing areas for children. Rents range from £5/8/- to £9/18/- a week. These rents may sound high, but most poorly-paid white collar workers can afford them. The apartments are large with modern, streamlined kitchens and bathrooms. Further Stuyvesant Towns, subsidised by the Government, are now going up all over New York for the low-wage earner.

On Long Island, New York, as full-page newspaper advertisements tell you, the biggest small-house housing project in the world is taking place. A new town of 10,000 houses with a population of 30,000 people has suddenly sprung into being, all built by an American promoter William J. Levitt in aptly-named Levittown.

It is a perfect illustration of the old joke about Bill Brown who comes home from work, strides into the kitchen and kisses his wife only to find he has kissed the wrong wife. Mrs. Smith replies happily: "That was very nice, Mr. Brown, but you live three houses further down the street."

The joke sizes up Levittown. For mile after mile, except for minor changes in colour or position of the windows, every house is exactly the same — a dead-pan sameness. Each house is the same distance back from the roadway, each house faces the same direction, each has the same square plot of a back garden, the same position for a chimney and same concrete path.

Levittown is the kind of atrocity that makes one wonder where we are heading in this world of machines. This type of mass production where everybody buys the same mass-produced motor car, the same house, the same tinned groceries and the same radio set, is setting the pace for Aldous Huxley's Brave New World.

Mass production is making up for world-wide shortages, is inevitable and probably a sign of the times. The housing shortage here is just as bad as in Australia and Levittown is certainly giving ex-servicemen their houses. They are going up at the rate of nearly 30 a day and like everything else mass-produced, the price is lower. A Levitt house sells for £3596 Australian or £26/2/- Australian a month with no deposit required. It would cost you as much to build a similar house for yourself in New York State for much less than £6,750 Australian.

I am not suggesting that Levittowns should be built in Australia — that would be an abomination — but our architects could learn something from the design of these small houses.

They have two bedrooms, a bathroom and a living room downstairs, with a large attic upstairs. This can be converted into two more bedrooms and another bathroom later on if the ex-serviceman needs the space.

The living room is at the rear of the house where it belongs. One living room wall is a 16 ft. view window with glass from floor to ceiling. This gives a view on to the rear garden instead of the street where the owner has no privacy. The kitchen is in the front where the ex-serviceman's wife has only a step to answer calls at the front door.

The bathroom, also in the front, is alongside so all the plumbing can be conveniently plugged in from the street without having to pass under the house.

A unique idea is the massive brick fireplace which is open on both sides into the kitchen and living room. It is attractively equipped with pots, hooks and irons for steak roasts and barbecues.

Built-in cupboard space is everywhere and the modern streamlined kitchen is equipped with dinette, refrigerator, fully automatic washing machine, electric stove, exhaust fan, and oil central heating system. All this goes with the house at no extra charge.

All-in-all the Levitt house is a house any couple would be glad to have — anywhere but in Levittown.
