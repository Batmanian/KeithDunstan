Americans Like Their Table Lamps

4 min read.

First published 01 Jul 1950

Visits New York's annual lamp industry show and discovers a country obsessed with table lamps, from vulgar pottery creations to spare Scandinavian wire-and-shade designs, talking to collector and connoisseur Jascha Heifetz about why the lamp business is both a showcase for American ingenuity and a magnet for bad taste.

In most American houses and hotel rooms there are no lights hanging from the ceiling. Instead, small lamps are scattered round on every table and bookcase. Six small lamps in one room is not considered unusual, but the ultimate in good taste.

When I suggested timidly to an American housewife that this seemed excessive, she replied: "Nonsense! Lamps are just what you need to brighten up a room — to give it colour and life. Think what you can do with a lamp. It's the perfect medium for expressing personality."

The lamp in this country is a showpiece. It is also big business. Every year the industry holds a show which takes six floors in the huge New Yorker Hotel. This year just to prove they were not going backwards the show occupied seven floors.

There was a complete variety. Some were great vulgar pottery creations, plastered every inch with roses and crowned with lush shades all tied up with bows and ribbons. There were others which looked like imitations of Siamese temples, Egyptian mummies and aboriginal art. When women asked what they were, salesmen answered eruditely: "Primitives, Madam."

However, there were many lamps at the show which combined utility with slick American simplicity. Others combined modern art and fine sculpture to make truly beautiful lamps.

One man who had a fine collection was Mr Jascha Heifetz, of 25th Street, Manhattan.

He explained the lamp situation in the United States this way: "There are thousands of lamps at this show and many of them are very ugly. That is because the manufacturers know little about design. They pick up a bottle, a vase, or a bit of crockery, put a bulb into it and place on top any cheap lamp they can find. Then they call it a lamp. The lamp business is something anyone can get into. It calls for little capital and all that is needed is a workshop."

However, he said, people who didn't have something to offer didn't last long. Too many people underestimated the intelligence and natural good taste of the average man and woman. He said that nine out of ten sellers actually had less taste than the people who bought their stuff.

Mr Heifetz belongs to a school which believes in fine sculpture, beautiful ceramics and first-class craftsmanship. His lamps often are not designed for reading or brilliant lighting. They are used on a hall table or in a living room where a soft light is flattering.

Not all people like such lamps. Many Americans who live in small rooms on small salaries prefer a simple multi-purpose lamp, which will throw brilliant light in almost any direction. Here, as with so much of America's interior decoration, a good deal of the inspiration comes from Sweden and Finland. These lamps consist of little more than a twist of wire, a bulb and a shade.

Most of them are all metal, including the shade. This may frighten you a little, but the metal usually has a satin finish, the designs have grace and you would be surprised how easily they fit into a room.

As for being practical these lamps will do anything. They are loaded with universal joints, so that the lamp will turn up to the ceiling, down to the floor or sideways. Further, they have the American triple-way switches which give soft, medium or bright light from the one bulb. They are designed for desks or to stand on the floor.

So much for the lamp industry in the United States. It is a wonderful outlet for American imagination, and it has provided employment for thousands of American painters, sculptors and industrial designers. Americans are a little lamp crazy, and they will pay £30 or £40 for a lamp.
