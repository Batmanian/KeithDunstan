One House Every 14 Minutes

5 min read.

First published 01 May 1950

Reports on Carl Strandlund's Lustron Corporation, which converted a wartime aircraft factory in Ohio to mass-produce porcelain-enamelled steel houses at the rate of one every 14 minutes, examining both the genuine appeal and the fatal commercial limits of the experiment.

Carl Strandlund, a successful American business promoter, began thinking along these lines in 1947. America could mass produce automobiles, railway trains and aeroplanes, why not houses?

He decided that the old method of making houses with bricks, mortar, timber, and nails was slow and inefficient. He set to work and designed a porcelain enamelled steel house that could be mass produced from giant presses like a motor car and put together like a boy's meccano set.

He made a trip to Washington and put his plans before Congress. His ideas seemed so simple, so fool-proof and logical that the Government lent him 33 million dollars.

He used the money to take over the giant Curtiss Wright fighter plane factory at Columbus, Ohio, and there he installed 23 acres of presses, furnaces and high-speed welding machines. He called his organisation the Lustron Corporation. Inside the factory, conveyor belts move at 20 ft. a minute, and Mr Strandlund claims he can turn out 100 steel houses in every 23 hours of continuous operation — one house every 14 minutes.

Personally, I would rather have soft toned brick or natural rock for my house; but what is the use of fooling ourselves? Labour costs are mounting all the time, building materials are sky high and the situation promises to get worse — not better. Housing for the average man is a financial burden which weighs him down for at least 20 years and often all his life. He has to tie up money he would much rather spend on education, recreation and travel. However, if the steel house survives its early tests in the United States, it may become the house of the future.

The big selling feature of the Lustron house is the fact that it is made of porcelain enamelled steel — exactly the same material that manufacturers use for making enamel pots, refrigerators, stoves and washing machines. Strandlund applies the enamel at about the same cost as two coats of paint.

Look at its possibilities! It is rust-proof, fire-proof, rat-proof and will never fade, crack or peel. Painting, of course, is unnecessary, and the entire inside and outside can be washed down with soap and water. This is the kind of thing that really reduces housework.

Interiors and exteriors come in a variety of colours. These have a subdued mat-like finish which retains a sparkling new appearance. All this means a huge reduction in maintenance costs because the walls inside and outside are made of the same material. The roof, which is leak-proof, is made of porcelain enamelled steel plates.

The skeleton of the house is made of steel framing, factory welded into wall sections and roof trusses. The walls are of double thickness, insulated in between with glass fibre. The square plates interlock with each other and concealed screws lock them to the frame. Workmen make this water-tight and air-tight by compressing a permanent plastic strip between the sections.

There are 4000 pieces to the house, compared to 30,000 for a conventional house. All the gear weighs about 12½ tons, and as soon as it comes off the production line away it goes from the Ohio factory on a semi-trailer direct to the building site.

The house is put together on a solid concrete slab and four men can do it in a fortnight. No excavation is necessary.

Yet here is a major drawback. Every house is built to exact specifications. You cannot add a room here, put in a doorway there or swing the kitchen round to the other side of the house. You have to take the choice of two designs that Lustron offers you and, like a battleship, that design is there to stay.

However, its simple delivery by truck and quick construction means that there are never likely to be mass suburbs of Lustron houses. That often happens with prefabricated houses and the result is horrible to see — hundreds and hundreds of houses all exactly alike. The Lustron houses will be sprinkled all over the US as a result of individual orders.

The house is selling in two sizes, 1085 sq. ft. and 1209 sq. ft. There is nothing very radical about the designs — a manufacturer cannot afford to be when he has to please everybody. Even so, the interior layout is hard to beat and Strandlund has put the small areas of the house to good purpose. The bedrooms have built-in cupboards and recessed dressing tables with 21 sq. ft. plate-glass mirrors.

The living room has a built-in bookcase, the dining room a china cabinet, and there is a servery into the kitchen, which is equipped with an electric dish washer, a percolator and a built-in electric fan. The heating system heats the house through the floor and all the fixtures, including the conduit and insulation, come complete.

It is a house full of gadgets, and Mr Strandlund promises new models every year. The Lustron fans will be able to trade in their designed houses for the later models, like motor cars. When Americans trade in their houses they will then "melt down" the old one — a staggering thought.

The Lustron Corporation has countless local rules to contend with which have not kept pace with modern architecture, keeping the steel house out of many areas. Furthermore, it is not making as much headway with buyers as one would hope. There will be scepticism, as there always is, when it gets to that stage.

The Lustron house is the best value for the money on the market, although the price is still far too high for low-income families. The Lustron Corporation is not yet wound up to full production and has not yet achieved its purpose. Later the price must come down.

But there is a ray of hope. America, which has countless local rules to adhere to and sets the type of house design that the world will one day use, is a perfect case of the idea of porcelain enamelled houses. It is not yet a case of the American public not getting used to the idea — it must be an admission of guilt.

Source: One House Every 14 Minutes, The Australian Home Beautiful, May 1950. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
