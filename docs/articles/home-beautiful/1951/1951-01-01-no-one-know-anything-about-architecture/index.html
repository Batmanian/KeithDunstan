No One Knows Anything About Architecture

8 min read.

First published 01 Jan 1951

Profiles the sprightly 81-year-old Frank Lloyd Wright, his savage wit, his hatred of skyscrapers and Victorian ornament, the Imperial Hotel that survived the Tokyo earthquake, the Guggenheim Museum that never got built, and his lifelong argument that great architecture should grow from the land rather than from borrowed styles.

Sprightly 81-year-old Frank Lloyd Wright, an architect with the savage wit of a Bernard Shaw, told an architectural school in London the other day: "No one knows anything about architecture. For 500 years the thing has been going down hill until no one knows a good building from a bad one."

He added: "I hope you realise prizes mean nothing. Judges throw out the best and the worst, and prizes as a result of competition go to the average of the averages."

Mr Wright wouldn't disagree with this. He once told a friend: "Not only do I intend to be the greatest architect who has yet lived, but the greatest who will ever live. Yes, I intend to be the greatest."

Such magnificent assurance, such outrageous confidence in himself, has carried him through a long life which has bashed itself against every conceivable rule and doctrine.

He has a violent hatred of the dreary, traditional type of American architecture — the type that you see in every city and town throughout the United States. In building imitation Gothic churches, imitation Gothic schools, imitation Moorish school houses and great, ugly, "folksy-looking" houses, Americans seem to have gathered the worst features of every form of design since the great days of Athens, and mashed it into one ugly conglomeration.

Wright was one of the great pioneers of the smooth, modern type of architecture which only now is coming to American cities. He pleaded for architects to make use of what he called the lesson of the machine — simple things, well made and functional. He also urged them to stay away from the "gingerbread" — useless ornaments and unnecessary curves. He was a pioneer in the use of casement windows, broad slab-like lines and, even in the heyday of Victorian architecture, undecorated surfaces.

Wright was born in Wisconsin in 1869. His father sent him to the local university to train for civil engineering, but he gave it away after his fourth year and left to be an architect. He found work in the drafting room of Louis Sullivan, a famous revolutionary architect in Chicago. Sullivan was the originator of the slab-sided office building and the phrase, "form follows function." He is the only architect to whom Wright has ever admitted a debt.

The first houses Wright built in and around Chicago in the 1890s still have a quiet, simple dignity free of all junk and fussy excrescences. He developed an uncanny instinct for the right kind of material. He loves great fireplaces with massive stone chimneys, low-slung designs with sweeping eaves and broad hovering roofs. He loves space above all things and his designs do away with unnecessary partitions, ugly fixtures and ornaments. He is so earnest about this he can't even stand pictures on the walls.

Less than 10 per cent. of Americans can afford or have use for architects, but those who call on Frank Lloyd Wright find they are swept away by his enthusiasm. He builds a house as a work of art, as if he were writing a poem or modelling a sculpture. He forgets all about costs and such items as building regulations. One of his pet sayings at times such as these is: "God give me the luxuries of life and I will willingly do without the necessities."

Yet among his clients he inspires a love for their houses as strong as for their own children. They then treat their houses not just as a means to an end but as a living thing — in other words as a creative work of art.

Wright was world famous long before he was acknowledged at home. It wasn't until 1915, when the Emperor of Japan sent a delegation of officials and architects to see him in Wisconsin, that Americans began to realise that maybe Wright was somebody after all. The Emperor wanted him to build the 4,500,000-dollar Imperial Hotel for Tokyo.

This was a four-year job and he set out to make a building that could withstand any earthquake. He built it on a great floating foundation of jelly-like mud and he gave the walls and the floors a revolutionary sliding quality. He even designed the plumbing so that the pipes would not break and the electric wiring so that trembles would not cause short circuits.

In 1923, Tokyo had its worst earthquake in history. Nearly 10,000 people died under collapsing roofs and for several days there was a complete news blackout around Tokyo. First reports that reached Wright said the Imperial Hotel, like all other buildings, was a shambles. But later he received a cable from his old friend Baron Okura which said: "Imperial stands undamaged. A monument to your genius in Japan. Thousands provided with uninterrupted service. Congratulations!"

In 1929 at his famous Wisconsin house, "Taliesin" (Welsh for "shining brow") he established the Taliesin Fellowship Foundation and it still runs today. Students come from all over the world to join the foundation. A recent class had young architects from India, Egypt and Palestine as well as from all over the United States. They spend half the year at Taliesin in Wisconsin and the other half at Taliesin West, in Phoenix, Arizona. They do all Wright's drafting and work daily over his plans. They rise at 6 a.m. for breakfast and do all their own cooking and washing. Their chief entertainment is a film on Saturday nights and dinner and music in Wright's living room on Sundays. It costs them 1100 dollars a year and there is a long waiting list of students who seek the honour to have worked with the "great Wright."

One of his most famous buildings is the Larkin building in Buffalo. He built this soon after the turn of the century at a time when Australians were still designing antique structures like Flinders Street railway station. Plain, undecorated rectangular lines caused a great controversy, but apart from its unusual exterior design it had such unheard-of things as air conditioning, metal furnishings and fire-proofing.

He also designed other famous buildings such as the Unity Temple in Oak Park, Illinois, where he made the first monolithic use of concrete. The house of Edgar Kaufman is so slung over a waterfall at Mill Run, Pennsylvania, that it looks as if it is about to take to the air. The Johnson office building at Racine, Wisconsin, noted for its slender tapered columns, use of glass tubing instead of windows and heating of the building through the floors instead of radiators, was the architectural sensation of 1937. Radiant heating was unheard of, but now it is being installed in thousands of buildings throughout the United States.

Another controversial building was the Guggenheimer Memorial Museum for New York. Construction of this building was postponed indefinitely in 1948 because of rising costs but it was probably the most perfect art museum ever designed. It looked like a great shell with a glass dome at the top. It had a spiral ramp inside that wound its way from the top down. Wright's idea was that visitors to the museum could take an elevator to the top and look at the exhibits as they wandered their way back down the ramp to the bottom.

The building was clear of all interior supports and the fibre floors were carried through from the outer walls. The Guggenheimer Museum would have been one of the first museums that didn't look like a museum.

But Wright has always kept apart and argued furiously with other architects. More than anything else he has a hatred of skyscrapers.

Of Radio City he says: "The crime of all crimes and no excuse for it whatever." Empire State Building: "Look at that thing." The United Nations Building: "It resembles a super craft in which to ship a fiasco to hell."

He said at Princeton University in 1947: "We have urbanised urbanism until it is a disease. The city is a vampire living on the fresh blood of others, seeking vital humanity. To many the city is a stimulus similar to alcohol, ending in a similar degeneracy of impotence. We are a mobocracy. . . This pig-piling of human huddling we call a city."

Wright's solution which has never come into being is what he called "Broadacres." In 1935 he designed a relief map with models of farms, factories and railroads spread on a grand decentralised scale intertwined with forest and parkland. The inhabitants lived in spacious one-storey Wright-designed houses, each with more than an acre of ground. The houses had every known mechanical device, and for large families, garages which would take three to five cars. "Broadacres," according to Wright, was free from all the smell and filth of modern cities. Its power came from coal burned at mines many miles away and its sheer breadth of space made it an impossible atom bomb target.

They never built "Broadacres" and they never built many other great Wright designs. As Architectural Forum said: "This is much more our tragedy than his." The world will always remember his great designs, but if we had them now the world would be an easier and better place to live in.

Indirectly he had something to do with the design of Canberra. The late Walter Burley Griffin, of Chicago, who won the world-wide competition in 1912 for design of the new capital city, was his pupil for three years.

He has, of course, established himself as the greatest American architect who ever lived. Yet he was way ahead of his time and this brought him many rebukes. Nobody asked him to take part in the Chicago Exposition of 1933 or in the New York World Fair of 1939. The United States Government has never commissioned him to design any public building and for that matter neither has his home state of Wisconsin. Until 1948 he had never won an important prize or medal. In that year the American Institute of Architects — an organisation he had never joined — gave him its gold medal.

However, Wright, a man often unloved by his fellow architects, has done more than anyone else to put life and realism into modern architecture. He was one of the first designers of modern living.
