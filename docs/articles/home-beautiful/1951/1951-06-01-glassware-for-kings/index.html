Glassware for Kings

4 min read.

First published 01 Jun 1951

Reports on Steuben Glass of Corning, New York, how Arthur Houghton and architect John Gates smashed a million dollars' worth of vulgar stock in 1933 to start again with sculptor Sidney Waugh, producing crystal of extraordinary purity that became the preferred presidential gift, with engraved bowls by artists including Salvador Dalí, Henri Matisse and Raoul Dufy.

For a thousand years royalty has exchanged such precious gifts as fine horses, white elephants, diamond-studded boxes of jewels, and rare manuscripts, but the favourite gift of the President of the United States is Steuben glass.

For Queen Wilhelmina's diamond jubilee he gave a bowl which depicted five of Stephen Foster's best-known songs. When Princess Elizabeth married he sent the now famous Merry-go-round bowl, engraved with merry-go-round figures. More recently, for the wedding of the Shah of Persia, he gave a bowl beautifully engraved with characters from American legends.

It is strange, almost an anachronism, that the United States should attempt to make fine glass. During the past 50 years America has mastered the technique of mass production to such an extent that any kind of hand labour is looked upon with contempt. The idea has been to produce, produce, throw away and produce again.

Yet in the little county of Corning, at the bottom of New York State, a group of craftsmen is making glass in much the same way as the glass-makers of Europe made it 800 years ago. The glass is reputed to be among the finest in the world.

Steuben glass started off in an odd fashion. In 1933 it was a subsidiary of the Corning Glass Works, and doing rather badly. Arthur Houghton, jun., grandson of the founder of Corning, wanted to do something different. He was disgusted by the vulgar red and blue, blinding coloured monstrosities so popular all over America.

He called in John Gates, a young architect, who didn't know much about glass but a great deal about design. Together, with lead pipes, they went to the company's warehouse at Corning. They put on gloves, aprons and goggles, and methodically proceeded to smash every piece of glassware in the building. Splinters of broken glass showered through the air. Vases and bowls worth four or five hundred dollars bounced off the walls. They reduced 20,000 items, worth a million dollars on the books, to one terrible mess on the floor.

When they finished the day was nearly over and it was sundown. They offered each other their congratulations, and then started to work on their new business. They called in Sidney Waugh, a successful young sculptor, and the three men, all under thirty, called a conference.

First, they had a group of highly skilled craftsmen from the old Steuben glassworks. Most were of European origin, and they had been glassmakers for generations. Secondly, Corning had just invented a new formula which gave a crystal of incredible clarity and brilliance. Steuben claims that this crystal is second in purity only to fused quartz or pure rock crystal, and is finer than the best crystal of Sweden, France, Czechoslovakia, Austria and Italy.

Elated by these assets and the enthusiasm of the young men, Houghton reported to the Board: "Let us have a small amount of capital and a reasonable amount of time, give us a completely free hand, and we will attempt to make the finest glass the world has ever seen."

By 1935, Steuben still had a very small output, but Houghton still had his confidence. He decided to put on a show in London, and gained the use of London's fashionable Fine Arts Society gallery. The show was way beyond all expectations, and police had to control the crowds. After that Steuben went on tour, and pieces of Steuben began to appear in museums all over America as well as in London.

The design of Steuben fits in with the trend of design that we see in architecture, furniture, and the most perfectly designed of household goods. It has simplicity of line, with complete absence of vulgarity and unnecessary ornaments. As in modern architecture, Steuben pieces mostly have thick, slab-like lines, with a massive use of glass and smooth-flowing contours which gather the light in great streams.

At Steuben they like to tell you the glass is all handmade by the same "off-hand" process which has changed very little since the monk Theophilus wrote it down in the 12th century.

Naturally, any product which calls for such individual care, particularly in the US, cannot be cheap, and Steuben is about the most expensive modern glass you will find. It varies in price from £3/10/- for a small ashtray to £700 for an engraved bowl.

The engraved bowls are really something to see. Steuben has engraved them with designs by such contemporary artists as Raoul Dufy, Salvador Dalí, André Derain, Sir Muirhead Bone, Henri Matisse, Moise Kisling, Jean Hugo and Thomas Hart Benton. The job of engraving one of these pieces often takes months of hard work.

Source: Glassware for Kings, The Australian Home Beautiful, June 1951. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
