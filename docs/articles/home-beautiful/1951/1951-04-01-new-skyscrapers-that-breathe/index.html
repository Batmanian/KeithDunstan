New Skyscrapers That Breathe

6 min read.

First published 01 Apr 1951

Reports on the firm of Skidmore, Owings and Merrill, whose Lever Brothers building in New York, all blue heat-resistant glass and stainless steel, with an open public patio at ground level, is showing American cities how to build skyscrapers that actually let in light and air.

One of the world's most breathtaking sights is to see a skyscraper city from the air or sea at dusk. Then the outlines of the buildings slowly disappear into the night and the skyline becomes a fantastic fairyland of lights, like a million holes punched in an umbrella.

But come closer, enter that city and you see life is not so pleasant. Those skyscrapers take away the light and air; they stack up the population in huge filing cabinets, so that at peak periods the streets become as crowded as a Bondi bus. These crowds everywhere leave their litter in such quantities that despite the efforts of gangs of cleaners, the city becomes forever a dustheap.

This is not an easy problem to solve. In cities such as New York and Chicago where land is almost as valuable as gold, skyscrapers are the only way speculators can make this land pay for itself. In New York there is one fine park, but, generally, the skyscrapers have sprouted in reckless fashion with little thought of planning. In places like Wall Street it is like living in the bottom of a dark canyon. On a winter's afternoon it is dark, gloomy and unreal. On an afternoon in midsummer the air hangs thick and damp — then it seems worse than the New Guinea jungle.

New Yorkers got their first idea how skyscrapers should be built from Rockefeller Centre. Here the great towers were spaced well apart. Between them were wide plazas and in the centre was a skating rink. Pedestrians on their way from one skyscraper to another could walk past flowers and trees. There was plenty of light and air with every office within 50 ft. of a window. This idea paid off handsomely. Office space is so sought after in Rockefeller Centre that some tenants have offered £450,000 a floor to vacate.

But others copied neither the spacing nor the concern for light and air. Instead they replaced light and air with a glowing system of fluorescent tubing. A series of ugly buildings has sprung up around the city. Most of them look like wedding cakes or ocean liners.

Now, at last, the firm of Skidmore, Owings and Merrill is doing something different — it is trying to make the city a comfortable place in which to live. They are putting up a skyscraper which is modern in every way. New Yorkers are calling it "our miniature skyscraper," because it is a mere 24 storeys high.

The building will be the new headquarters for Lever Brothers, and it is a perfect example of the way modern architects are trying to express themselves in pure geometric forms. It is being built on 53rd Street on Park Avenue. In appearance it is like a vastly improved version of the United Nations building. It has no masonry to become black and ugly — the entire frontage is blue heat-resistant glass and stainless steel.

From the ground it gives you the impression it is floating in air. It has no ground floor. The building is supported by columns and the area where the tower meets the ground is sheathed in floor-to-ceiling glass. All the ground floor is open to the public and Lever Brothers plan to turn it into a patio with trees, fountains and shrubs. By doing this, they will pass over £A100,000 a year in unrented shop space.

The roof of the second floor is another great patio with gardens, seats and recreation space for employees.

It connects to the office cafeteria which occupies the third floor and makes the base of the blue-glass tower. The tower takes up only 25 per cent. of the available space and no office is more than 25 ft. from a window. This compares with some offices in New York which are more than 90 ft. from any window space. Of course, it has everything any modern office should have — underground parking space for 80 cars, electrically operated scaffolding gear for washing the windows, air conditioning, acoustically-treated ceilings and fluorescent lighting tubes set in troughs. The cost will be around £A3 million.

The Lever Brothers building is just one of the many designed by the firm of Skidmore, Owings and Merrill. The Museum of Modern Art in New York is so impressed by their work it is running a one-firm exhibition — something it has never done before. The firm consists of nine partners and 322 employees, including designers, draftsmen and city planners. Of all the large architectural firms in the United States their work is the most consistently good.

Their aim, according to one partner, is this: "We try to build buildings that will serve the needs of the people — the people in them as well as the people who own them. We want the appearance to be straightforward, simple with construction logically expressed. That results in repetition and simplicity, with the extra visual excitement coming from the choice of materials and the relation of their colour and textures."

Another unique building by Skidmore, Owings and Merrill is the Terrace Plaza Hotel in Chicago. Here they solved the problem of noise from traffic by lifting the hotel floors above the street. The hotel is above a large department store and to get to the hotel lobby you must go by express elevator from the street. The elevated hotel has its own terrace on the eighth floor where the residents can sit out in the open, sip their martinis and get a splendid view of Chicago. Best of all, from the owners' point of view, the rental from the department store plus the revenue from the hotel is a first-class way of making money.

Skidmore, Owings and Merrill are also building the new Bellevue Hospital on Manhattan's East Side. It will be 20 storeys high and will cover 11 acres — once again giving light, space and air to crowded Manhattan. It is also of simple, geometric construction and it is interesting to compare it with Melbourne's Royal Melbourne Hospital, a building now 10 years old. They are very similar in design.

For the Ford Company in Detroit they are building an administrative centre composed of three units, an 11-storey office for Ford, a six-storey office for Lincoln-Mercury and a three-storey service unit. The service unit looks like something out of H. G. Wells. It has parking facilities for 2000 cars with great elevated roadways which run into the building at all the various levels.

In the Del Monte shopping centre project in California they give you an idea of the suburban district of the future. Here they have designed a series of double-storey shops set in a landscaped area of 10 acres. The shopping centre is built in smooth, modern units complete with parking areas on both upper and lower levels. This idea does away with the terrible problem of what to do with the car when you go shopping.

Skidmore, Owings and Merrill are doing something to make American cities worth looking at. They are putting into practice the dreams of others — that human beings should not live cluttered up in cities without space, light, grass and trees. In fact, they are giving the world a lesson in architecture.
