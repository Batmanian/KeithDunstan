How to Enjoy Hot Christmases

5 min read.

First published 01 Dec 1974

A defence of the Australian habit of serving roast turkey and plum pudding in midsummer heat, tracing Keith Dunstan's own Melbourne Christmases from the 1880s boom-era house of his childhood to a flying doctor's Christmas at Mt Isa, an Antarctic station's six-bottle dinner, and a Barrier Reef yacht.

If you don't get roast turkey and pudding with brandy sauce on Christmas Day, when will you get them?

Back at the turn of the century, Henry Lawson, the most authentically Australian of writers, a man who could distil the very essence out of the gum trees and the ash out of the camp fire, wrote a tale called 'The Ghosts of Many Christmases.' He told of Christmas eating damper in the Outback, Christmas in a greasy six-penny Sydney restaurant, Christmas out on the western goldfields, where dinner was five pounds of corned beef boiled in a kerosene tin.

...apparently they weren't bad Christians, either.

How many of your own Christmases can you trace back? I remember as far back as when Santa Claus, though no longer real, was a wonderful presence, and the fire was too tenderhearted to disillusion a child just by addressing me as 'son'.

We lived in a vast old house in Melbourne, built in the grand boom period of the 1880s. At Christmas, the chimney would make sounds — the hot north wind finding its way in — and by bedtime the old building would start to cool. To my attic bedroom, these creaks sounded like the movements of some dreadful intruder. I didn't believe in Santa Claus, but I believed in burglars, even ghosts.

'George... Jesus!' A soft, fistily phrased explosion from Aunt Edith.

Handkerchiefs. And then the hunt for the most important parcel of all, the one I must absolutely have been watching for, about which I had been dropping hints for months.

One year, it was a complete set of lead soldiers, British and opposing Germans, all in their correct uniforms, complete with horses and cannon. I set up my lead army by the glare of torchlight and played out the Christmas battle until the dawn arrived.

Holly on the Tent Pole

People wonder why Australians insist on Christmas cards with the inevitable snow scene; the robins and the holly; why, in temperatures of 40 degrees, they still drink brandy sauce for Christmas pudding. Was it just tradition, or just excessive Australian sentimentalism?

At the height of the camping season, the women cook a full-scale Christmas dinner, turkey, pudding and all, which is so delicious that it can be done even on barbecue grills and in camp ovens. Tacked to the tent pole, Christmas cards depict thatched English cottages under snow — a dream world, a cold Christmas from the Shires — while outside the temperature climbs past 35.

And even Henry Lawson once wrote of a 'fair-dinkum Australian Christmas dinner' with '...everything cold except the vegetables, the fan playing on the veranda and vines outside, the men in their shirtsleeves, all smoking and the women and girls fresh and cool and fashionably dressed in lovely summer dresses and looking like boiled carrots!' (Possibly he was being ironic.)

Not so very different, with all respect, from wanting a hot kitchen producing roast turkey and plum pudding on a day when the thermometer outside says something quite different.

Christmas at Mt Isa and Mawson

One Christmas, in order to write a contrast story for my newspaper, I telephoned two distant places — the flying-doctor base at Mt Isa, and the Antarctic station at Mawson. The flying doctor, Ian Robinson, practises over an area the size of England; his patients communicate with him via radio across 200 station properties and 800 portable transceivers in Land Rovers. Ian Robinson's daily radio clinic had started at 8 a.m. It was a quiet day — a matter, really, of the doctor wishing all his patients a merry Christmas. He sent his greetings crackling over the radio to all the homesteads. Then, in the blazing heat of Mt Isa in December, Robinson went up the road to the local pub for a Christmas dinner — hot turkey, of course, and plum pudding and trimmings.

At Mawson, the officer-in-charge, Neil Roberts, reported over the radio telephone that the sun was shining on Christmas, and the temperature at the Antarctic station was minus 15.6 degrees C. Roberts had just been out for a run of golf on the all-ice golf course, playing with yellow golf balls. No need to hurry the round — the sun had shone since November 12 and would not set again until mid-March. Now it was time for Christmas dinner.

'We've got turkey and suckling. There's a proper written menu — roast duck. Here we go, six bottles of champagne between 25 of us, and we have the right wine for each course.'

Christmas on the Reef

Occasionally, I must confess, Australian Christmas can combine with tradition and be utterly good. A friend named Geoff Little lives at Shute Harbour on Hayman Island, on the Barrier Reef. His Christmas begins on a two-masted yacht heading out of harbour into a golden day of sunshine, threading through the islands to an uninhabited beach — foraging for fish, diving for oysters, building a driftwood fire, dining on barbecued coral trout and fresh oysters, then drifting home on the evening wind.

But, with all respect, I love hot roast turkey and Christmas pudding with brandy sauce, and if you don't get them on Christmas Day, when will you get them?
