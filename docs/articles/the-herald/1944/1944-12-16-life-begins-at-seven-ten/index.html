Life Begins at Seven-Ten

4 min read.

First published 16 Dec 1944

A comic account of the silent, clubbish regulars on the early bus to work, followed by a train journey to Melbourne spent with a port-swigging airman, swinging kitbags and three mischievous schoolgirls.

Whether a man is successful or not is often judged by the hour at which he travels to work. If that is so, I like Hitler am going down steadily. Once I used to cling to a strap in the eight-thirty, but now I sit comfortably and sleepily in the seven-ten.

When I caught my new bus for the first time I was glared at by my fellow occupants with all the hostility that women would show towards a man who has inadvertently walked into their powder room. Under their steady scrutiny I blushed and decided that I must have committed some social crime regarding my appearance.

As I afterwards discovered, it was not so. The seven-ten, as opposed to the eight-thirty, carries a very select group of a dozen or so souls, who consider that their bus is more select than a city club. One is not elected a member; it is only after the passage of time that one is accepted.

The strange part about these people is that never a word is spoken, yet we knew each other almost intimately. This intimate knowledge has been gained through having nothing else to do but study one's fellow occupants for half an hour daily. I know nearly every article in their wardrobes, where they live, their taste in literature, and their jobs.

There is one man who, like Dagwood, is always late. As the bus leaves his stop he chases after it at a speed which would outclass the Stawell Gift competitors. He leaps aboard the moving bus, still with only half his clothes on; what he is not wearing he carries over his arm. Everybody else in the bus watches him fascinated, while he puts on his tie, sweater and coat; pulls out his comb and does his hair. I am waiting for the day when he is really late and has to shave.

Travelling in trains is altogether different. No sooner have you found a seat than someone points out to you the state of the weather and what is going to win the first race on Saturday. The trip which I made to Melbourne recently at first promised to be a peaceful one, for the train was nearly empty. I shared my compartment with an airman, who from the start was all out for comfort.

As soon as the train stopped at the first station he pulled out an empty port bottle and started to sing. I was a little amazed at this, for I could see no reason for such gaiety, but my amazement soon turned to admiration when I saw the would-be occupants of the compartment take one look inside and then move off somewhere else muttering something about the curse of liquor. The train moved on and the airman, looking sheepishly at me, put the bottle back in his kitbag. 'I'm never without it,' he said. The ruse worked well until we were invaded by three schoolgirls, who were probably drawn in by the singing rather than scared away by it.

I sat back, lit my pipe and thought, what lovely girls! Their ages were about 16, 9 and 12 years. We hadn't gone far when the youngest started to cry. I felt an awful heel. After all it was a non-smoking compartment, and my pipe has killed many a budding friendship. I put it out and said, 'There, there. No more smells. Pipe's out.'

'It's not your pipe,' she said, sobbing harder. 'I forgot to kiss mummy good-bye.'

As the train approached Melbourne sobbing schoolgirls and empty wine bottles didn't stop our compartment from being filled, because it was either a case of sit in with us, or out in the passage. The airman, in order to make room, was forced to hang his kitbags by ropes from hooks on the ceiling. Peace then came to the carriage and I achieved what I had been trying to do since the journey began. I went to sleep.

A kitbag swung across the compartment by its long cord and hit me full in the face. There were shrieks of girlish laughter, the loudest coming from the small girl, who had perpetrated the act. I was thinking of homicide, but I laughed gaily, pretending it was a wonderful joke.

This sort of thing continued for some time, until I felt that I would get out and walk rather than continue the ordeal. Just as the small girl was on the point of undoing my shoelaces, with everybody else encouraging her by saying, 'Isn't she a dear,' I hopped out of the carriage at one of the outer suburbs. From there I caught a bus to the city. No one said a word, and that was the way I wanted it.
