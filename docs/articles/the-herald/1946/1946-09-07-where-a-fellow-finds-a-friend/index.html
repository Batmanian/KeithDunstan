Where A Fellow Finds A Friend

4 min read.

First published 07 Sep 1946

A visit to the new Victoria Police boys' club behind Fitzroy Town Hall, where constables, Fitzroy footballers and Ben Barnett coach inner-suburban lads who would otherwise be roaming the streets.

Three boys, aged 16, 12 and 11, were charged at the City Watchhouse last night on three counts of shopbreaking and stealing.

The boys are alleged to have been carrying out a number of robberies in the Carlton district.

They will appear before the Children's Court early next month.

Such stories are in the press every day.

Impressed by children's court figures showing that 75 per cent of child delinquency springs from unhappy home life and that 80 per cent of child delinquents come from inner suburbs, Victorian Police are following a NSW lead in running in the heart of an industrial suburb a police boys' club.

In the back of Fitzroy Town Hall (and with the aid of Fitzroy Council), the Victorian Police are conducting an experiment in befriending boys who need adult friendship. They cater for lads who, through no fault of their own, otherwise would be roaming the streets.

I called at the club one night to have a chat to the boys. Their clubroom was long and dingy, but everywhere there was furious activity.

The bigger boys, still in their working clothes, were playing a very husky game of basketball. Instead of nets for goals, they had rings drawn on the walls. The smaller boys were wrestling on rubber mats; others were hammering at each other with boxing gloves. One boy was trying to avoid a swiftly moving punching bag.

Judging by the shouting and laughter filling the room, these boys did not need much to enjoy themselves. I thought, 'If this is all that's needed to keep the boys off the streets they should have had it years ago.'

Calling several of the boys over for a chat, and shouting above the din while they panted for breath, I asked a few questions.

'What do you think of the Boys' Club?' I said to one boy.

'I think it's great.'

'Do you come every night?'

'Yes, we all come every night.'

'What did you do before the club started?'

'Hung round the streets. I used to get picked up by the wireless patrol about three times a week — just for loitering.' And grinning, he added, 'But it hasn't happened since.'

They were all eager to tell me about the club; how half a dozen players from the Fitzroy team came down one night to coach them with their football; how they were getting physical culture and weightlifting lessons; how a doctor and an optician were treating them free; and how Ben Barnett gave them a lecture on cricket one night.

Constable M. McNamara, of the Russell Street Police, who is in charge of the club, told me how one night he showed the boys over Police Headquarters. While they were there a man reported that his motorbike had been stolen. They watched, fascinated, the police system go to work. In two minutes, patrol cars all over Melbourne, and every police station had been told.

Soon the bike was recovered, and one boy said, 'Well, what hope have you got?'

Constable McNamara said 50 per cent of the boys had been in trouble; but now they were learning respect for people and property. Soon they would be moving into the Fitzroy Stadium, where they would have a billiard table, boxing ring, and table tennis. 'There we will be able to introduce new educational activities, and before long we will have a pool of reliable boys that Fitzroy business men can draw on.'

The Fitzroy Club is just getting under way, but there are 2700 youth clubs in Victoria with a total membership of 70,000.

In them 130 policemen are working in their own time.

The National Fitness Council wants to co-ordinate all of them by building in each large centre a central club, similar to a community centre. This large club will help the little clubs by providing expensive equipment they could not afford.

Discussing with me this club movement, the Children's Court Prosecuting Officer (Mr P. Loftus) said, 'Give the children their clubs, playgrounds and recreational reserves and keep them open, and you will keep juvenile crime down to a minimum.'

Source: Where A Fellow Finds A Friend, The Herald, 7 September 1946. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
