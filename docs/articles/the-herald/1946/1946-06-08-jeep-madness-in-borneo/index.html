Jeep Madness In Borneo

4 min read.

First published 08 Jun 1946

Among the last RAAF officers to leave Borneo, Keith Dunstan rides the North Borneo jungle railway behind a jeep fitted with railway bogies, a five-hour pushing, sliding, landslide-crashing journey to Beaufort and on to Tenom.

By Keith Dunstan, who found that the end of the war did not mean the end of adventure in Borneo. He has just returned to Melbourne, being one of the last RAAF officers to leave Borneo.

I thought I had seen everything in the way of troop trains. I have travelled in cattle trucks across the African desert, braved the 3ft. 6in. line to Townsville, slept in luggage racks and lain amongst the butts on the floor of a smokers' compartment, but I found the jeep train 'out of this world.'

When I was told I could catch a train from Western to Beaufort up the coast of NW Borneo I was very bucked, as trains are such a rarity in the Pacific.

But it was no ordinary train; a jeep was the engine, with the wheels stripped and bogies in their place so it could run on rails. Behind were two tiny carriages, one covered for passengers and the other open for timber.

Chinese and Malays were clambering aboard, clutching hard-won tickets. They were issued by the major in charge of the area, but the Chinese station-master seemed to be making a fortune in gifts of cigarettes and canned milk. He was popular. As the carriage had no seats, I sat on a case of bully beef. There was a strong smell of natives, fish and stale rice wine.

I wished the train would start. But there was no hope of its starting without being pushed. So we all gathered round the train, and with the poor little four-cylinder jeep motor screaming, it slowly moved. When it had gathered a little pace, everybody started to climb aboard.

Clattering along at 30 miles an hour, I admired the paddy fields, rivers, rubber plantations and the heavy-timbered jungle where the trees met high overhead, forming a dark, leafy tunnel for the train.

We were travelling at full speed when the natives screamed for the train to stop. They were wise, for they started their yelling a mile before their stopping place. The train takes half a mile to even slow down.

The driver put on his brakes and slowly the train lost speed until it was doing about eight miles an hour. They shouted again and the train slowed down to a crawl. The driver didn't stop completely in case he could not start again. Men, women and children jumped from the moving train, then running alongside, dragged their luggage from the carriage. I saw one woman throw her naked baby out of the window for her husband to catch below. He caught it.

It took a quarter of a mile to do this, while all the time the driver, terrified the jeep was going to stall, was shouting: 'Have you finished?' ... 'Have you finished?'

It took five hours to reach Beaufort, where we were to change trains for Tenom the next morning. Tenom is a beautiful village, high up in a valley, 90 miles inland. This time we had one carriage, two jeeps and a crew of coolies whom, I presumed, were for additional power. They were... Before we had gone far, rain started.

While it was dry, the jeeps had been straining to climb the hills. Now, in the rain, all eight wheels just spun on the greasy rails. The coolies hopped out to sprinkle sand on the rails, and again we pushed, with water streaming off our bodies. The coolies couldn't sit inside, four having to sit on the bonnet of each jeep. I didn't envy them their job, sitting precariously on those bonnets, shivering with cold, and the train threatening to leave the rails at any moment.

Charging downhill at 30 miles an hour, we swung round a sharp turn to be confronted by a landslide, barely 50 yards ahead. The driver applied full brakes until the wheels stopped spinning, and shoved the jeep hard into reverse. It made little difference, the train kept going ahead with the wheels spinning in the opposite direction. We crashed into the landslide and climbed a pile of rocks. Natives, fish, bags and men were thrown everywhere, but nobody was hurt.

It took an hour to get the train moving again. We continued the mad journey to crash into two trees, splash through a flood with water running through the floorboards, and to finally arrive at Tenom with only two casualties.

An English major at Tenom was quite unimpressed by our story. He just said, 'You should have been here when we had Australian drivers. They used to push their jeeps along at 50 miles an hour.'

For once I was glad that I was not with Australians.
