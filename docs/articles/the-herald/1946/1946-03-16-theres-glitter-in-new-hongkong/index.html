There's Glitter In New Hongkong

6 min read.

First published 16 Mar 1946

Fresh from a year in the Pacific, Keith Dunstan finds post-war Hong Kong one big black market of pre-war luxuries, glittering multinational cabarets and stripped, firewood-looted flats, overshadowed by a Japanese war memorial on the island's highest peak.

If you want a holiday and you have a thick wad of good Australian pound notes, I recommend that you go to Hong Kong. That wad won't last long, but you'll have a good time with it. You will dance in glittering cabarets, be waited on hand and foot in palatial hotels, eat beautifully cooked food and, best of all, drink icy cold Australian beer and wine.*

All this luxury struck me like a hammer, because for the last 12 months I had been in the Pacific, living on monotonous tinned food and never seeing a civilised town with buildings made of solid brick and mortar.

I wandered about the streets like a boy from the outback on his first visit to the city; wide eyed and open mouthed. The shops were packed with pre-war goods that had been stored away for five years. They are unobtainable in Australia, but may be bought here at black market prices.

Naively, I asked the price of a fine American pen that I wanted to replace my rusty steel nib. The Chinese smiled as if he were giving it away and said: 'Only 160 dol.' — roughly £12/10/. I thought again, and I moved on, making a price list as I went.

  • Beautiful finely patterned white silk, 35/ a yard.
  • Plain white silk, 14/ a yard.
  • Nylon silk stockings, 260/ a pair.
  • A camera film (maybe five years old), 30/.
  • An ordinary wooden pencil, 3/.
  • Hotel room, a day, without meals, 40/.

I paid 8/ for two gins in a hotel lounge.

Of course these are black-market prices, but the city is just one big black market, and the police close their eyes to it. In Singapore at least an attempt was made by telling the troops not to eat at civilian eating houses and not to pay high prices for souvenirs.

In Hongkong I saw a senior officer buy 12 yards of silk at 35/ a yard. As long as the Chinese can sell at these prices they will continue to do so. They looked upon me as good game, and when I walked past the more crafty ones would call out, 'Special prices for Australian.' I knew that, too, specially high.

Disappointed, I gave up trying to buy anything, and I decided to take a look at Hongkong's night life. I found a cabaret on the top floor of a palatial hotel.

A band was beating it out in fine style, well-dressed couples were dancing on a polished floor, the beautiful ballroom was brightly lit, and waiters were rushing in and out the tables with drinks.

I stood at the entrance taking all this in, when a squadron leader came up to me and said, 'By jove! An Australian. What a piece of luck! Do come in and join the party.'

Celebrity

Around the table at the edge of the dance floor was a New Zealand major, a Norwegian lieutenant, an RAF wing commander, a Canadian civilian, and an Englishwoman, who was a celebrity pianist. They had plenty to talk about, so my best contribution was to listen.

But what a strange place it was: this luxurious cabaret, an excellent band and every class, creed and nationality chatting and dancing with each other.

An American was teaching a lovely Indian girl to jitterbug, a turbaned Indian captain was dancing with a white girl, a French sweater girl was dancing with a Royal Marine, a pretty Chinese girl was with a naval commando, there was an officer from the Chinese Army and even a few women off the streets. I sat and watched, fascinated.

The Canadian pulled up a chair beside me and said, 'Well, it's good to see a dinkum Aussie. I'm the manager of the Hongkong Hotel. Would you like to have lunch with me tomorrow?' I never knew that Australians were so popular.

His name was Mr Medley, young, good looking and yet aged beyond his years. While sipping Australian wine, he pointed out different people to me. The French girl I mentioned before, her father had been killed by the Annamites. He pointed to the neighboring table and said, 'It was there that I became engaged to my wife in 1939.' He had been in Hongkong during the occupation. When he got out he joined the Canadian Navy, and is now discharged.

I was introduced to war-scarred Hongkong by an ordinary Chinese business man, who hadn't been out of the city for 20 years. He wore a shabby brown coat, a pair of trousers that belonged to his grandfather, and a pair of shoes donated by a relief organisation.

He was quite wealthy, but he still couldn't afford the fantastic prices for new clothing, and nothing had been available during the occupation. He had lost his home, his furniture, and everything he possessed. He was now living in his sister-in-law's flat in Kowloon, just across the harbor.

Together we walked through the streets, and I pressed him to tell me about the days of the occupation, but he wasn't anxious to talk about it. He was probably thinking of the time he spent in a Jap prison camp, how his brother-in-law had been imprisoned for a year, and finally killed for espionage activities for the British.

He wanted to forget everything that reminded him of the Japs. A burnt-out car lying in the street, some Japanese characters on a railway siding, or the uncleaned up debris of a bombed building would make him annoyed. I made a faux-pas by asking about a large monument on the highest peak on the island — 2000 feet up. It was a gaunt, ugly, four-sided building made of stone, towering up into the sky, and seeming to dominate the whole city.

'Yes, everybody asks about that,' he said. 'You can't escape it. It is a memorial to the Japanese dead, built by forced labor. I wish they would tear it down, the way they tore ours down.'

Desolation

We detoured around destroyed buildings and on to the residential areas. Here there were lines of empty flats, completely stripped. The floor boards, doors, windows, picture rails, and everything made of wood, had been torn out; locusts could not have made a better job of an orchard.

I asked my Chinese friend about this, and he said: 'The Chinese looted these houses for firewood. They had no other way of getting it, for there is no timber on Hong Kong. More than half the houses are this way, uninhabitable and, as yet, unrepairable.'

Unlike the houses, the people themselves are not beyond repair. There is plenty of food in the market and there are ships in the harbor. The people are anxious for news and for business with Australia. To prove this, many money changers will give more dollars for an Australian pound than they will for the English sterling.

They took all my pound notes off me in quick time, and I left the city with ten cents in my pocket.
