Cole's Funny Books — Funnier Than Ever

6 min read.

First published 12 Mar 1966

A fond revisit to E.W. Cole's Funny Picture Books, still selling 19,000 copies a year after 90 years and entirely unchanged, featuring Cole's Patent Whipping Machine for Flogging Naughty Boys in School, his Electro-Micro Scolding Machine for Naughty Girls, and his spirited offer of £1000 to anyone who could build a flying machine.

Back in the days of Nod it was utterly essential for all self-respecting Melbourne children to read Cole's Funny Picture Books — 'The Family Amuser and Instructor'. On the front of the book is a rainbow which read: 'Cole's Books — As Strange As It Looks — Contains More Than A Million Books:— "The most Common And New And Old Books You Want There Is." '

Alas, Mr E. W. Cole's magnificent book arcade in Howey Court closed in 1928, 10 years after his death and all seemed to be finished. Last Thursday your correspondent was walking along John Hawkins when he noticed an ad on the side of a tram: 'Cole's Funny Books — Funnier Now Than Ever'. It couldn't be. The only thing to do was to contact Mr E. W. Cole's grandson, Cole Turnley, out at Hawthorn. Said he: 'Oh yes, we've had a lot of advertising, but you've got to realise that after 90 years these books can only become classics.' He had a point. They stopped selling well 50 years ago. We sell 19,000 of them a year, including my Cole's Funny Book No. 3, which has been through 21 editions.

'But — but,' one said, 'are they exactly the same?' 'Exactly,' said Mr Cole, and that's what makes them funnier than ever. They are so very Victorian they have become the ideal thing to buy as the ideal gift for the man who has everything.'

The books came to hand and it was quite the happiest thing to take place in 1966. They were indeed exactly the same: the splendid pictures of Cole's Book Arcade with the message 'Unequalled in any city in the world. It is 3 storeys high, 600 feet long and an average width of 45 feet; its public walks are half a mile long; its galleries supported on cast iron columns. Free music recitals are given every afternoon and evening. Intellectual, well-behaved people collect and friends feel happy in the Palace of Intellect.'

And the puzzles, the rhymes, the riddles and the pictures were the same but ... one thought and almost flew. This incredible page 41 — could it still be there? So it was. 'Cole's Patent Whipping Machine for Flogging Naughty Boys in School.' It operated, and by the use of canes on a drum it could flog four boys at a time. Under the picture were the words: 'Testimonial From a School Master. To Mr Cole, Book Arcade, Melbourne. On one day the machine was introduced, 741 boys were punished for various misdeeds: for single offences — John Hawkins for spitting, William Winning for being, George Higginbottom for crying, Edward Daring for swearing, Henry Wheeling for calling names, Peter Bitting for biting ... and so on.'

Oh yes, deep into the book there was still the page desolating blizzards of boys ... and the great advantage of the Electro-Micro Scolding Machine for Naughty Girls. 'After it has been in use for a short time the girls will all have acquired good behavior; but the Machine will not become idle, as, with a trifling outlay, it can, without any boy remaining now, be turned into a Praising Machine, for they can be made to raise in a gentle voice — in a harsh one. In fact it will recite, preach, sing, whistle, scold or praise in exact tones anything that is recited, preached, sung, whistled, scolded or praised into a tube, which will be heard 40 miles away.' There are 1000 names for Dollies, Doggies, Pussies and Babies and some solid stuff on Australia: 'I think that Australia for its size, is, all round, the best country in the world. Its climate is pleasant and health giving. No desolating blizzards, few sunstrokes. In edible produce, for both size and quantity, it stands the highest.'

And there's advice from 'The Vulgar Little Lady': 'Then servants are vulgar and I am genteel So really 'tis out of the way To think that I should condescend To speak to them more than I may Or treat them like gentlefolks ... too, they.' 'Gentility,' Charlotte,' her mother replied 'Belongs to no station or place Not all the fine things that fine ladies provide Should teach them the poor to disgrace.'

Mr Cole covered everything. He was concerned with world government, the unity of man, with religion and with science. On page 113 we have a remarkable offer of October 1900: 'The undersigned firmly believe that man has already made machines to go over the land faster than the swiftest horse, and he will make machines to fly through the air as fast, and finally faster.' Cole offered a bonus of £1000 to any man who shall within the next two years build a flying machine to go by electrical, chemical, mechanical, or any other means, except by gas, a distance of 100 miles, and shall come and stop in front of the Book Arcade, Bourke St., Melbourne, Australia, as easily and safely as a carriage stops there now.

And Mr Cole gave a splendid model for a flying machine, a floating platform sped up and along by 21 opening and closing umbrellas.

Yet perhaps the most useful chapter today comes from a treatise against drinking and smoking. There are some excellent pictures, e.g. 'Brother who smoked and therefore destroyed his vital organs, becoming vile-looking, small and sickly' alongside 'Brother who didn't smoke and therefore grew good-looking, big, healthy and strong.'

Then there's the fine picture of two bearded gentlemen: 'Two talented men soothing, comforting and gradually poisoning themselves with tobacco and drink.' And the editorial matter is very strong: 'Smokers are always at the bottom of the class in school and college, and backwards at all kinds of study. Excessive smoking causes mental and physical laziness in boys and men. Medical men and observing experts affirm that many diseases are caused or accelerated by the use of tobacco, among which are the following — Heart disease, consumption, cancer, ulceration, asthma, bronchitis, neuralgia, paralysis, palsy, apoplexy, indigestion, dysentery, diarrhoea, constipation, sleeplessness, melancholia, delirium tremens, insanity. Smoking often leads to despondency. Smoking sometimes leads to suicide. Tobacco fumes, especially in small and poorly-ventilated houses or rooms, injure the health and kill multitudes of infants and children. It is even pretty much a security risk.' According to Mr Cole: 'A third of the recruits for the Army are disqualified through smoking.'

Cole Turnley believes that the possibilities of Cole's Funny Picture Books have yet to be really exploited. He would like to see a great Australian musical written about the life of Mr Cole. For a start, he feels, there would be two superb numbers, one incorporating the Scolding Machine with the girls going 'Yeah, Yeah' and one could imagine the beautiful beat to be had in a nice Whipping Machine number. The flying machine also could make a good scene.

Your correspondent had hour upon hour of happy reading and finally went to bed with the thought:

Books, like strong drink, will drown man's cares But do not waste his wealth Books leave him BETTER, drink the WORSE In mind, and purse and health.

Source: AROUND MELBOURNE For the Man Who Has Everything Cole's Funny Books funnier than ever, The Bulletin, 12 March 1966. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
