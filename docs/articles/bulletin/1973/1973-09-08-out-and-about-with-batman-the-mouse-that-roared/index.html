The mouse that roared

5 min read.

First published 08 Sep 1973

Disney World ambassador Frank describes to Keith Dunstan how Walt Disney secretly bought 43 square miles of Florida under assumed names, built a 1057-room hotel with a monorail running into its fourth floor, and now attracts 12 million visitors a year, while a Japanese former prisoner of war on a pilgrimage to see Phar Lap's stuffed hide provides the funniest sight Frank has seen at Disney World.

THIS IS the 50th anniversary for the Disney organisation and they have just had an invasion. We had Mice. Bonnie Drury, 19, of California, Disneyland's 1973 ambassador to the world. Bonnie was indeed bonnie, all smiles, and one could imagine her playing the lead in Cinderella, Red Riding Hood and Sleeping Beauty and no end of Disney parts.

We were also visited by Frank, a Disney World public relations man. He was tall, suave, obviously from Wisconsin, and his teeth were so perfect you could almost see the light glinting off them. And this was the man who wanted to tell us about Disney World — the new addition on 43 square miles in Florida.

I explained that I had been to Disney World only last year.

'Where did you stay?' said Frank. Actually there were two places where one could stay. There was the Polynesian Village, which was designed to give all the atmosphere of a native village in lush Polynesia with every comfort that late 20th century U.S.A. could provide. I remember the lobby called 'The Ceremonial House,' where undoubtedly the biggest ceremony was paying the bill. It was air-conditioned to give a non-Polynesian climate and in the centre there was a glorious rockery complete with waterfall, coconut palms, ferns and orchids. As for the accommodation — there were Tarzan-like native longhouses ... typical air-conditioned native longhouses with tinted glass windows, wall-to-wall carpets and private bathrooms.

I told him that I stayed at the contemporary hotel.

'Oh you do know,' said Frank, 'that place is unique. It runs at 99.8 percent occupancy all the year round.'

Yes, I did know that. Even before I left Australia the Disney World organisation demanded that I pay the two-day bill in advance and they would not confirm the booking until they received the cash. The price for a single room was $56 a day. Admittedly the 1057-room hotel was unusual. There were two mighty parallel walls, 15 storeys high with balconies on either side. Imagine those walls glassed in at either end so that there was an enormous concourse in the centre — something like one of the famous London railway stations, but on a blown-up scale. They called this area the Grand Canyon.

Actually, the concourse was on the fourth floor and, in utterly Buck Rogers style, a silent monorail train on a soaring overhead track swung straight into the building to land you at the bottom of the Grand Canyon.

When you go to Disney World you have to leave your car in the 13,000 car-park and make use of Disney public transport. I remembered there was a choice, apart from the monorail — one could travel by vintage steam train, Chinese Junk, Mississippi paddle steamer, antique double-decker bus or horse-drawn tram.

Frank explained that Disney World unquestionably was now the greatest single entertainment complex in the world. Last year nigh on 11 million people went through. This year they expected 12 million.

I could believe that, too. When I was there, hundred-yard queues lined up for every attraction in the magic kingdom, waiting to pour into Fantasyland, Tomorrow Land, Frontierland and all. As Bob Hope said, 'Who would have thought that a mouse, a cranky duck and seven dwarfs could create such a gold mine.'

There are two championship golf courses and Frank was keen to talk about the great Disney golf classic, won twice now by Jack Nicklaus. But anyone can enter the pro-am contest. There are places available and it costs $1000 to enter. For this you get accommodation for four or six nights, unlimited rounds of golf (with use of electric cart), unlimited use of Disney transport, admission to everything in the magic kingdom, a Walt Disney golf blazer, a Walt Disney golf shirt, a dozen Walt Disney golf balls, and a commemorative photograph of your pro-am foursome etched in metal.

Frank said they were improving the place all the time; Disney World never stopped still. For example, they were now building Buena Vista houses. These houses, utterly luxurious, were designed by Disney's top academy award designer — swimming pool, golf course, on one side, lake's edge on the other. You must appreciate these are not ordinary alligator-ridden, normal Florida lakes. Disney had all the lake bottoms scraped, and replaced with pure white sand to give the whole thing the correct look of a Dorothy Lamour paradise.

My last impression of Disney World was of thousands and thousands of people moving past wearing Mickey Mouse ears. I asked Frank what was the funniest thing he had seen there. 'A Japanese with an American camera,' he said.

I had been trying to work in my own contribution. We had on board a Japanese, a former prisoner of war who had spent the war in Japan in a POW camp. He had a strange and curious desire to make a pilgrimage to Melbourne's stuffed effigy of Phar Lap. He said, 'I heard so much about that durned animal from Australian POWs. I was almost personally accused of poisoning him. They weren't always joking either.'

I explained to him that the feeling now was not quite so bitter. We were beginning to accept the situation. A number of noted creatures like Murray Rose, Rod Laver and Bruce Crampton had gone to the U.S. and had failed to be poisoned.

Source: out and about with Batman: The mouse that roared, The Bulletin, 8 September 1973. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
