Horsetrader Henry

5 min read.

First published 06 Jan 1973

A review of Peter Blazey's political biography of Henry Bolte, noting the dearth of Victorian state political biography and assessing Blazey's account of the premier's press management strategy, his brusqueness in cabinet, and the agony surrounding the hanging of Ronald Ryan.

FRANKLY, it was a relief when Sir Henry Bolte retired. It had been like living too close to a regimented sergeant major, an overwhelming school teacher or an absurdly monomaniacal managing director. Every time one opened a newspaper, there was Sir Henry giving quote no. 1,753,872, and his grinning peanut of a face was inevitably in the cartoon.

I nearly went crazy reading this book. It was three nights of torture. Here was the whole Bolte thing again, the era I had lived through. One received every darned tiny detail about Bolte from his cricket, football and rabbit-skinning exploits to grand gestures like being photographed kissing his 88-year-old mother. Yes, one remembers that event too. It was good for many, many votes.

Bolte, Bolte, Bolte, it's all too soon. Not that this isn't a good book. Indeed as one who has tried and tried to be a student of Victorian history, I dip my lid in reverence and respect to Peter Blazey. Search the shelves of the Victorian State Library and you will get almost a zero answer regarding good biographical material on state or colonial politicians . . . Tommy Bent, Munro, Albert Dunstan, Alexander Peacock... they didn't rate biographies.

So they tend to be lost to history. Until Bolte, state politicians (and this goes for almost every state) have been considered too small time for the pen of the political biographer. It just seemed a little too soon for Henry Bolte, that's all. Couldn't he have rested in the vat for a year or two?

Blazey has been both state roundsman for the Australian and scholar. He was subjected to those Bolte press conferences every day and he has been a research man in the political science department at Melbourne University; an ideal combination.

His book is at times repetitive, at times laborious like a cricket match, but then he takes off with splendid verve, producing very interesting insights into the Bolte character. It is well documented in a scholarly fashion and the index is complete; an utterly necessary book for the political animal.

It's interesting to note Blazey's approach. Clearly, he tried very hard to be fair to the man. On one side you could detect an enormous respect for the strength, moral honesty, the basic intellectual ability and the sheer political guts of the man; and on the other, a horror for his arrogance, publicity seeking and total lack of sensitivity.

He gives a summing up fairly early in the book with these lines: 'For all the premier's political flair, his overcoming of opposition and his financial dexterity, at heart he lacked a developed philosophy of social justice and had little awareness of the problems of the family man. He was, as his original backers had intuited, a very talented small-town horsetrader who would repay their patronage by keeping a conservative government in power and allowing them and their friends to develop their business interests with a minimum of government disturbance . . . The conservative forces innate in Victoria, the weakness of the ALP allied with such political checks as the rural gerrymander and the DLP, and the sheer charismatic egotism of Bolte himself, were too strong for his government to be removed or even threatened with removal.'

Blazey writes of Bolte's toughness in the party room, how he maintained splendid discipline, how he would frequently dress down errant members . . . 'the backbenchers were the schoolboys, the cabinet the prefects and, as a refugee from Bolte's anger, Arthur Rylah frequently played the role of school matron.'

There is much on the hanging of Ryan, the big fight that Bolte was so determined to win. Blazey does reveal the agony and strain it actually placed on Bolte himself, plus the arrogance that had to come out.

There was much speculation at the time as to what Bolte was actually doing at the very moment that Ryan died. Was he comfortably in bed? Was he eating his bacon and eggs? Blazey reports that journalists frequently asked Bolte this question and he always claimed he couldn't remember. But several years later, at the Hotel Canberra, he mused in his bluff way: 'What I should have said was I was having a shit.'

Part of Bolte's success, says Blazey, was due to the way he handled the press. As soon as he became premier he decided to hold a daily press conference and he did that for 17 years. He never wanted his senior ministers talking to the press. Keep control right in the centre, and the best way to keep them out of the papers was to say everything with that daily conference.

So that was the reason for the constant, colourful Bolte dialogue and you get a fair bit of it in this book — such as that memorable line in August 1970: 'It's a lot of bloody nonsense this permissiveness . . . Sweden and Denmark are nonentities as countries.'

There was the time when he was in conflict with the teachers, making lines like 'They can strike until they are black in the face and it won't make any difference.' Then refusing to meet them over pay claims, he said: 'I don't have a doorstep low enough for them to sit on.'

Blazey writes that Bolte considered that one his best quote of all.

Source: Books: Horsetrader Henry, The Bulletin, 6 January 1973. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
