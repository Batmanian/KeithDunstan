The heart of the matter

5 min read.

First published 24 Nov 1973

The year's contradictory medical advice on heart health has Keith Dunstan hopelessly confused, as sugar, coffee, salt, jogging, fast driving, budgerigars, bottle-fed infancy and working all threaten cardiac arrest, while three to six drinks a day, church attendance, married sex and eating like an Eskimo offer protection, with a Danish dentist recommending removing all teeth early as the safest precaution of all.

I AM a very nervous person. I lie awake at night listening to my heart beating. I think, 'My God, any minute it might stop. There was an odd little pain in it this afternoon. Have I been treating it right? What about those potatoes I had for lunch, the whisky I had before dinner. I haven't done any push-ups since last Thursday.'

Now the Federal Minister for Health, Dr Everingham, comes out with an almighty attack on sugar. He said that refined sugar was a second rate food, a third rate drug and a third rate poison. Heart organisations, he said, had found a high incidence of coronary diseases related with a high intake of sugar.

I've had enough. I'd like to declare a ban on all comments about the heart for at least six months. I'm so confused I don't know who to believe any more. Dr Everingham also knocked alcohol — bad for the heart too.

All right. On November 12 we had a story from New York which stated that researchers from Kaiser Permanent Medical Program had found that drinkers had fewer heart attacks than teetotallers. So you can look after your heart by taking three to six drinks a day.

At the same time they found smoking was crook for your heart, coffee was crook for your heart. Dateline Auckland, November 2: we are told that, not only is sugar bad for the heart, but research teams find that Rarotongans who ate highly-salted corned beef had heart attacks.

On October 22 a National Heart Foundation officer advised that if you are to survive you must get steady exercise — jogging, walking, swimming or cycling. Last year one of our great newspapers started a jogging campaign. 'Get out there and jog — everybody.' Fit chaps like Ron Clarke and Herb Elliott led the way. On the very morning the campaign started a character in a track suit was found dead on the footpath in Brighton. A middle-aged jogger.

On October 3 there's a story from Glasgow. Heart attacks have been linked with 'soft water.' So you try to cut down on the water in your scotch. On August 25 the Michigan Heart Foundation makes an assault on high cholesterol foods. Lay off animal fat. Don't eat too much butter or eggs.

But then this month a U.S. specialist says that cholesterol has nothing to do with it. Look at the Eskimos, they eat fat until the roofs of their igloos fall in, but they don't get heart attacks. Diet wasn't in the story according to this doctor. Driving yourself to the minute, sitting up at night worrying why you haven't achieved more today, that's what causes heart attacks.

And later still, a doctor in Chicago reports that sex is the answer. A good healthy sex life is important to a good heart. If you don't get enough sex you become frustrated, and the resulting tension causes the pump to break down. But then you have to be careful. Victor Wynn, a professor of human metabolism at the University of London, says there is a significant increase in coronary diseases among young women taking birth control pills.

Researchers at the Johns Hopkins University in Maryland found that non-churchgoers suffered twice as many heart attacks as those who went regularly once a week. Regrettably he did not give a breakdown on this. It would have been interesting to know whether non-churchgoing Roman Catholics had more heart attacks than non-churchgoing Baptists, Anglicans and Methodists. Or how about non-synagogue-going Jews?

An Italian cardiologist, Bruno Caru, of Milan, found that adulterers were more prone to heart attacks than married people. However, simple fornication was all right.

A doctor in Dundee found that keeping budgerigars was bad. These could strike you down with heart trouble. Dr Coleman was puzzled by the budgie theory. He wondered if the attacks were caused by fear that someone would leave the window open.

Oh! I could go on and on. Dr G. R. Osborne, a leading British pathologist, found that people who died young from heart disease were probably bottle-fed babies. Another doctor tells us that the ingredients of tomato sauce are good for the heart. So presumably those vast pie-eating footy crowds should live for ever.

Dr Vernon Coleman, an English doctor, recently compiled a list of advice for the London Observer, stuff that he had picked up from the newspapers. A Danish dentist, he discovered, advised you to get all your teeth out. The teeth were a favorite harboring place for bugs that attacked heart valves. Yes, if you have your teeth out early it also solves the problem of having them damaged by refined sugar.

A cardiologist in Newcastle suggested that eating onions was a good heart safeguard. But on the other hand a doctor at the Johns Hopkins University in Maryland found that non-churchgoers suffered twice as many heart attacks.

Oh! I tell you! Eggs, coffee, salt, milk, sugar, tobacco, water, all cause heart attacks. So does speeding. The Lancet states that Peter Taggart of London's Middlesex Hospital said that the excitement of fast driving builds up fatty acids in the blood. Fatty acids accumulate on the artery walls, lead to the hardening of arteries and, lo! a heart attack. Frustration of traffic jams is an additional cause.

I don't know what you have gleaned from all this. Let's see. Heart disease is on the side of the good. It likes churchgoers. It likes people who have lots of lusty sex, but preferably in holy wedlock and without the pill. Don't keep budgies, don't eat sugar, don't drink coffee, drink four beers a day, keep away from water. Drive a slow car, keep off salt, milk, eggs, cream, eat onions, live like an Eskimo and don't work. Several doctors advise that work is bad for you.

There is just one more survey I would like to see. The incidence of heart attacks among those who read all the advice on how to look after your heart and those who don't read at all.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The heart of the matter, The Bulletin, 24 November 1973. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
