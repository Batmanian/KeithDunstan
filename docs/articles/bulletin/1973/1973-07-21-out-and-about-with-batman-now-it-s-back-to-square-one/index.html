Now it's back to square one

5 min read.

First published 21 Jul 1973

Melbourne's decades-long city square saga returns to its starting point when English finance company Star Holdings collapses with over a million dollars in losses, meaning the Regent Theatre need never have closed, Hilliers' chocolate shop need never have shut, and the battle to save the Regent is on again, prompted by a letter from Barry Humphries of Canterbury.

THE COMPETITION for city squares in Australian capitals is desperately keen. Brisbane went through fearsome political and public traumas to enlarge its city square. Sydney had similar problems in closing off Martin Place. I understand they now call it Martin Plaza.

Here in Melbourne we have always had a yearning for a grand city square, and hangings. It has been the longest serial ever perpetrated on this city, longer and even more tedious than Blue Hills and 96. The agitation actually has been going on since Hoddle first laid out the place in 1837.

In 1960 the city council began to do something about it and progressively it pulled down a series of buildings at the corner of Collins and Swanston Streets. So the designers went to work and created the first stage of our square. They put down an extraordinary vista of dull concrete slabs. You would describe it as a classic Edwardian state school playing ground.

Others might feel that it was a good layout for a crematorium before the niches went in. Because, after all, the council did put in some flower beds composed of immense board of works sewerage pipes laid on their side. But the stretch of concrete was breath-taking and tourists could be forgiven for believing that Melbourne was preparing for the world's Olympic hopscotch championships.

Stage two was to pull down the great old Regent Theatre and extend the square up the hill; and the theatre actually closed in June 1970 and all the tenants in the Regent Theatre buildings and Wentworth House were tipped out that November.

Please don't think anything was not done to save the Regent — the band of Hoyts lovers was immense. One of the fiercest campaigns was conducted by Clive Bush, a retired gentleman in South Yarra, who sent off furious lengthy telegrams to everyone who would listen. The PMG benefited from Mr Bush at the rate of $600 a year.

The council announced that facing the square, the English finance company, Star Holdings Ltd, would put up a 52-storey hotel-office building.

Public fury then worked into a nice crescendo — what with the complexities of international finance, what with the even greater complexities of politics, federal, state and local, Star Holdings at a loss of over a million dollars has pulled out and we are in exactly the same situation as we were in June 1970. The Regent Theatre need never have closed, Tim the Toyman need never have moved, Hilliers, once a great chocolate company, once the chocolate malted milkbar capital of Midstealeal, need never have closed down. Indeed one of the former proprietors there described the whole business as 'the greatest council cock-up in the history of the world.'

But it has put everything back into a nice state of balance once more. The battle to save the Regent is on once again and even Mr Bush is sending telegrams as of old. To simple minds the Regent-lovers' logic would seem to be unassailable. The government is planning to spend more than $8 million on a city concert hall. On the other hand the city is planning to pull down the Regent, which seems rather better equipped than the Sydney Opera House — it has a theatre which seats 3200 and another that can take 1500. The acoustics are excellent, the dressing rooms are good, the stage is immense and there are no quarrels whatever over the size of the orchestra pit. It is not even very old. It was rebuilt only 26 years ago.

The Herald for the past fortnight has been running an 'it's your square' page, calling to the public for ideas. Some have suggested, keep the Regent, some have suggested a Japanese garden, which might be a good idea considering the importance of our new ally. One writer suggested facilities for two-up, another called for a happier city and why not create a Luna Park there with ferris wheels and a big dipper. The writer was careful to add that the architecture of these devices should be in keeping with St Paul's Cathedral and the Town Hall.

But the letter I liked best appeared in the Sun letter columns rather earlier:

Sir — Why can't those fuddy duddies who want to preserve the Regent Theatre in Collins Street live in the present. However this is not so much the issue as what is going to replace it. I, and many of a like mind, say that the Shrine of Remembrance should. For many years now the long uphill Anzac march has proved a trial to our senior citizens. If the shrine were dismantled and re-built on the Regent site then the problems of these citizens would be over. And think of all that valuable land on St Kilda Road just waiting for development.

It was signed Barry Humphries, Canterbury.
