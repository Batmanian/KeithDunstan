Last of the great footy finals

13 min read.

First published 29 Sep 1973

With the VFL's contract with the MCG expired, the question is whether the grand final will eventually move to Waverley Park, which opened in 1970 and will hold 80,000 by next year but can only be reached by car through catastrophic traffic jams, while the MCG remains one of the five biggest grounds in the world and the only place where a human being can have the opportunity of being hooted by 120,000.

THE VICTORIAN Football League grand final is on Saturday. As usual you cannot buy tickets on the day — all seats are pre-sold, and all go to Victorians. Ah, but the airlines are outside Victoria, so TAA and Ansett are running special grand final flights for interstate visitors. You can take your pick — Canberra or Tasmania. Ansett is advertising that they will put you up at the International Hotel in Canberra and there you will see the show on a king-size 8ft by 8ft television screen with a better view than you would get at the MCG. It is not stated whether you can take your own cans and floggers or whether pies will be served. Alternatively you can go to Devonport or Wynyard in Tasmania.

TAA is offering an all-inclusive trip to the Wrest Point Casino for $55.50. You get a smorgasbord lunch, all afternoon in front of the telly with the footy and a chance to play the tables before returning at night.

It is impossible to say precisely how many people will be at the MCG. The grand final record was in 1970 with 121,696. Last year it was 112,393, which is odd considering all the tickets are pre-sold. But you see there are those unpredictable people the Melbourne Cricket Club members who may or may not turn up and there are the even more extraordinary people, the football fans who have paid hard cash for their seats, then don't go. There are always some thousands of these.

However the intriguing question is this: are the grand finals at the Melbourne Cricket Ground coming to an end? The contract has run out and as soon as this match is over hard discussions will begin between the VFL and the trustees of the Melbourne Cricket Ground for a new one. Perhaps the VFL is not ready to stage a grand final at its luxurious new Waverley ground next year, but could it be two years off? Or will the contract be another five years or 10 years at the MCG? Maybe five years would be a good idea, but right now nobody knows, least of all the authorities at the MCG.

An old editor-in-chief once pointed out to the staff of the Melbourne Herald: 'Public relations people don't take you to lunch because they like the colour of your hair.' Clearly the VFL didn't build Waverley because they like the colour of the hair of anyone at the Melbourne Cricket Club. Their yearning all-absorbing ambition has been to have a supreme ground of their own.

Let us look at the merits of the two arenas. Ian Johnson, secretary of the Melbourne Cricket Club, in effect manager of the MCG, says that his ground is one of the five biggest of any kind in the world. 'There is a bigger one in Rio de Janeiro,' he said. 'I believe there is a bigger one in Chile; a very big one I believe in one of the Continental countries — could be in Prague. There is certainly nothing comparable in the U.S. Wembley in London holds 98,000. Hampden Park in Scotland may have more accommodation, but it is mostly standing on slopes. Oh yes, we're certainly in the first five.'

Regardless of the quality of the play, the vast concrete colosseum on Brunton Avenue is a very good show in itself. It looks the ideal place for the watching of the eating of Christians, and Grand Final Day with 120,000 inside is an awe-inspiring sight. But on the awe chart it is the sound that is more important, and it begins when the band starts to play God Save the Queen just before the first bounce. The crowd is straining, anxious and you never hear beyond 'God Save...' up goes the roar. The noise is like an enormous organ that rises and falls, sensitive, activated by every action on the field. There are not many places where a human being can have the opportunity of being hooted by 120,000 and that happens at the MCG.

The Melbourne Cricket Club is the oldest Melbourne institution — it goes back to 1838 and the building of the MCG itself began in 1853. Parents for many generations have put their sons down for membership as soon as they were born — it was more important than finding a school.

But while we are wallowing in nostalgia there are some amusing ironies in this tussle between the MCC and the VFL. The Melbourne Cricket Club invented the game Australian Rules and one of Ian Johnson's predecessors was the man mainly responsible. He was T. W. Wills, born at Molonglo, now Australian Capital Territory. His father sent him off home to Rugby and Tommy returned as a top class cricketer and footballer.

The year was 1856 and Wills wrote off to the sporting newspaper, Bell's Life in Victoria and suggested that there should be a football club to keep the cricketers fit during the winter. The MCC adopted his suggestion immediately and instructed T. W. Wills, H.C. A. Harrison and W. J. Hammersley to draw up the rules for a new game.

Now if you want to blame one person for the heretical split in the codes in Australia then Wills is your man for he had been captain of the Rugby College football team at the very home of football. But he didn't want Rugby here. He thought the Rugby tackle was too dangerous on the hard Australian grounds, so he designed the high flying Australian Rules game instead.

You must appreciate that the club believed the game was only a diversion. The Melbourne Cricket Club would not have the game on the main oval. This was considered to be a preposterous suggestion — imagine what football players would do to the beautiful turf. Instead they used a double-sided grandstand and the football was played outside in Richmond Park. However, in June 1877, a game was played on the main arena. The match was between Melbourne and Carlton, and it drew a crowd between eight and 10,000; with gate receipts of 200 pounds.

Indeed the Australasian cricket writer said: 'This pretty clearly demonstrates that football is the most popular of our outdoor sports. Who ever heard of such an attendance at a cricket match. Who wouldn't have a cricket ground and let it after this? Impecunious cricket clubs take heart of grace, and let your ground for this purpose say in the month of June, when the damage to the turf signifies but little.'

And so they did. Soon the football clubs were playing on all the cricket grounds for the whole winter season, and it was looked upon as an Antipodean miracle the way the turf would recover and be ready for cricket only weeks after the end of football.

Yet here was the rub. Cricket got there first and cricket controlled all the venues. Collingwood Football Club today is the outstanding exception as the club that controls the destiny of its own ground. So here was the sport that drew the crowds, and made the money, but remained like the junior married couple, always paying the rent in mother's house.

Naturally the off-spring grumbled about the terms, and the sense of humiliation ran deep, particularly when the Melbourne Cricket Club still retained the grand final series. So the Victorian Football League decided to build a ground of its own.

Kevin Coghlan, sporting writer for the Herald and ex-Collingwood player says it all began through the Olympic Games. VFL Park, Waverley was conceived in the mind of Sir Kenneth Luke, the league president, back in 1958. Sir Kenneth says the club Carlton could have been transformed into a 'dream' stadium, but they ran out of time and ran out of patience over costs. So the government went back to the MCG with a new plan, to the club which had originally rejected the scheme. And Sir Kenneth, president of the 1956 until his death, became the prime mover behind the establishment of Waverley. A great businessman, he was always impatient of the league system with its 12 delegates and divided control.

His great ally through all this was the VFL administrative director, Eric McCutchan. In September 1962 for $500,000 they bought 212 acres of grazing and market garden land at Waverley, 15 miles from the city. They were just in time, the same land today in the era of 'glorious housing schemes' would be worth at least $60,000 an acre, and the League could be up for ten or 12 million.

Their idea was to build the first ground ever designed purely for Australian Rules football and one of the world's great football stadiums, an area that would never be stained by cricket. Cricket grounds recover quickly from a football season, but the reverse is not true. After several generations of thick black Merri Creek soil have been applied to the centre for the making of Test wickets strange things happen. This is the almost mystic material described through hundreds of thousands of words by such eloquent people as Neville Cardus, but it is black glue after the first wet football match and misery to the footballer.

They planned virtually a perfect grandstand-to-grandstand carpet and really the biggest ground in Australia. There is 30 feet from the boundary line to the fence. Eric McCutchan says they did this for two reasons. Now, even if you are sitting close to a fence you get a complete view of all the players, head to toe. And it is extremely difficult for a sprinting player to injure himself against the fence.

Waverley opened in 1970 with a capacity of a little over 40,000. This year with further construction it was 65,000 and next year with a completed circle of stadia and a triple tier on the members stand it will be 80,000. Of course, it is luxurious throughout. For example there are 86 private boxes. These are wall-to-wall carpeted with all sorts of special comforts plus special food and drink service from the caterers. A four-seat box costs $200 a year, a six-seat box, $300. Next year when the new levels are added there will be 50 more of these boxes.

Eric McCutchan radiates confidence about Waverley, and he says the membership figures of Waverley Park speak for themselves, 27,000 members with 13,000 on the waiting list.

But it could be a case of smiling on the outside and crying on the inside. The knockers have been around in squadrons, battalions and regiments. They have tossed these complaints. Waverley is: (a) Too big. Players finish each quarter 'dropping with exhaustion.' (b) The costs are astronomical. It is bleeding the league clubs white to the detriment of the still squalid other grounds. (c) It will never in 100 years have the drawing power of the MCG. (d) It is too far out. No public transport. Traffic is a total disaster story.

I tossed all these things up to Eric McCutchan and he fenced with the skill of the practised man who has been doing it these past 15 years.

He says the ground looked over-large, particularly when the stands went only half way round. 'The size was an optical illusion. The truth is that it's very little different from the MCG. Waverley is 58.217 metres from the centre square to the goal. The MCG is 53.590 metres.'

The strain of paying for Waverley has been such that there was a near revolt this year, led by Richmond which shares the MCG with the Melbourne club. There was a threat to form a breakaway from the Victorian Football League with discontented clubs made up of say, Richmond, Melbourne, Collingwood, Fitzroy and possibly other takers.

The VFL now owes around $7.3 million on Waverley. The Fitzroy president, Ern Joseph, claimed the VFL was endangering the future of football with its extravagant expenditure on the new ground. It was repaying its loans over eight years whereas if it borrowed sensibly it could do it over 25 years and put the money into club grounds which were losing spectators because of their poor facilities.

Eric McCutchan doesn't agree with any of this and he is not worried by revolt threats. If you pay off loans over 25 years you have to go to merchant banks and pay a higher rate of interest. Then he points to the higher financial dividends from VFL Park.

'At VFL Park we get all the broadcasting and TV money. We have all the catering rights. We take all the parking proceeds and we receive money from everybody who comes through the turnstiles. We get a dollar from B-category members and we put in 60 cents for each A-category member. Everyone at VFL Park is a daily payer.'

Eric McCutchan says a spectator always is worth considerably more money at VFL Park, but the MCG is still the better crowd drawer. For example, since 1970 Richmond has played Collingwood twice at the MCG and twice at Waverley. At Waverley the crowds were 29,939 and 55,827. At the MCG they were 82,191 and 72,659. Take the same figures — Richmond against St Kilda ... 33,489 and 31,502 at Waverley, 38,149 and 44,348 at the MCG. Or take Melbourne against St Kilda — 22,570 and 23,507 at Waverley and 39,830 and 44,348 at the MCG.

Ian Johnson claims that the MCG is closer to the city and better fixed for public transport and parking than other large sporting grounds in the world. He says there is the tram and two railway stations within 10 minutes' walk.

He says the ground can be emptied of 120,000 people in 11 minutes and all dispersed within half an hour. In 1965, for example, the final siren went at one minute to five and at 5.30 pm the railways reported that their job was done. They had cleared 35,000 passengers. The tramways reported 'all clear' at 5.40 pm. They had cleared 16,000.

In 1972 and 1973 two finals were played at Waverley and four at the MCG. The second semi-final — Carlton versus Collingwood — drew 60,072, Waverley's record. The ratio was 60,072 people — 20,000 cars. How else could one get there? There was only car or bus. The result was a classic traffic jam. The Sun News Pictorial said the real game started when the footy was over, 'the great new driving-home game, a game that required skill and patience.' It took 1½ hours along 'the crawlway.'

According to the Sun many just stayed there, had picnics or sat in their cars reading magazines and newspapers. One woman reported that she went to the St Kilda-Richmond semi-final at the MCG, left 10 minutes before the siren and was home at Springvale, 12 miles off, in half an hour. Her husband went to Carlton-Collingwood at Waverley and didn't get home to Springvale, 12 miles off, until 6.45 pm ... it took him 1½ hours.

Eric McCutchan says this great traffic jam could easily have been avoided. 'We printed charts. We had them in the newspapers. If only motorists had studied them they would all have been out of the car park with no fuss, no bother. The trouble was they all fought to get to the same exits.

'We had a new freeway opened only temporarily for the occasion. By next year the arteries will have been widened and everything will be much better.' He also said there would be consultations between the premier and the appropriate ministers.

But even when you confront him with the incredible pictures of that traffic jam Eric McCutchan is undaunted. 'Look,' he said, 'for sheer beauty you can't beat Waverley. This is the growth area. This is where the population is going to be. We have only done what the retailers are doing. Look at your Northlands, Southlands, Chadstones and the rest. The trend is away from the city. We are building now for the future as we have to do.'

So the great grand final is really this — the MCG versus Waverley. Obviously, both grounds are needed, but it is a question of degree. There are now four finals at the MCG with two at Waverley. The VFL could decide to reverse this with two at the MCG and four at Waverley. Many believe that with 80,000 capacity Waverley will be ready in 1974 for a grand final.

Sentiment, tradition and fear that Waverley is not yet equipped to handle such a big event will probably make the VFL delegates pause. Perhaps five years from now would be a more likely proposition.

There is another problem to be considered, more serious and strangely ignored. Admission charges will go up heavily next year, yet week by week patronage has never reached the peak of 1963. Last year's attendance for all home matches was 2,934,919. This year it was 2,833,711. 1972 was sunnier than the damp 1973, but should there be any declines in a city with a booming population? Myers would have been horrified with such a result — departmental managers would have been dismissed in legions.
