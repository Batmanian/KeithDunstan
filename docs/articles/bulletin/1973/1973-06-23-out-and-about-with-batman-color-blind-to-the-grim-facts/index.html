Colour blind to the grim facts

5 min read.

First published 23 Jun 1973

Melbourne's commercial dream of selling objects in football club colours always fails, from Gas and Fuel Corporation porcelain stoves in all 12 club colours to Myers' wash-and-wear synthetic wigs at $6.50, while Keith Dunstan's 43-year survey of attendance figures reveals VFL crowds have fallen from nine percent of Melbourne's population in 1930 to only 5.3 percent today.

THERE IS A strange commercial dream in this town that an object has only to be produced in football colors and immediately sales magic will follow. One remembers the poodle shop in South Yarra which produced winter jackets in footy colors. They were reversible with, say, Melbourne team colors on one side and Collingwood's on the other. So should Melbourne be failing miserably at half time a fickle poodle could quickly switch its allegiance to the other team.

One remembers the time when the Gas and Fuel Corporation produced a complete range of gas stoves in all 12 club colors in porcelain enamelled steel. There were also club towels, and husbands no doubt gained an extra satisfaction in drying up the dishes with their favorite side. There were even babies' nappies in club colors.

This season has been a hard one. Peters has produced an ice cream named 'Footy.' It is caramel in flavor, it is in the shape of a football, and presumably you can lick your most beloved football team all over.

Don't go away, there is even worse than that. On the appropriate footy Saturday Myers ran this advertisement: 'Let your football team go to your head — wear a wig in your team's football colors! If you're game — we've a whole new collection of colored synthetic wigs in all the football teams' colors. You'll look fantastic and everyone will see what a strong supporter you are. They're wash and wear, and will fit any head. If it rains — no worries. Just brush your wig out when it's dry. Come select your team colors. Only $6.50.'

Immediately I hurried off to Myers and it was all too true. Right in the main Bourke Street entrance they had a wig stand and fair dinkum, they had a self-conscious young woman behind the counter dressed in an Essendon sweater. Her crowning glory was a nylon wig in brilliant red and black stripes. I got the impression though, that sales were not going awfully well. I asked the spectacular young lady about the state of business and she said, guardedly: 'Er... picking up.'

All right, your pioneer is going to reveal a startling truth. This business of selling objects in footy colors is nearly always a failure in Melbourne. The Gas and Fuel Corporation barely sold a single one of those footy stoves. The poodle footy jackets disappeared after one season, you can no longer buy a footy nappie, a soft drink firm that tried to sell club soft drink gave it away very smartly. I have no indication how Peters is faring with its footy ice cream, but there's not much interest in our suburb.

Indeed, there is a great mass of the population in Melbourne which actually does not follow football. In the interests of social science I have made an elaborate survey. I have gone through the newspapers for 43 years and carefully charted football attendances against population. I have taken samples prior to 1930, but before then one can only obtain crowd estimates, not actual figures. But even so, your crowd-population graph is a steady downhill slope.

A sampling in 1923 shows that 12 percent of the people of Melbourne were watching football every Saturday. By 1930, 9.3 percent were at the football every Saturday afternoon and the population was 1,032,500. The percentage at the game was 9.1.

Just think that over. There was no television, radio was just beginning, and the newspapers gave the game space only on Saturdays and Mondays. Our percentage was still over 9 percent in 1935 and 8.5 in 1938. During the war it dropped way down, but the great euphoric love was up to 9.1 again in 1946. After that, things were never quite the same. From 1952 to 1955 the percentage was around 7.5. In 1960 it was down to 6 and now, despite a hysterical newspaper, radio and TV cover, it is way down to 5.3.

Not only is the game losing in percentage, it is losing in actual numbers. The average weekly parade of devotees last year was 133,405. In terms of numbers, the best year was 1963 when the average was 152,187. Ah, but if we had maintained our percentage of 1946 the weekly turn-out would now be 227,500.

You may well ask why this has come about. Your correspondent has a number of theories. The number one reason is the affluent society. People own motor cars now and there are simply a thousand and one better things to do. Two, conditions at football grounds are dirty and uncomfortable. Three, the city has become very large. Football used to be the passion of an inner ring of industrial suburbs, where people often had quite violent suburban loyalties. The citizens of Collingwood loathed the citizens of Richmond and Richmond people often had a useful contempt for Fitzroy and nobody at all liked North Melbourne.
