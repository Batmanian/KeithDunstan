Wrest Point isn't Tasmania's costliest joint

5 min read.

First published 01 Sep 1973

Hawthorn's desperate and expensive pursuit of returning full forward Peter Hudson, who damaged his knee playing against Melbourne a year ago and has been the subject of front-page leads and middle-page spreads ever since, prompts Keith Dunstan to call for an Anti-Cartilage Council and a Chair of Cartiology at the University of Melbourne, since if a club is buying a player for $60,000 it would be fair to ask that he come pre-operated.

THE MOST MOVING drama of the Melbourne football season has been the return of Peter Hudson. It reminds one of the day when the faithful in Sydney built a little rotunda and waited for the second coming of Christ. They never doubted that if Christ chose to return to earth the logical place would be Sydney. Hawthorn's attitude to Peter Hudson has been similar.

Peter Hudson was the greatest match-winning full forward of modern times, some say of all time. He has not played football since the opening game last year, April 1. On that dreadful day, playing for Hawthorn against Melbourne, he had kicked an amazing eight goals when, just before half time, he fell awkwardly when taking a mark and badly damaged his knee joints and tendons.

He returned home to Hobart, a favorite son, and took over management and part-ownership of a leading hotel at Glenorchy. Ever since, Hawthorn have needed Hudson desperately and both newspapers and television have followed the drama. There have been front-page leads about Hudson's knee, and middle-page spreads, and it is safe to say that not since John Cain stole Tattersalls from Tasmania in 1953 has Hobart been honored with such personal coverage by the Melbourne Press. Reporters and TV teams have been down there for weeks at a time. Back on July 16 there was big news. Hudson would be back to play for Hawthorn on July 28. 'The grandstand will shake,' said one football writer. 'Hawthorn can now take the flag,' said another.

However, Hudson had to continue the operation of his large hotel. He employed up to 60 people on Saturdays. So the great round trip was Melbourne and back in a fully chartered Cessna 310 or a Piper twin Comanche, which would cost around $400 to $500, nigh on $9000 a season. Your correspondent figured that by the time they paid Hudson, the total cost for him couldn't be less than $25,000 a year or around $250 a goal.

But Hudson didn't come on July 28, nor did he come the following Saturday, and the awful tension mounted steadily. Hawthorn's replacement full forward, Michael Moncrieff, was suspended for three weeks, and the pained cry reverberated through Hawthorn, 'What about Hudson?' But still he wasn't ready and Hawthorn went down, out of the final five. There were still twinges in his knee and many suggested that Hudson, like Gunsynd, would never run again. But the second coming finally arrived. Hawthorn, after five false alarms, picked Hudson to play at full forward against Collingwood last Saturday.

I am wondering whether this at last will cause some action. Hudson's knee is certainly not the first tragedy of its kind. John Coleman, Hudson's greatest rival on the full-forward stairs of fame, went out in exactly the same way. One of the greatest continuing stories this year has been Des Tuddenham's knee. Now, Mr Tuddenham is the captain and coach of Essendon, and his passion, devotion and courage literally drives that team. An Essendon without Tuddenham is unthinkable. Some say that Tuddenham's knee has had as much coverage or uncoverage as other world items of anatomy, like Raquel Welch's bosom. One lady, Mrs Peg T. Hall, of Elwood, even wrote a poem to Lou Richards, of the Sun News-Pictorial, which began:

Last week the headlines read to me, What do I read in the headlines? The latest on Tuddy's knee.

The last thing he said to his colleagues: Oh, what are we going on with? On election night in Melbourne During the results of local TV, They interrupted the counting To bring us news of Tuddy's knee. In despair I paid a visit to church Three 'Hail Marys' for Tuddy's knee.

Last week Francis Bourke, the champion of the Tigers, went down with a knee injury and one of the papers had the headline 'Tragedy Strikes Richmond.' Some people claim that 90 percent of all players eventually retire through some form of knee injury, a point that has always received surprisingly little publicity. We have an Anti-Cancer Council, a National Heart Foundation, organisations to fight TB, asthma and polio, but where is the organisation to look after the knee? Why no Anti-Cartilage Council?

Not enough research is going into the problem and maybe it is time the University of Melbourne thought of a Chair of Cartiology. This is not getting things out of proportion. It is generally agreed, among all Victorian newspapermen, that Polly Farmer's knee was perhaps the biggest newspaper story of 1962. It was photographed repeatedly. Zoom lenses came within 2in. of it on television. Teams of specialists discussed it. We followed that cartilage through all its stresses and twists until at season's end it was here that the State Museum missed an opportunity. Mr Farmer's cartilage could have been put on display for all time, like Phar Lap's heart in Canberra.

I'm surprised that the Victorian Football League by this time has not set up a research foundation to discover whether knee-cap transplants are possible. Some surgeons have suggested that all footballers should have their cartilages removed at 17. This is a vital matter. If a club is buying a player for $60,000 it would be fair to ask that he come pre-operated or with spare knee caps.

The knee injury is the most talked about of all injuries. It is as disastrous to the footballer as the fragile sesamoid bone is to the racehorse.

Except there is a difference. Here in Victoria when a racehorse breaks its sesamoid bone we do things more simply. We shoot him.
