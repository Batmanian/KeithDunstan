Sacrilegious identity crisis

5 min read.

First published 24 Feb 1973

The VFL's new ten-year rule allowing veteran players to transfer freely has caused Melbourne a collective mental breakdown, with icons like Doug Wade going to North Melbourne, Carl Ditterich sold to Melbourne for $82,000, and Ron Barassi transferred to Carlton, destroying the tribal suburban loyalties that gave football its passionate meaning.

MELBOURNE could have a total mental collapse this year, a sort of mass nervous breakdown. It could come about because of a dangerous heretical influence in its only deeply felt religion, football. First let me give you a little history. Australian rules football originally became so passionate in Melbourne because it was played between tightly knit suburbs. The clubs recruited within their own suburbs and because of the lack of public transport, spectators came from within their own areas. And it was all very healthy. The people of Richmond loathed the citizens of Collingwood, the men of Collingwood detested those of Carlton, and I think almost everybody hated the gentlemen of Melbourne.

This tradition continued almost to the present day. Jack Dyer in his masterly book 'Captain Blood' writes of his hatred for Collingwood. 'It isn't a friendly dislike — they rankle me. If they win they gloat, if they lose they hide themselves away and sulk. When they lose they never visit your room or congratulate you.' He also writes of Richmond's loathing for North Melbourne. He says it dates from the days when the word went around that two loads of gravel and rocks had been dumped at the players' race at North, ready ammunition for North supporters in the event of defeat. Richmond decided discretion was the better part of valor and forfeited the match.

Do you know that over the years they have had a tradition at Collingwood — an annual dinner where each player is presented with his sacred guernsey? There's a fanfare and out steps the player. A scout comes forward bearing the treasured guernseys on an embroidered cushion. The president makes the presentation. It has all the mystic qualities of the elevation of a bishop or a cardinal.

All right, there's the background. Well, this year the Victorian Football League introduced an extraordinary rule. Players who have given 10 years service to their clubs are now free to transfer to any club of their choice. So chaos has ensued. Doug Wade, Captain of Geelong, one of the great full forwards of football history, has transferred to North Melbourne. After a Geelong premiership I have stood on the steps of Geelong town hall and watched the Cat citizens give him a welcome like a Caesar returning from his triumph after conquering Gaul. He goes for $10,000 a year. Barry Davis of Essendon also has gone to North Melbourne for $10,000. John Rantall of South Melbourne is another to go to North. George Bissett the little god of Footscray has gone to Collingwood.

And nobody knows what Len Thompson will do. Now Mr Thompson, 6ft 6½in, 16 stone 4lb, 25 years, won the 1972 Brownlow Medal. Collingwood offered him $7000 a year. He wants $15,000, $681 a game. So he has retired. Can you begin to imagine the grief this has caused? Take the example of Mark Cameron, 16, of Bentleigh. He saved up $75 out of his weekly $35 earnings as an apprentice butcher, to make a banner 100 feet long. On that banner he painted just two words, Len Thompson. What does he do now?

The biggest case of all is that of Carl Ditterich. He is supposed to be the bogey man of football, a 6ft 4in blond giant. He was suspended for four weeks in 1965, six weeks in 1966, two in 1967, two and four weeks in 1970. Melbourne have bought him from St Kilda for an estimated $82,000, which makes him the most expensive footballer in Victorian history. Ditterich gets $10,000 a year for three seasons and $32,000 when he retires. St Kilda also gets $20,000 to buy him out of a three year contract. Alf Brown of The Herald estimates that his kicks will cost Melbourne between $80 and $100 each.

Then how about the perfidy of Ron Barassi, former captain and coach of Carlton. It was bad enough when he originally left Melbourne back in 1965. I remember letters to the newspapers at the time. One Demon supporter wrote that he sobbed upon reading of the transfer. Is he sobbing still? Another, a lady at Lilydale, said she had taught her budgie to say 'Come on the Demons' and 'Kick a goal Barassi.' Presumably after that episode she strangled her budgie.

All this, I assure you, has received about four times the cover of the Eucharistic Congress. Mr Ditterich's first training evening in Melbourne had the entire back page of The Sun and a little of the front as well. It was almost equally well recorded in The Age.

But what does this do to you mentally when the ones you loathe, the brutes that you have been abusing over the fence for years, suddenly turn up in the guernsey you adore? What happens when your beloved god of only a winter ago now gallops on to the ground as a hated enemy? The mental forces created by such events can only be awe-inspiring. The final result will be breakdown, bitterness, disillusionment.

Dr Margaret Mead, the great anthropologist, has written much on what happens to a tribe when it loses its religion, its ancient culture. The vacuum left behind must absorb something, usually drink and vice.

Evidence of our breakdown is already clear, judging by the enormous amount of canned drink consumed by stupefied spectators at our sporting grounds. What else will we have to fill the vacuum? Perhaps we will go for pokies like NSW or blackjack like Tasmania.
