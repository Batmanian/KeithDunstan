Bubbling over with enthusiasm

5 min read.

First published 08 Dec 1973

Seppelts Great Western opens its first on-site liquor store with an air-conditioned Mercedes bus from Melbourne and champagne flowing from 10.15 am, as Keith Dunstan explores the 1865 gold-miners' underground drives now crammed with two million bottles and meets Jack Floyd, chief shaker for 30 years, who jiggles 25,000 bottles a day to settle the deposit onto the cork.

CELLAR SALES on the spot at the vineyard used to be rather non-u. The retail outlets did not like it nor was the licensing department over keen. Now everybody is in the act. The tourist who drives up to your vineyard is a much-loved creature. You provide a spot for him to cook his barbecue, you even provide a slide, swings and such for the kiddies. The wine does not cost a cent less than the retail price in town, and there's no need really to drive any further than your local licensed grocer. Ah, but down there in the heady atmosphere of the vigneron's cellar, among all the beautiful oak storage, the grape casts a strange spell. Inevitably everyone goes home with a loaded boot.

The business at some vineyards has been enormous. Small family shows have found that now they have to put in a full-time sales staff. The last to break into the act was Seppelts at Great Western, just beyond Ararat on the main road to Adelaide. The tourist traffic there was always tremendous.

Last week Seppelts had a formal opening of their vineyard on-the-spot liquor store and it was all done in grand style. A large air-conditioned Mercedes bus left Melbourne at 8.15 am containing the Director of Tourism, Mr Bill Moran, assorted representatives of the travel industry, liquor commission, wine trade and Press. They started serving the champagne aboard at 10.15 am and from then on it came in a non-stop flow all day. They gave it to us in the tiny 200ml bottles. These are the new champagne-selling triumph — no cork at all. They have a metal ring and a handy rip-top seal with a plastic cap. This is wine's answer to the beer can. The passionate hope is that wherever two or three Australians gather together, whether at the footy, cricket or under a tree, they will now instead get stuck into their mini-bottles of champagne. Will the ecologists object less if the country is littered with champagne bottles instead of cans?

Whatever happens, the sales pitch appears to be working. Ray Moon, national sales director of Seppelts, said they budgeted for a 25 percent increase in champagne selling for 1973, but they have gone way beyond that.

Well, as you know, the Great Western vineyard is one of the sights of the country. There are 1½ miles of underground cellars, called drives, because they were dug by the gold miners circa 1865. They are crammed with about two million bottles.

But the crammed drives are really no good for the intense production of 1973. There are now vast brick buildings for storage, and a man with forklift truck can do the work of 25 down below. They still use correct champagne methods, but they have been pepped up somewhat. The Imperial Reserve, a 15-month champagne, spends, you will be shocked to hear, 12 months of its life with a crown seal. The vintage Brut Imperial Reserve is five to six years old and gets handled in the classic manner (no crown seals there) about 35 times.

I went down into the drives and they are quite marvellous. The walls are covered with a long black hairy mould. When you touch them it is like stroking a cocker spaniel. They are named after the great of the past, Lord Hopetoun Drive, Sir Dallas and Lady Brooks Drives, Lord Huntingfield and Dame Nellie Melba. Dame Nellie went there in 1902 and 1929 and legend has it that in 1902 they gave her a bath in champagne. I don't believe the legend. It would take at least 180 bottles and no champagne company would be as generous as that.

However, seeing the wine industry is apparently concerned about taxes, it would be a very good stunt if they invited both Mr Whitlam and Mr Crean to Great Western and gave them a bath in champagne before the TV cameras. Then in their honor there would be the new Whitlam and the Crean Drives and a brand-new legend.

But I have been side-tracked, or side-drived. Down below I met Jack Floyd, the chief shaker. The shaker's job is to shake the bottle so that the deposit from the secondary fermentation moves down and settles on the top of the cork. He does this to each bottle once a week over a period. He turns it through an angle of about 10 degrees and his jiggling has to be done with marvellous precision. Not since I once saw a character in a U.S. roadside diner making hamburgers, have I noted such superb economy of movement. He was a motion study dream. He went through the racks jiggling a bottle with either hand, at amazing speed. He admitted that he shook 25,000 bottles a day, but had done 30,000 on a good day. Well, he's been doing it for 30 years, so he must be approaching a total of 225 million bottles jiggled.

He retires in another five years and he is training another shaker. But Jack made it clear that this was a technical job and the under-shaker would have to go through a long apprenticeship.

We returned to the surface for the formal opening. Mr John Seppelt, the company director, was there. He said that when his grandfather first came to this country Mr Charles Sturt investigated him to make sure he was a suitable migrant. But that was nothing to the rigorous investigation that the Victorian Licensing Commission had conducted to make sure they were suitable people to have a liquor store at Great Western.

Mr Moran made a speech telling us what a good thing it was to encourage tourists like this, then he took a bottle of Imperial Reserve Seppelts champagne, told us what a sacrilege it was to break it, then smashed it against the wall of the new liquor store.

I thought about the smashed bottle and as the liquid trickled across the concrete I tried to bear the sacrilege. I knew there were at least another five million of them inside.
