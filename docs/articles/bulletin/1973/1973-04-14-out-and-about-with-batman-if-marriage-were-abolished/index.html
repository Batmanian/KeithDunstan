If marriage were abolished

5 min read.

First published 14 Apr 1973

The Whitlam government's proposed divorce reforms prompt Keith Dunstan to calculate the social and economic consequences of marriage's abolition, from confused dinner party seating and turbulent social pages through to the collapse of the wedding photography and reception industry and the ruin of jewellers.

TO TELL you the truth I am very concerned about marriage. Mr Whitlam should have called me in as another one of his consultants.

The Catholic Church here in Melbourne has looked at the proposed new divorce laws and a spokesman for Cardinal Knox said last weekend that it was clear Senator Murphy wanted to abolish marriage. I wouldn't be surprised. Remember those beautiful days last December when Gough had just taken over and Mrs Whitlam was saying everything. Someone asked her then about marriage and she said it had lost its significance. 'I was for it 30 years ago, but not now,' she said.

It is certainly disappearing in many areas. There was a story from New York last week. Two baseballers and their wives had decided to swap partners. Oh yes, they still loved their wives, they said, but thought a change at this time of life would be very good for them. Melbourne has quite a remarkable number of footballers. Most of them are changing their teams right now — I'm sure they will find it equally easy to change their wives.

Mr Whitlam, too, is promising low rent flats for newlyweds. As cartoonist Colette pointed out in the Melbourne Herald the other day he is also offering the cheap quickie divorce and the cheap pill. I mean you can just keep on getting married, with a new wife every year, and a new low rent flat every year. Life could be very interesting.

However, if Gough or Lionel Murphy do call me in as a consultant, there are a few things I'd like to point out before they abolish marriage.

I have been married for a long time and nearly all our friends are married. It has been a very convenient arrangement really, particularly for dinner parties. I'm a shocker for remembering names. I even write names of wives and vice versa down in a little book so that I will remember them.

It is very nice if you ask, say, Bill to know that it is 99.9 per cent certain he will bring along Angela, that Joan will bring along Murray, that Bob will bring Elizabeth. I mean if Bob suddenly turns up with 18-year-old Cheryl, and Joan has done a swap for a ruckman from St Kilda, you're in total confusion. How the blazes do you keep track of people?

Oh, I suppose you could put notices in the newspapers. One of the tabloids had a fascinating notice just a few weeks back. A Miss So-and-So of Frankston wished to announce the birth of her son at Frankston Community Hospital. Both she and the father, Fred So-and-So of Balwyn, were happy. You could get variations on this theme: Fred Chislehurst who was living with May Ockington, has now teamed up with Roma Beaver, and Roma's friend Cedric Ockroyd has switched to tram conductress Elsie. Fred Fosby of South Yarra wishes to announce that he has left his friends in Great Western Street, and has now moved to a commune in Carlton where he is living with Freda, Brigitte and Norma. Or: 'Harry and Michael who flatted together in Walsh Street have swapped partners with Roger and Peter who flat in Caroline Street.'

Think of the possibilities! Look how the social notes will be stepped up in pace! Not only will we have engagement and marriage parties but at least for a time we will have a proliferation of divorce parties. And, if the style of marriage changes, undoubtedly it will have its impact on the federal sphere.

Wives have always been very useful electorally. I mean look at Joe Lyons and Enid. Look at Robert and Dame Pattie. How could Billy McMahon have succeeded without Sonia? What about Harold and Zara?

We will have a remarkable situation if there is a new wife in the Prime Minister's Lodge every 12 months. Personally I would be most interested to see what effect this would have on Woman's Day. Would they also change their columnist every time?

Then, of course, there will be another interesting angle. Our children suffer murder from aunts, uncles, relatives and such. Every time they come they like to play the game which we call 'are they really legitimate?' 'David's got his father's chin and his mother's hair... but isn't it odd, Jane doesn't really look like either of them.' Under the new Senator Murphy era everyone will not only have more relatives but conversation will be more exciting.

Then there is the economic side. The number of people actually supported by the marriage trade is enormous. There are 10 pages of photographers in the pink pages, most of them live off wedding pictures. Kinky wedding shots are terribly in now. You have yourself looking at your bride sitting up in a tree at the botanic gardens or in the ghost train at Luna Park.

There are reception houses, which also live off weddings. You can't play it straight there any more either. You can go Don Quixote Spanish, Colonel Sanders American colonial, wall-to-wall carpeted, air-conditioned medieval-British, table-thumping community singing, beer-drinking Munich or even C. J. Dennis, Sentimental Bloke Australian.

There are 173 taxi firms and you can be taken to the church by vintage car, Rolls-Royce, or, and it's terribly 'in' at the moment, by horse drawn vehicles, hansom cabs, coaches, Buckingham Palace landaus and such.

Then think of all the clergy, choir boys, organists, flower shops and all. Think of the jewellers, the engagement ring industry. If there are quickie divorces all these will flourish superbly with more and more marriages, but if as the Catholic Church fears, marriage disappears altogether, then, I am fearful.

Personally, I do not propose to throw vast co-habit receptions if any of my daughters decide on this novel idea.
