A man as tall as a telegraph pole

5 min read.

First published 15 Sep 1973

David Williamson returns from London after The Removalists' West End run, surprised by critics who called it evil, revolting and unsuitable for Australian tourists, while Harry M. Miller's 60-foot sign outside the Royal Court Theatre confirms the classic Australian principle that you must win distinction overseas before Australians will admit you are any good.

DAVID WILLIAMSON is back home again. 'I came straight through from London in 30 hours,' he said. 'I went through all the classic jet withdrawal agonies. It took me three days to recover — my body not knowing where it was. Going to sleep at the wrong times. Waking up at the wrong times. I'd never do it again.'

One imagined David Williamson trying to drape his 6ft 7in frame around a Qantas seat for 30 hours on end, and even the thought was enough to give rigor mortis to the legs.

Harry M. Miller has written of the excitement, the pure pleasure it gave him to see a sign 60 feet high illuminated outside the prestige West End Royal Court theatre, The Removalists, by David Williamson.

Before that, only the little Nimrod theatre had taken an interest in The Removalists. It was an example of the classic Australian principle, almost a Newtonian law of motion: You have to win distinction overseas before Australians will admit you are any good.

Naturally you are still suspicious 'how did you really go over there?' you ask.

'Pretty well,' he said. 'I was very happy with the reviews I received from the Times and the Guardian.' But he was surprised at some of the others, not so much for knocks they may have handed out but the way they interpreted the play.

The Daily Mail, for example said, 'What a clobber for the cobbers' and went on to say that it would hardly win any awards from the Australian tourist commission. The Daily Telegraph said, 'Don't join the police force in Sydney.'

'My favorite though,' he said, 'was Harold Hobson in the Observer. He wrote, "Very competently and powerfully written but an evil play. Properly it should be staged in a brothel."

'Evil? That astonished me. I have never been called evil before.

'Then Milton Shulman in the Evening Standard wrote, "I have rarely laughed at anything so much, that was so revolting."'

So we tried to look for reasons. 'Yes,' Williamson said, 'the play was a shock to some critics. This wasn't the sort of play they expected from Australia. They have an image of Australia as an indolent, amiable place, where there is no violence. And they misread entirely what I was trying to say about police brutality. This was only a fragment of the story, really. The real brutality comes ultimately from the young copper because he is inexperienced and he is goaded beyond endurance by the people all around him.

'And of course, this was shocking in London. They like always to think, anyway, that their police have an image of being very fair and humane.'

But what about the other point of view, as suggested by the Daily Mail. There are plenty of people in high places out here who believe that Australian State playwrights should write pure, wholesome little plays as public relations for the Australian tourist commission.

'This is true,' he sighed. 'Some of the Australians who saw it in London were a bit narked. They said, "This is an image we are trying to forget."

'Once such people get to London they build up a terrific image in their minds of what Australia is like; then they get all worried about what the English think of us.

'But what can I do? I can't do anything else but draw on my own Australian experience, write the play as I see it and hope that this theme is not just local ... but universal.

'Yes, we did get good houses. And I don't think they were all just Australians living in London. The cast told me you could pick the nights. One night there would be Australians out there and the Oz gags would really go over. The next night the same gags would fall flat.'

So what's next? He says that Don's Party will go to London before the end of the year. Then The Removalists will open in Cleveland, Ohio. 'I wouldn't be surprised if this doesn't do better in the U.S. than in London,' he says. 'I think they will understand the theme.

'If it's a success it would follow the classic pattern. It would move to off-Broadway somewhere up New York State, then on to Broadway itself.'

One can only sit in envy and observe a phenomenon — a young Australian playwright, so busy he might be an advertising man. Or a real estate developer. Here he was trying to cope with interviews, trying to move house, and at the same time dashing to Sydney for the weekend.

'I've got to work with the cast and polish up lines for What If You Died Tomorrow,' he says. 'I have to do some last minute re-writes. That always happens with a new play. It's going on at the Opera House.' The lofty David Williamson reminds one of a T. S. Eliot line which seemed to go like this, 'God bless my soul, the man's as tall as a telegraph pole.'

He's certainly a telegraph pole in the Australian theatre.
