Getting a visa for Ayers Rock

5 min read.

First published 17 Nov 1973

A House of Representatives committee's recommendation to restrict tourist access to Ayers Rock as a national heritage area would create the world's first example of tourist rationing, prompting Keith Dunstan to propose relieving the pressure by turning Hobart into a free port, building a Bibleland near Alice Springs, and appointing an anti-tourist officer to restrict access to Phar Lap, Chloe and Ned Kelly's skull.

LAST WEEK A House of Representatives standing committee on environment and conservation recommended restrictions on tourist access to Ayers Rock. The committee says that the whole Ayers Rock — Mount Olga national park should be called a 'national heritage area.'

The chairman of the committee, Dr Henry Jenkins, said there had been too much enthusiasm for encouraging tourist convenience and access without thinking about the effect on the environment.

Do you realise what this means? Apart from the stand-offish communist countries this could be the world's first example of tourist rationing. And the rationing of tourists had to come. Anyone who has travelled the world during the past two years knows the agony of being a tourist, of being landed, say, in that vast ant-heap of misery, Chicago airport, of trying to hump one's bags to find a bus, to find a taxi, to find anything to get out of the maze.

A tourist in Europe is now a humiliated, loathed piece of merchandise. I have stood with aching knees for two hours waiting for my turn to see over a royal palace or a cathedral in Vienna. When your turn does come you are shovelled en masse and you are damned lucky if you get to see the glories of Frederick the Great or whoever, because the guide calls 'keep moving.' The production line must never slow. Nowhere is it any different. It might take you four hours to get into the Tower of London, or even longer in the great queues for Chartres Cathedral, St Peters in Rome, Westminster Abbey or Versailles. I believe that there are even queues to look at the Duke of Bedford's lion park. He has a chairlift there which takes the tourists on a two-mile trip above the English lions, elephants, giraffes and such.

London has closed off Oxford Street for large parts of the day to cope with the invading money-spending hordes. A year or so ago the crafty tourist used to go to Europe in the winter and inspect the pyramids in the summer, even go to Hawaii and Tahiti in the wet season. Not any more. The cheap off-season jumbo jet package tours have fixed all that. The world tourist centres are now equally vile all the year round.

So you see Ayers Rock could be a trend setter. The House standing committee hasn't explained yet how the tourist rationing would work. Presumably you will have to apply for a special Ayers Rock visa and these will be issued at Northern Territory consulates, euphemistically called tourist bureaus. Visas will be rare and they will have all the excitement of a visit to Peking and a tour of the great wall.

An alternative would be to take some of the pressure off Ayers Rock. The trouble is that in Australia we have far too few tourist attractions to go round. The Americans are very clever at this — apart from their two Disneylands I see that they are now building a huge Bibleland. All the attractions of the Bible are being re-created — the Tower of Babel, the falling walls of Jericho, Golgotha and the Crosses, and you will be able to see Christianity working as it originally happened. We could have a Bibleland near Alice Springs. The terrain and climate would be ideal.

I think lion parks have been a little overdone, but we could have a people's park. An ideal locale would be just out of Cairns. You could fill this park with the swinging generation — hippies, commune-lovers, pot-smokers, pop festival lovers. Tourists could then drive through with their windscreens wound up and see how the other generation lives.

An engineer at Swinburne College in Melbourne has come up with two ingenious ideas. One is the creation of a new reef in Port Phillip Bay by the dumping of thousands of used cars. This would be perfect for creating a fishing habitat. He also suggests the building of a 3000 ft mountain just outside Melbourne, to be made of rubbish. The mountain eventually could be beautified and turned into Australia's first suburban skiing resort. What with all the empty cans which would be used in its construction, no doubt it would be called Mt Fosters.

The nostalgia trade should also be emphasised. The vintage train runs around Victoria are now doing good business. I would do these on a vast scale. Instead of using modern diesels on the Indian-Pacific run, immense business could be had by acquiring a famous vintage steam train like the Flying Scotsman. Americans find famous old steam engines utterly irresistible. There would need to be something exciting at the other end, so perhaps it would be wise to turn Perth into a free port, a local Singapore, and load it with cheap cameras, transistors, mini TV sets, watches and tape recorders.

Meanwhile, here in Melbourne, the local Minister for Tourism has launched a million-dollar campaign to attract tourists to Victoria. The theory is that one third of international tourists never get past Sydney. Once they have seen the harbour and the Opera House they feel that they have seen Australia.

Some of us, however, would be inclined to put forward the argument that the tourists are not wanted anyway. I think we should announce right now that we are appointing an anti-tourist officer. That from now on the numbers of visitors to see Captain Cook's cottage, Phar Lap, Chloe and Ned Kelly's skull will be heavily restricted. You will have to state in writing why you want to go into the Melbourne Cricket Ground and see one of our sacred football matches.

In short, Melbourne will be on the restricted list and you will need a visa to enter. I pass this idea on to the Minister, Mr Murray Byrne. It could be the greatest tourist gimmick yet invented.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Getting a visa for Ayers Rock, The Bulletin, 17 November 1973. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
