Initiation into the wine cult

4 min read.

First published 24 Mar 1973

Keith Dunstan attends two landmark first-vintage dinners in the Hunter Valley, visiting the ultra-modern automated winery at Hollydene near Muswellbrook and Rothbury's ecclesiastical great hall, where he receives a low pass in his grape-identification test and Len Evans advises him to drink harder.

THE FASCINATING thing about the old Hunter was its true, indigenous pioneering look. There were the uprights of vintage bleached gum, galvanised iron and, of course, earth floors au naturel. Tourists would come to the area expecting to see something like French chateaux, and would look a bit puzzled to find not even a cellar.

Then there were the wine-making methods. When temperatures rose too high the wine-maker would toss in two and a half hundredweight of ice and the classic scene to be witnessed was Murray Tyrrell thrusting his arm into the fermenting wine. Yes, he could judge progress by the sensation of prickles. Why, in 1966 Max Lake at Lake's Folly actually foot trod his first vintage and I understand some of Sydney's leading virgins took part in the tramping, just to ensure the mystic sanctity of the first wine.

I tell you, it is almost hard to believe the change. Recently your correspondent has been to two grand first occasions. Number one was the first vintage of Hollydene on the Wybong road not far from Muswellbrook in the Upper Hunter. It is the result of the restless inspired energies of Marie and Ken Commins. Ken Commins started all this five years ago at an age when most men have already long since retired.

Now a million dollars later he has 200 acres of semillon and cabernet grapes, intensely cultivated. They have been raised on a refined and improved drip system. The vines are like so many patients in a surgical ward on intravenous feeding.

But the winery! It's awe-inspiring. You can see it from miles away. It is a winery-in-the-round, but medieval in character. It looks like London's original Shakesperian Globe Theatre. For heaven's sake, there's a sign outside asking you to wipe your feet. Inside, it was simply beautiful — like the inside of an automated ice cream factory, poetry in stainless steel, coloured plastic tubes and painted girders. You don't see wine at all — the grapes go straight into the crusher, the juice is pumped up into stainless steel fermenting tanks which are in a lofty circular gallery. Then it goes down through the press and into underground tanks.

All this is the conception of Ian Hickinbotham, the wine-maker, one-time Kaiser Stuhl, one-time Penfolds, presently owner of Gini's restaurant in Toorak. Goodness knows how he sticks his arm in to check on progress. In the midst of all this clinical purity there was a party to celebrate the first vintage with champagne and John Walkers Australian lamb Bennelong pies.

The second great occasion was the inaugural ribbon dinner at Rothbury. Now as you know Rothbury has an edifice which is known as the Pokolbin Opera House and it looks as big as Melbourne's Exhibition Building. It stands guard over 750 acres of vines. The chairman of Rothbury, Len Evans, wants to make Rothbury a cult, complete with society.

One can't begin to describe how noble the great hall of Rothbury looked. You know how some people go to church just to observe the ceiling. This ceiling was magnificent, dark beams against white, so ecclesiastical you instinctively tended to cross yourself. Then down the nave were three vast baronial tables, on which were cheese, bowls of fruit, earth-coloured crockery. The meal was melon and prosciutto, followed by rack of lamb and whole beans and cheese.

But the big exercise of the evening was the training of members. There are four degrees of ribbon membership — white, green, red and purple. Purple is immensely illustrious and perhaps no more than 20 will ever aspire to the purple.

We were given five glasses and told to identify the grape varieties. We were told they were blanquette, verdelho, chardonnay, rhine riesling and semillon. Mr Evans gave us hints on the characteristics of each, then we had to identify them.

We filled out our examination papers and handed them in. Then came the prize giving. About 20 people scored a green, including a number of ladies. It was like a degree conferring ceremony: they stepped forward, shook hands with Rudy Komon and he placed the ribbon, which was adorned with a pewter Rothbury tasting cup, around their necks. Next came the triumphant white ribbon winners, who had received a low pass. Your correspondent was advised by Mr Evans that his paper was 'a total disaster.' 'The only thing for you to do,' said he, 'is to keep in training. Drink harder. Show more application next term.'
