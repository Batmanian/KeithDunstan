Full steam ahead for GM-H boss

5 min read.

First published 16 Jun 1973

Bill Gibbs, managing director of General Motors-Holden and new chairman of the loss-making Victorian Railways, should take a lesson from Echuca, where the annual steam and traction rally assembles entire squadrons of vintage Fowler steam engines, a steam pipe-organ and an eight-ton 1924 Sentinel steam truck whose party trick is tipping in three directions.

MR BILL GIBBS, managing director of General Motors-Holden soon will become chairman of the Victorian Railways, a failing concern which is losing an escalating $38 million a year. You may have heard we have had a few strikes here lately. The drivers are not satisfied with the safety and comfort of the new Silver Bullet suburban trains. Some enthusiasts have already suggested that as power units for these trains Mr Gibbs may wish to switch to GM-H Sedan de Villes or Kingswoods or (why not?) GT Monaros.

Personally I think this would be the wrong approach. There should be a return to steam and if Mr Gibbs would care to go to the Murray town of Echuca he would find enough steam to power the entire Victorian railways.

Echuca has become the vintage steam capital of the world; a marvellous place in which to find old steam engines, because they were used in the Mallee to clear land for soldier settlers, used on the farms for digging dams, used for making chaff, ploughing, everything.

I have spent my life making a special study of nostalgia freaks and I used to think the tram lovers were the most obsessed, but now I believe the steam lovers are the furthest gone of them all. It is a very strange, acquisitive disease. It is not enough just to possess one steam roller or 20-ton traction engine — you have to go on acquiring more and more. There are at least six gentlemen around Echuca who possess entire squadrons of steam engines and all lined up together they look like some gargantuan puffing armored division of awesome power.

For example, there is Rod Watson, of Moama, who has a 100-acre dairy farm on the banks of the Murray. He was reported recently as having 15 engines on the property plus 20 steam tractors. The flagship of his fleet is his 1919, 18-ton Fowler steam forest clearing locomotive. He has it fitted with a fog horn from a World War I battleship. As he rumbles across the paddocks and lets loose a few blasts, it causes considerable surprise among the cows.

On the Queen's Birthday weekend the armored might of Echuca got together for its 10th annual steam and traction rally. As far as the eye could see there were puffing, hissing, panting metal monsters, dray-loads of good red gum firewood were being pushed around to cater for their ravenous appetites and the flats were alive with the constant shrieking of steam whistles.

Actually it was worse than that. One steam-lover, Sam Marshall, had invented a pipe organ driven by steam. It was a single pipe, attached to a lever, and by adjusting the length of pipe you could get a one-and-a-half octave range on the shriek. Oh, he did things properly! He brought up from Melbourne, Bruce Brown, organist at St James Church, Glen Iris. So all day we had a steam whistle version of 'Edelweiss,' 'If you knew Susie,' 'Waltzing Matilda' (in honor of Mr Whitlam) and even a St James Glen Iris number or two, like 'Onward Christian Soldiers.'

Mr Marshall had the most imposing machine there, Susie Jane, a 1920 Fowler, height about 16 feet and when steamed for action, 27 tons. Oh lovely, it was, clean, shining, with a great spinning flywheel and lots of exposed gears and lots of satisfying pistons pumping back and forth.

It had a massive winch underneath which took 1000 yards of cable. The idea was this. You put one puffing monster on one side of the paddock, another on the other and at 6 mph you winched a 10-furrow plough back and forth. According to Marshall, you could do 50 acres in a day.

It is not very hard to pick the steam freaks — they tend to wear blue overalls and authentic blue engine-driver caps. Look for the total absence of skin on the knuckles and permanent grease stain in the pores and under the fingernails. Wives sometimes have a haunted look through not having seen their husbands, except for feet protruding out of a boiler, for about five years.

But, by golly, there were some fascinating vehicles. Stan Hazelman has enough steam power to light all Melbourne, but for my money the best is his 1924 Sentinel steam truck. It weighs eight tons, the boiler is right in the driving cabin and you drop in the red gum logs as you trundle along — speed 20 mph, automatic transmission, no brakes, just turn off the steam or slam her into reverse. It used to belong to the St Kilda council, and it was the wonder of Melbourne.

Stan recently had it at a motor show. A salesman was demonstrating the brilliance of his 1972 tip truck. The story goes that Stan said: 'That's nothing — can your truck do this?' Just by turning a valve Stan could make his truck tip straight back. Then by releasing two catches he could make it tip to the left, or to the right.

Another fleet owner is Phil Symons, an Echuca chemist. He has an assortment of 11 traction engines and steam rollers, but with a local solicitor, Ian Stewart, he also owns a steam-powered river boat, called the Etona. It won this name back in 1898 because the Bishop of Adelaide was an Etonian, and the Etona used to ply up and down the river as a floating chapel. Now Phil Symons and Ian Stewart have converted it into an 11-berth cruise ship, with toilet, hot and cold showers and galley and the aft-cabin with five bunks is the old chapel. But I understand that Mr Symons does not dispense any church services.

It was a great nostalgia day for exhibitions of log buggies, log splitting, rope-making, spinning, weaving and there was even an old character there who was ladling out fair dinkum drovers stew from an iron pot. Mr Gibbs might get on to that also. It's a vast improvement on the railways' pies.
