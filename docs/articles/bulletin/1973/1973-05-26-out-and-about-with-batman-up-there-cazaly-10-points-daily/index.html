Up there, Cazaly (10 points daily)

5 min read.

First published 26 May 1973

The Broadcasting Control Board's new Australian content points system throws an imagined commercial TV station into crisis, as its executives frantically try to squeeze poetry, literary criticism and ballet out of 81 hours of weekly football coverage, eventually hitting on a David Williamson sex-and-football drama combining 'Number 96' with the MCG.

IT IS NOT generally realised what an awful shock the Broadcasting Control Board's new TV code has been. The new points system was leaked only last week and for your benefit I have the full tape of the bugging of Austral Violence Television's resulting conference. As you must be aware, AVT-11 Melbourne has easily the top ratings in Australia.

I can't positively identify who was present, but I do have their first names. Here is a faithful record of what took place:

Harry: Jeez, are they out of their flaming tiny minds? I reckon it's just a plot, a bloody ALP plot to ruin us.

Ron: Yair, it will be lovely won't it. We'll go to British Tobacco or Unilever and ask 'em, Please will you sponsor an hour of poetry readings at seven o'clock on Thursday nights. Gawd. Can you imagine!

Alf: Or an hour of ballet. Yair, yair, Melbourne just adores ballet.

Harry: It's not fair. We've done more Australian content than any other channel in Australia. Look at that gold toady award up there. How did we get that? By giving the public what they want. Alf, let's have a look at the figures again.

Alf: Mmmm, well, each week we give 72 hours to football and football comment, nine hours to boxing and five hours to Hector's 'Homicide' and things like that.

Ron: And that's work for Australians on Australian TV.

Harry: Those Labor blokes don't give a stuff about that.

Alf: All right, look here. Your 'Homicide' gives you five points, right? Your sport gives you half a point an hour.

Ron: It's bloody wicked.

Alf: Now wait a minute. Your ballet, poetry and local drama is worth 10 points. Now our 81 hours a week of football and boxing is worth only 40.5 points, but if we switched it to poetry and stuff we'd get 810 mighty points.

Harry: Yair, and who'd be watching? Eric Westbrook and Dame Mabel Brookes.

Alf: No, I've got an idea. We'll combine the two. Who's that woman up in Queensland who's always carrying on about pollution? Judith someone or other.

Harry: Wright.

Alf: Yair, Judith Wright. Hire her. Well, why not? We announce the teams for Saturday on Thursday nights — why not get her to put them all into verse. There's your 10 points for a start. She could do them in heroic couplets.

Ron: Bloody oath. Why have couplets? Ever heard any of this modern poetry? It doesn't even rhyme any more. You'd get away with anything. And we could have a footy quiz, Richmond players versus Carlton.

Harry: Wake up. We do that already, and quiz shows are worth only half a point.

Ron: Well, um, let's see. It says here literary criticism is worth 10 points.

Alf: Now you're talking. I see a program for that. All our great commentators, Lou Richards, Jack Dyer, Harry Beitzel, Percy Beames make their predictions. We could get a panel to discuss their opinions and the results. Why not — Max Harris, John Pringle, Ross Campbell... all discussing the game in a nice literary style.

Harry: Tremendous. But we've got nine hours of sport on Sunday. Keeps us afloat. How we going to handle that?

Ron: Yair...ah... yair. And the footy replays on Saturday night. I reckon we could say there's a relationship between footy and ballet. We could run the film to the music of Sleeping Beauty, Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy, n'stuff like that. But the Sunday sporting show might be more difficult.

Alf: I've got an idea, but it might be expensive.

Harry: Bugger the cost, we've got to do it.

Alf: I suggest we bring in John Sumner from the Melbourne Theatre Company — you know, the whole lot from the Russell Street Theatre and we could get David Williamson to write the script.

Ron: Turn it up. Did you see his last couple of plays? Gawd, that 'Don's Party' for instance, the language and the sex on stage — phew, burn your ears and singe your eyeballs.

Harry: Great. If it's art we'll get away with it and 10 points an hour at the same time.

Alf: Yair — can you imagine it? 'The Last Tango at the MCG' ... an R-certificate Australian Rules football show. That would be the greatest combination in the history of the world.

Alf: Well, I don't see why we can't do it. For news we can get the theatre company either from there or at the Pram Factory to re-enact all the beaut incidents on Saturday, the blokes that get kicked on the ground, the fights — they could do a beautiful modern ballet sequence with boozing in the outer, can throwing, even chundering.

Harry: I think you're really cooking at last, Alf. And Williamson could use his talents on the inner tensions of the thing. I see, say, the nymphomaniac daughter of a top coach — she's hungry for sex with the 6ft 4in, $20,000-a-year, but failing top ruckman of the hated rival team. Not only is there awful tension here, but this $20,000 ruckman is not delivering the goods. I mean, he's not in the best-player list for 13 consecutive Saturdays. His fitness-fanatic coach has banned all sex. The girl's father and the rival coach catch them both having sex in the dressing-room half an hour before the start of play. Imagine what David Williamson could do with that.

Ron: Oh boy, what tremendous television. Number 96 with high-marking! We could do it in colour and sell it all over the world.

Harry: Come to think of it — I believe Senator McClelland is a fine man. He's lifting us out of ourselves.

Alf: And Judith Wright and David Williamson will be grateful for the work that we'll be giving them. I wonder if we can work in Patrick White some way.

Source: out and about with Batman: Up there, Cazaly (10 points daily), The Bulletin, 26 May 1973. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
