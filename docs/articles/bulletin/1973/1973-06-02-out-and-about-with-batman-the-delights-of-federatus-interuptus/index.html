The delights of federatus interuptus

5 min read.

First published 02 Jun 1973

Keith Dunstan proposes that Australia's restless states capitalise on Queensland's secession agitation by de-federating on May 9, 1974 or 1975 with all the pomp of the 1901 Federation opening in Melbourne, complete with marching girls, six separate national anthems and Queensland's military likely taking over Government House.

YOU MUST have noticed what is brewing. Queensland wants to secede. Western Australia and Tasmania have wanted to secede for years. Victoria is convinced that its taxpayers alone are virtually supporting the entire commonwealth. New South Wales thinks the rest of the commonwealth is having it on. South Australia knows it is intellectually superior. All the States in defiance of Gough Whitlam are appealing to the privy council against acts of the commonwealth. Decentralism is the new, exciting, rallying cry. So why wait — let's do it.

As an amateur historian, could I point out how terribly interesting this is. We could have a lovely function and celebration. The actual day on which the Duke of Cornwall and York opened Commonwealth Parliament, the true day of Federation was May 9, 1901. I think we should stick to this date and try to organise our secession or freedom day for either May 9, 1974, or May 9, 1975. And, I feel, we should de-federate with equal pomp and magnificence to what we did in 1901. And I'd like it to be in Melbourne.

Yes. Melbourne was superbly decorated, beautiful illuminations, triumphal arches all over the place. For example, at the corner of Collins and Russell Streets we had a whopper, big as the Arc de Triomphe. It was the Queen Victoria Arch complete with a sculpture of her late majesty on top, plus a crown.

Now for the opening ceremony we had 12,000 people seated in the Exhibition Building — the largest concourse of people ever seen indoors in Australia, according to 'The Argus.' The Duke said: 'I now declare the Parliament of Australia open.' The Duchess of York touched an electric button and that was the signal for trumpets to start blasting, for cannon to start booming and for Union Jacks to go up poles on all the State schools right across the continent. The 12,000 stood up and cheered and Mr Musgrove's opera company sang 'Rule Britannia' and 'The Hallelujah Chorus.'

That night there was the state concert for 10,000. 'The most magnificent function ever attempted in Melbourne,' the Exhibition was tastefully decorated with bunting and ferns and Nellie Stewart sang 'Australia,' an anthem specially composed for the occasion.

Oh, there was a march past of 10,000 citizens composed of the friendly and temperance societies and the trade union unions. There was a march past of 15,000 troops at Flemington and the greatest fireworks display in the history of the colony at Albert Park Lake.

Naturally, we must do this equally beautifully in reverse. Prince Charles or even Prince Philip would be eager to come here for the de-federation ceremony and I am sure we can get a booking day for the Exhibition around May 9 — we can squeeze it in between the motor show and the home-furnishing display. There's no hope of finding 15,000 uniformed troops for a march past at Flemington, but I'm not despairing — we could double that number with marching girls.

I don't know about Nellie Stewart singing 'Australia.' After all, Mr Whitlam was running a competition to find a national anthem. Now with de-federation it will be much more interesting and exciting, each state will be able to have its own competition and we will be able to sing six lovely national anthems at the new ceremony.

I understand that archways are no longer popular as decoration for big occasions because they impede the traffic, but here in Melbourne we can now do better. We always hang such lovely things from our tram wires at Moomba and at Christmas. Then at federation there was a whole crop of tea towels which sported pictures of the Duke and Duchess. For de-federation mugs with pictures of Prince Charles and any young lady he cared to bring.

Personally I am more interested in how the six new separate nations would go their own way. The army would have to be divided. There are now nine battalions, but these are rapidly being reduced to six, so each state could take one battalion and each state, of course, would get four Fills plus a few Mirages. Queensland under Mr Bjelke-Petersen, I feel would be glad to get away from the rotting, effete attitudes of the south and would move further to the right.

I wouldn't be surprised if the GOC North Command, now General Office commanding the Queensland Army did not move into Government House and take over government, probably some time in 1975.

Tasmania on the other hand would move the other way. Immediately the Tasmanian Government would declare Hobart a free port and we Melburnians would rush there for our cheap trannies, mini-TV sets and cameras. Hobart would also have Hong Kong overtones of exotic sin. Some of Hobart's best A-graded National Trust Houses would be turned into establishments of joy.

There would be talk of an economic union but it wouldn't work. After each state treasurer had failed to balance his budget for the first time customs barriers would be set up at Albury, Wodonga, Coolangatta, Birdsville, Mt Gambier and all the rest. It would be particularly necessary to cope with all the trannies and cheap scotch coming back from Hobart.

There would be all sorts of advantages, each state as a separate nation would be able to field national sporting sides. Our cricket matches against New South Wales would become test matches, our governors would become people of importance once more and the system of honours-giving would take on a new glow and glory.

As for Canberra, you wanted to know about that didn't you? Commonwealth public servants will be no problem. There will be such a bureaucratic boom in each state that they will be quickly absorbed. Yet many politicians will be out of work and here the saving will be immense. Canberra of course, will have some very imposing and unused buildings. But we can have our own 'ruin,' our own dead town, marvellous for tourists. Imagine the tourist buses pulling up all day outside the dead, deserted Government House. What lectures the guides could give, what tales they could tell the tourists about those incredible, absurd years, spent between 1927 and 1974.

Source: out and about with Batman: The delights of federatus interuptus, The Bulletin, 2 June 1973. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
