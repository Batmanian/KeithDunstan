Fashion gets it in the neck

5 min read.

First published 03 Nov 1973

Attending the Sydney Opera House opening in an outdated suit, Keith Dunstan consults Melbourne fashion designer Mike Treloar, who reports that ties are dying except for enormous Oscar Wilde bow ties, open-necked shirts are the new look and chest hair is the status symbol, while Italian tie-maker Ottavio Ermini arrives to argue that the tie is the key of a man's personality and the Japanese will pay anything for a Gallieni pure silk spinnaker.

AFTER ATTENDING the opening of the Sydney Opera House I am very disturbed that I am not keeping pace with men's fashions. More than ever it is necessary for politicians to look avant garde and young, so they are setting the pace.

Mr Jeff Bate, for example, was wearing the latest in safari suits. You know the type, buttons on all the pockets, buttons all over the place. I always take note of him because he was the first to re-introduce mutton chop whiskers and our worst fears came true.

Mr Don Dunstan, premier of South Australia, was there in an all-white suit, dark blue shirt and white tie. At night he wore a white turtle-neck evening shirt with a black velvet jacket. Mr Al Grassby was in a vivid royal blue suit in patterned velvet and with this he wore large black velvet bow tie and a red striped shirt.

Most interesting of all though was Mr Lynch, deputy leader of the Opposition. His evening wear consisted of a red shirt. Think of the reddest shirt you ever saw and double its redness. He matched this with a large black velvet bow tie and a black velvet jacket.

But it was no ordinary jacket. It had cords and loops across the front like a uniform of the Queen's Hussars.

Clearly if one was to keep pace with Mr Lynch, Mr Bate and Mr Grassby it was important to study what was going on, so I called on Mike Treloar, the brilliant young Melbourne fashion designer.

Mike was wearing an open-necked shirt. His lapels were cinemascope in dimension and you could have made another suit out of the turn-ups on his baggies.

During the coming season, said he, it would all be nostalgia for the thirties. The movie version of F. Scott Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby was about to hit us and this would make it stronger than ever.

So Mike has a complete range of suits in stripes and vintage bookmaker's checks and he has gone heavily for Lady Chatterley's gamekeeper lover's checks with cloth caps. And cardigans — yes, dear old woollen button-up cardigans. If you don't like those there are tennis sweaters. They are like the sweaters the very nice schools used to hand out if you made the first XI.

As for ties, Mike doesn't wear one. This was very interesting to me. I have always been infuriated by ties. They are useless objects. At least belts and braces have a sensible job of work to do.

'Gee, I think I only possess one tie,' he said. 'I keep it for funerals. Look, ties are going right out. If you go around the agencies in town, fellers just aren't wearing them any more.'

He reported that the only ties that are 'in' are bow ties. It's not the bow tie you had for the Melbourne centenary in 1934. They're crafty these people. Your bow tie now has to be immense — Oscar Wilde large and sloppy.

Well, if you don't wear a tie, can you wear a skivvy? Mike shuddered. He hasn't worn a skivvy for three years. No, you wear open-necked shirts.

I looked at the other gentlemen in his fashion house and they were all in open-necked shirts, showing off the hair on their chests. Clearly in 1974 it will be important for hair on a chest to be seen.

'Yair, they're much more sexy than a tie,' he continued. 'It's got to the stage with me now that when I see a man with a tie on I think he looks odd.'

So something else is coming back. Remember the old open-necked sports shirt? It had a soft pyjama collar. It also had a tag loop, which if you were desperate you could hook over a button on the other side. It was nigh impossible to put a tie around that collar.

Well then, all those are coming back except that they are in colours 40 times more brilliant. However, I am confused. Last week one of the world's great tie experts, Ottavio Ermini, export manager of R. V. Gallieni of Milan and Como arrived in town.

He was shocked when I gave him Mike Treloar's news. He said: 'The tie is not necessary as a garment, yes. But it is necessary to you as a man. It is the key of his personality. It is what stops your suit from being a dull ordinary thing.'

Oh, Mr Ermini was very moved. He said that his firm under Gallieni actually invented the shape of the tie back in 1889, and in many parts of the world ties were known as Gallienis.

Sales, he said, were splendid throughout Asia, particularly in Singapore, Hong Kong and Japan. Asians had a fierce desire to be Westernised and the tie was the Western symbol. The Japanese would pay anything for a tie, and a Gallieni tie in pure silk, large as a spinnaker, costs between $12 and $14.

So that's very interesting: in Asia the Japanese are wearing ties to be Western civilised; in Australia the young are dispensing with the tie to be proletariat uncivilised.

And this is why the formal institutions, the banks, the insurance houses, the big old firms still insist on their staff wearing ties.

They have the idea that all the revolutions in history have been caused by chaps who don't wear ties.
