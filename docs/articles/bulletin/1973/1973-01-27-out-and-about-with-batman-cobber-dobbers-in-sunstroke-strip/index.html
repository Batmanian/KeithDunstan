Cobber dobbers in Sunstroke Strip

5 min read.

First published 27 Jan 1973

Melbourne's second water crisis in five years has produced a new community phenomenon, the neighbour who rats on illegal waterers, garden bore-sinking at $1200 a pop, and a booming trade in plastic buckets and rubber bath-water diverters, prompting Keith Dunstan to suggest the city rebrand as Sunstroke Strip.

MELBOURNE was always a little short on 'world's bests' but my parents always used to tell me with pride that (a) we had the world's best water supply, unequalled anywhere for its limpid purity and quantity, (b) that Flinders Street railway station was the world's busiest, (c) Chloe was the world's most artistic and unique hotel attraction, and (d) Phar Lap stuffed up at the museum was the world's best racehorse.

Flinders Street railway station has deteriorated to an ugly horror that has to be pulled down and no longer is there any evidence that it is the world's busiest station. Stations in Tokyo are far more thrillingly congested. Young and Jackson's through sheer beer gallonage used to be the champion hotel in Melbourne. Now it is not even in the first 10.

I am convinced that Chloe no longer pulls her weight. She wouldn't turn on one beer drinker in a thousand. She is there still, standing starkers, rather oddly, in a stone grotto. But in this permissive society she needs to do something more. Walk out of the saloon bar, turn right and you can buy 50 how-to-do-it books with love play in 27 positions.

And Phar Lap? I don't think the younger generation understands or grieves any more for that great animal.

But the pride-killing shock is the water shortage. For the second time in five years we have the agonising experience of the city drying up like an old biscuit. The situation is such that I am allowed to hose between 7pm and 9pm on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays. I live on the even-numbered side of the street. The odd ones over the road can hose on Tuesdays, Thursdays and Saturdays.

When they're hosing I am allowed to bucket. 'To bucket' is a verb in strong use here. You can define it easily: 'to bucket, to be about to bucket, to have just been bucketing.'

I use two. One is under the tap being filled while I am emptying the other on the plants. If I am at the end of the garden I have to sprint like hell to get back before the other bucket is filled.

Six hours of bucketing a week and you get as fit as Herb Elliott.

But I have received advice on how to improve this technique. You use a long plastic pipe or hose and fit a small tap on the end. This way you extend your plumbing system to the buckets on site, so to speak, and you turn your tap on and off without breaking the law.

But you have to be careful what you do because the Board of Works has actually asked people to dob in their neighbors for sly watering. So we have created an entirely new style of community with the neighbor who snoops over the fence trying to catch the wrong-doer. The Board of Works has stated that there is no need to give your name when you dob in your neighbor. So now there are several hundred prosecutions pending, mostly as a result of cobber dobbers.

There are various methods around this. A rich character up the street has put in a bore. It cost him $450 by the time the drilling was done and he installed a pump and all. His garden looks extraordinary, a little piece of green Old Ireland surrounded by Simpson's Desert. His sprinklers go day and night, so he had to put out a sign: 'This house uses bore water.' It's the only way to save him from cobber dobbers.

Of course, the bore people are flat out all over — racecourses and golf courses. Huntingdale has had to relinquish the state open golf tournament and Moonee Valley is under threat of cancelling race meetings. You must realise that things are immensely grave when Melbourne is talking of cancelling race meetings.

But, as I say, bores vary in price and if you have to go down 200ft, the price is $1200 or more.

The city nurseries are suffering their worst depression ever, but the hardware stores are having a lovely time. They are selling plastic buckets and watering cans by the millions. A genius out at Doncaster is making nice money out of fibreglass lily ponds. People are buying them as storage units for their used bath water.

The stores are also selling rubber gadgets for diverting water out of the kitchen sink. What's more you can buy 60-foot hoses with a timer more sophisticated than anything available from our gardening writer on the ultimate effect of Zip, Wham, Wow, Gob and all the other detergents on rhododendrons.

But curiously enough Melbourne has failed to make use of its assets in this matter. For the past century Melbourne weather has been a national joke. You know the sort of stuff: 'The one place where you need sunglasses, cough lozenges, a raincoat and sunburn cream all on the one day.' The truth is our annual rainfall is only 25.79 inches. Brisbane gets 44.81, Sydney 47.54, Perth 34.01 and on the mainland Adelaide is the only state capital that gets less than we do with 20.81.

The Victorian Government Tourist Bureau should capitalise on this. It could call St Kilda, Elwood, Beaumaris, Mordialloc 'sunstroke strip.' It could put posters in railway stations and airports offering such slogans as:

'Come to Melbourne and forget your umbrella.'

'Melbourne — City of the Golden Sun.'

'Waterlogged, miserable, mildewy? Find relief this summer in sun-soaked Melbourne.'
