Ear-bashers' annual orgy

4 min read.

First published 15 Dec 1973

Melbourne's school speech nights, unchanged in pattern since Keith Dunstan first attended 45 years ago, follow an invariable formula of Gilbert and Sullivan, the headmaster's report on school finances, a visiting dignitary who has been booked as guest speaker at every school at the same time, and speeches so excruciating that one is left wondering why there have been campaigns for the abolition of examinations but none for the abolition of school speech nights.

WE ARE DEEP into the school speech night season. They have been taking place right across the nation. Town halls, shire halls, municipal halls have been booked solid throughout. There has been much talk lately about the abolition of examinations, but who has suggested the abolition of school speech nights? Nobody.

It is interesting how little they have varied with time. The pattern invariably works this way. They start with the entertainment. The first form sings madrigals, second form, Gilbert and Sullivan. Gilbert and Sullivan is the rage right across the nation this year. Third form might have a touch of the Road to Mandalay. In my time, I think, it was compulsory by order of the Education Department, like God Save the Queen.

The headmaster or mistress gives the report. The chairman of the school board gives his message, a tale about the suffering and approaching death rattle of the school finances — 'We will have to pull in our belts.' I mean his horror has had to express his horror over the school's classification. I mean his horror has had to express itself in magnificent prose — being classified A and at the same time financially embarrassed on this scale, it is a sign that some messiah is about to descend from the virtue of the institution in which they are working.

Over the past year, the most long-suffering parents reported this year that his children were spread over several schools so he had to endure several speech nights. The same fellow was booked as guest speaker for them all.

After, the visiting dignitary's speech there is the presentation of the awards. That takes several of these occasions carefully. Many given of speech night addresses it was music that old papers and copy them out word for word.

But how do you vary speech nights? One could check at school functions and the number of original things going on at so speech nights is minimal.

What is the answer? Does it always have to be so stiff? Why not a musical turn? Can't we get the headmaster to get up? Or a barbershop quartet from the fourth form? And speeches. Couldn't some brave headmaster try a brief, 10-minute speech and refuse to speak for more than that? One year the headmaster of Geelong Grammar circulated his speech on a little 45 rpm disc and it was such a good speech you couldn't resist playing it over and over again.

Of course, the speeches are the most excruciating part. I have been in agony where all the speeches have been patiently stored so that parents and children can take them and keep them forever as mementos. Making it an ideal, ready-made souvenir subject. But the particular elements that Smart uses are not drawn so much from well-worn stock-in-trade. They are newly minted ones, brilliant red container trucks, gaily striped street furniture and signs, bus terminals and endless multi-storeyed housing blocks, de-humanised machines for living in.

Of course, you can go to sleep, but you must do it very carefully. Well, also I feel there is much loss of face in doing this. So you have to suffer ... now I personally would suggest that at all ages the school leaves. You are on the threshold of life. The world says go and go into the world.

Over the past 45 years I have attended at least 30 speech nights, and I look upon myself as a veteran. The great man was the war veteran who can speak of his experiences on the Kokoda Trail, or the great Liberian campaign. I genuinely suggest that there should be campaign medals for those who have endured these hard speech nights. Do you remember that the RAAF used to give chevrons on the arm for years of service? I think you should be able to wear a red chevron for each five speech nights.

Admittedly back in the old days much more noise was taken of these speech nights. If you search the newspapers of the eighties and the nineties you will find that the speeches were reported in full, column after column. Oh, they dealt with the same things; mostly they dealt with the lack of finance for education, the poor quality of the school buildings, the decline in Christian spirit and the loose moral fibre of the younger generation. Indeed, I have a theory that many givers of speech night addresses have taken very old papers and copy them out word for word.
