The big statue wrap-up

5 min read.

First published 10 Mar 1973

Inspired by Max Beerbohm's idea of covering London's statues rather than unveiling new ones, Keith Dunstan proposes a programme of veiling ceremonies for Sydney's pompous colonial bronzes, beginning with the dumpy Queen Victoria near the Supreme Court and moving on to Captain Cook, Captain Arthur Phillip and the weather-checking King Edward VII.

OF COURSE Sydney suffers from appalling statues. I wonder if I can help. Max Beerbohm used to be a crusader against London's statues. He believed no man or woman deserved to be immortalised in bronze or stone. Something horrid happened in the freezing of human features; either they became absurd or they acquired a look of awful pomposity.

As a young man he was militant. He wanted to rid the city of the blight by having the statues, in various appropriate cases, melted or crushed. But over the years the indomitable Max mellowed, for after all, he was a gentle man. He wondered whether it was right to do violence to effigies of the dead.

Suddenly he discovered a solution. The year must have been 1910; he was visiting a fishing village near Genoa and he noticed a statue, all covered, waiting for an unveiling. On the pedestal was the sign 'Umberto II.' He had never been to an unveiling ceremony so he thought he would wait. Ah, but this statue of King Umberto already had been veiled for a year. It turned out that the city authorities erected the statue on the waterfront without permission of the fishermen. The land had been granted in perpetuity to them and they didn't want it. They remembered that he had been kind to children, but no one could think of anything else he had done. So the fishermen hired a lawyer and took the matter to the council. They had no objection to the statue so long as it remained veiled.

So Beerbohm looked at this statue and thought it looked rather charming veiled and threw a very pleasant shadow. He could even imagine his own vision of Umberto or anything else he liked.

Eureka. This was the solution. Why not cover up all London's statues? Instead of unveiling ceremonies they could have veiling ceremonies. At the rate of four or five veilings a week they would go through the lot in a year. The prime minister could take his choice of the various statesmen, poets and philosophers he wished, and give the oration at these veilings. Others he could pass on to lesser ministers. Oh, it would be enormously interesting.

Don't you think this would be a good idea for Sydney? You would be hard put to find too many poets or philosophers. Sydney statuary is tremendously light on those. Robbie Burns is up there near the Art Gallery of New South Wales — he is the standard cliché in almost every town. Australian poets and writers never get the breaks.

The Sydney statues almost entirely are divided between royalty, politicians and explorers; in that order. In every case the object has been to impress upon the obviously wayward populace the immense power of authority. Even chaps like Matthew Flinders, who were getting around in the tiniest of boats, are dressed like Admirals of the Fleet.

For our first public veiling I think we should go for the Statue of Queen Victoria up there near the Supreme Court. The Governor, Lord Carrington, unveiled it in 1888 to celebrate the 50th year of Her Majesty's reign. For awe-inspiring her populace she is carrying all possible weapons — orb, sceptre, crown, sash, medals. The Bulletin was upset even then. 'Victoria was a short, fat, dumpy, squat, obese, melancholy old woman.' And here they create a statue of a young girl.

Over in Hyde Park we have the enormous statue of Captain Cook. The veiling here would be an ambitious and exciting project. Remember the American sculptor who came here several years ago. He wrapped up great chunks of stone and at one stage he even wrapped up a section of the Sydney coastline. I am sure he would consider Captain Cook a marvellous challenge.

Cook has a telescope in one hand and the other he is holding out to see whether it is raining. I find it very interesting the remarkable number of statues in Sydney that do make this gesture.

That awful statue of Sir John Robertson in the park between the Mitchell library and art gallery is the same. Sir John with his enormous beard and badly fitting suit, has his hand out to check on the weather, obviously a very necessary thing to do around here.

I think the statue of Captain Arthur Phillip in the Botanic Gardens can out-pompous them all. He is as big as Cook and at his feet he has a series of massive characters, male and female, all built like Carl Ditterich. One very big girl has a single breast exposed.

In Macquarie Street we have King Edward VII, imperious, dressed like a general in the Guards, feathers, medals, thigh boots, nose in the air, looking longingly up the road to what undoubtedly would have been his greatest love in this town, perhaps the poker machines and the girls of Kings Cross. Indeed there are two large girls emblazoned on the front of his pedestal.

Why not get down to the job this autumn? We could divide the ceremonies between the governor, the premier and the minister for culture. The governor, of course, would be ideal for orations on royalty, the premier for politicians and the culture minister for poets, philosophers and explorers. 'I now declare this statue veiled' and lo, a nylon cord would pull the sculptor designed covering together.
