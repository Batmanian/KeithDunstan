Too late into nostalgia

6 min read.

First published 19 May 1973

Keith Dunstan's five-year-old business plan for Batman Atmosphere Laboratories, to mass-produce antique-look decor items from Greek columns to plastic cast-iron lacework, has been comprehensively beaten to market, with firms already selling machine-made antique bricks, fibreglass castles and miniature Waterloo cannons at $500 apiece.

FIVE YEARS ago I had a brilliant idea. I would start a business to be known as Batman Atmosphere Laboratories Pty Ltd, or even better, Batman Applied Nostalgia Inc. We would purchase 12 acres of factory land near Dandenong, delicious plastic cast-iron lace front and Buckingham Palace-style iron gates.

For our first production line, according to my prospectus, we thought we would produce Greek atmosphere — Ionic and Corinthian columns, lashings of statues, particularly those girls depicting 'Hope,' 'Modesty,' and 'Spring.' Our busiest line would be 'Marriage of Venus,' a very big topless girl, clutching a piece of cloth over her crutch, the other hand held somewhat hopefully as if she were waiting for Aristotle Onassis to turn up. We would make fountains, cupids, angels and perfect reproductions of Michelangelo's 'David' to the very best standards of the Victorian Chief Secretary's Department.

For our Edwardiana department we planned coach lamps, big cast-iron lamps for the garden exactly like the late departed lamps from St Kilda Road, and for plastic cast-iron lacework we felt we could produce 1200 yards a day (in 15 fabulous non-rust colours). Oh, yes we would have kerosene lamps, with beflowered glass shades, marble fireplaces made out of fibreglass and no end of antique telephones would be there, especially the gold French style.

For chi-chi boutiques we would turn out antique cash registers, brass scales, and wire string holders to hang from the ceilings. For discotheques we would be flat out producing the more reprehensible Aubrey Beardsley prints and for the Edwardiana Victoriana restaurant trade we would be awfully busy. There would have to be a whole department flat out making superbly correct, terribly bad 19th century oils of fat Pre-Raphaelite maidens, bosomy country girls with milk-white skin, arms around red setter dogs and such. I would want lots of large oak frames, ancient pictures of the February 1892 meeting of the international order of Rechabites and the 1887 champion Carlton brewery tug-o-war team. They would also be flat out making restaurant tables and chairs out of imitation railway sleepers, plus imitation antique shearing shed sheep-dung floors for partitions and ceilings. The best artists in the country could make my farmer's hams and bacon and Abbot's Lager antique advertising mirrors. What else? The nautical trade of course — rope nets, brass port and starboard lights, compasses, davits and lifeboats and we would turn out Japanese glass balls in nets like beer bottles.

Oh, I was too slow off the mark. My chance to become a millionaire has been dashed. All the aforementioned items are being done. Scout's honor there is a firm which is making antique machine-made hand-made bricks with the actual crumbly look complete with imitation black soot marks. There is a firm which is making plastic cast-iron lace for balconies, lighter and better than the real thing. At Garden Week in the Exhibition Building this year you could purchase a range of fountains, ready-made Japanese stone bridges made out of cement and Japanese antique stone lanterns made yesterday are no problem at all.

Oh yes, and particularly fascinating is the news that a Seaford builder, Mr Roger Simpson, has started building pre-fabricated castles. He builds them out of large fibreglass modules, bolted together at a cost of $1000 a square. Therefore his standard 16-square castle costs $16,000, but if you wish you can keep on adding modules until you have your own William Randolph Hearst menage. The surface of the fibreglass both outside and inside is simulated bluestone. Every mark and pore of the bluestone is reproduced so that even close up it is almost impossible to tell the real thing, says Mr Simpson. The castles have an enclosed courtyard, battlements, and an eight-foot Norman tower on the roof, slit windows and a massive front door which looks as if it would lower down like a drawbridge. A fibreglass moat can be added at an extra charge. Inside there are massive pine beams, sand-blasted to give that 14th century look. I was a little disappointed to hear that Mr Simpson was installing wall-to-wall carpet and electric lighting in his first castle, but it is not compulsory.

But things have gone even further, the nostalgia trade has taken on very high tone. Have you seen the advertisements from the Limited Historical Editions Company? Currently they are offering scale models of the nine-pounder field pieces used by the Duke of Wellington at the Battle of Waterloo. Wellington had only 156 guns so only 156 are being offered. The replicas are only 15 inches long with a wheel diameter of six inches but 'each gun' we are told, 'is individually made and the barrels proofed and test-fired by the worshipful company of gunmakers in the city of London which proofed actual barrels used in the battle against Napoleon. An individual battle certificate of authenticity, together with the proof certificate, is issued to each subscriber, and signed by Major-General B. P. Hughes, CBE, Royal Artillery Headquarters, Woolwich, London. The guns and limber are mounted on mahogany display bases with brass inscribed plaques bearing the owner's name and the number of the gun.' The price, landed in Australia, is $500 a gun.

Your correspondent telephoned the Limited Editions people and expressed scepticism over the number likely to be sold. 'Oh, no,' said the lady there, 'they are going very well, particularly to retired majors and regimental associations. There were only 156 for the whole world and this time Australia received the first offer.'

Also on offer are the Queen's Beasts' teaspoons in honour of the silver wedding of Her Majesty and HRH Prince Philip. You get a set of 10 teaspoons with a series of heraldic beasts on the handle in a leather bound case embossed with the royal cyphers in gold. Price $480 a set. There are 2000 sets of those for the whole world.

So we are beaten again. What's left? All I can think of is, say, a limited set of Ned Kelly's armor, made out of vintage ploughshares at Wangaratta (I stand to be corrected by historians) exactly as done by Messrs Kelly. I would make only 50 sets. Each one would be stamped and signed by the Victorian chief commissioner of police and they would come on a stringy bark, silver mounted coathanger. Price $2000 a suit.
