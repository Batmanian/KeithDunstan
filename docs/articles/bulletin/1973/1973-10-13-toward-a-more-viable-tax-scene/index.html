Toward a more viable tax scene

5 min read.

First published 13 Oct 1973

Treasurer Crean's inflation problem could be solved by a cliche tax ranging from $50 for the return of "churching the old whore" to 25 cents for each "in fact," since Keith Dunstan's monitoring of Parliament and TV current affairs reveals an epidemic of "viable," "cogent," "in this area," "gut feeling" and the coming word "elan," with Senator Gair's "fleas on an old dog" worth another $50.

TREASURER CREAN clearly is in terrible trouble with the economy. Raising the interest rates is a weak palliative in checking inflation. He needs new and ingenious methods of taxation and it just so happens that your correspondent has the answer.

I suffer a great deal listening to Parliament and I have a habit-forming disease which I have been unable to break — I watch TV current affairs programs. As a result my poor tired brain throbs with political cliches and 'in' words. They come back and back until it drives one almost to the point of hysteria. I would tax these and the rate would range from $50 down to 25c a time. The scheme would be easy to police through Hansard and TV tapes.

You may be surprised at that $50 figure, but some politicians by the re-introduction of dead old cliches can provide a frightening new pollution over a long period. I would fine Mr Snedden for the return of 'churching the old whore' and Senator Gair another $50 for his 'fleas on an old dog.'

You don't have a thought or a belief any more, you have 'a gut feeling' ($2). You don't talk, you 'communicate' or 'conduct dialogue' ($1). 'Now' has disappeared right out of the language. We have 'at this point in time' or even better 'at this definite point in time,' a bad one, $5. Our best revenue producer will be 'in fact' and 'in point of fact,' 25c. That may sound a miserable fine but bear in mind that I have heard Mr Bob Hawke 'in fact' nine times in one program.

Things are no longer possible, they are 'viable' or 'non viable,' 50c. And 'cogent' now is making a brilliant run, $1.

The above I have merely given as examples. I will leave pricing to a 'select sub-committee.' However, I will now give my full cliche list which I hope will form the basis for the sub-committee's submission to the Treasury.

'Abrasive' is very strong at the moment. You will have noticed that members tend to have abrasive arguments. 'In this situation' and 'in this area' are dearly loved. For example, you don't merely talk about traffic. It is now important to say 'in this area of the traffic situation.' This is true. This is for real,' or a very good phrase which has almost mystic overtones as if you have discovered the ultimate solution, 'This is what it's all about.'

Whenever you are talking about big companies they are 'multi-nationals' or 'conglomerates.' You have 'in depth reports' and you have 'in depth interviews.'

You must keep up with lines like 'defence capability,' which has been used for almost anything — 'defence capability, dollar capability, wheat capability.' Learning to live with oneself. 'Theatre of war' is a forgotten cliche. Vietnam 'situation' is not a 'conventional war environment.'

'Scene' has come a long way in the past few years, particularly with the young. Almost any situation can be referred to as a scene. If you are a young member you can refer to your electorate as your 'scene.' A big fine here is in order.

'Pragmatic' is strong, 'predilection' is doing well and 'cathartic' never had such a good year. 'Charisma' is getting tired. Oh, how they have searched for that. Now politicians are leaning more to 'panache,' but the coming word, you watch for it, is 'elan.'

Another coming phrase is 'main thrust.' It has had a wonderful run just in the past few weeks, and even the political journalists have picked it up. It is used mostly in the 'main thrust of my argument.'

But then have you noticed 'this is?' I am sure that 'this is' could make almost as much money for the Treasury as 'in fact' and 'point of time.' It comes with a number of variations — 'This is right,' 'This is true, This is for real,' or a very good phrase which has almost mystic overtones as if you have discovered the ultimate solution, 'This is what it's all about.'

Here's a solid 50-cents-a-time list which you are unlikely to avoid this week: Not to worry. The deciding factor. Learning to live with oneself. At the other end of the spectrum. You tell me. Profitability. Finding new horizons. On the top level basis. Opted out. The cultural revolution. Spoiling the image. In the final analysis. Part of the establishment. Change the social relationship of the nuclear family. On each end of the spectrum. No basic guide lines. Guide lines index. It's as simple as that. Work it out for yourself. Right across the board. Consumer liquidity.

What else? 'Ethos' is going well with the young intellectual members. I also hear that they are looking for a leader with 'soul.' 'Soul' has hit the top in Britain and it is not easy to define. Bob Dylan would have 'soul,' whereas Frank Sinatra would not. Sydney Poitier would be a soul man, Dean Martin certainly not. J. F. Kennedy, yes. Mr Heath, no.

Our list will have to be revised almost every week. You find them everywhere. I even noticed that an art critic referred to a painter's work as an 'oeuvre.' 'Oeuvre' could well find its way into Hansard.
