We're all so delighted, obviously

5 min read.

First published 09 Jun 1973

Princess Anne's engagement to Lieutenant Mark Phillips revives Keith Dunstan's memories of covering Princess Margaret's engagement in 1960, when every newspaper deployed the word "obviously" to imagine royal feelings, Collins Street jewellers had replica engagement rings on display within days, and correspondents speculated the honeymoon must obviously be in Australia.

MY HEART was warmed by the news that Princess Anne was engaged to Lieutenant Mark Phillips, of the Queen's Dragoon Guards. You see this ensures another flow of royal tours, and being very nearly the most royal-toured reporter in Australia this is important. I think I was the first to talk about the Queen's radiant smile, and then as the years moved on it was the warmth of her maturity, the extraordinary purity of her lovely English complexion, the sapphire blue of her eyes, how no photograph ever did her justice. Oh, there was nothing I didn't stoop to. I even talked of the warm, human wit of Prince Philip. No, that's not true, I never stooped to that.

But I do claim to be the originator of 'obviously.' That's the trouble with royal tours — they lay down the law that the royal person must never be quoted directly; nor, except on rare occasions, can one get close to the royal, so one has to imagine their feelings, so I invented 'obviously.' The Queen was obviously pleased to receive a bouquet of flowers from four-year-old Barbara Binks, or the Queen was obviously thrilled at the breath-taking beauty of the utter aridity of Simpson's Desert. Then there was the time she was obviously shocked at the near nudity of some bikini girls in the Moomba parade. That was a strident 'obviously.'

So it will all be on again — new cast, new radiance, new obviouslys. I was very interested to read the formal announcement: 'It is with the greatest pleasure that the Queen and the Duke of Edinburgh announce the betrothal of their beloved daughter...' Were they really pleased? Did they really have the greatest pleasure? Well, it is the utterly standard formula. I checked back to February 27, 1960, to Princess Margaret's engagement and there we had: 'It is with the greatest pleasure that Queen Elizabeth, the Queen Mother, announces the betrothal of her beloved daughter, the Princess Margaret to Mr Antony Armstrong-Jones...'

Actually it is intriguing to go to that time. Margaret received a much better news cover than Anne. She got the entire front page of the Melbourne Herald and page two and page three, plus her life story which began with brilliant speed in the Saturday magazine the next day.

'All Britain gasped at the news,' we were told. 'Not a breath of rumor had appeared in the European or American press.'

A. J. P. Taylor in the 'Sunday Express' wrote: 'There can rarely have been a weekend when more hearts ran over with innocent happiness. Everyone in this country, in the Commonwealth, throughout the world rejoices at the news of Princess Margaret's engagement. I must confess that I never expected to find myself moved by the private affairs of the royal family. Yet it happened. Many other hardened hearts must be softened today. Already there is talk of giving Mr Armstrong-Jones a title. Yet what prouder title can he possess than he possesses at the present? He is a commoner, marrying a princess for love. This happens sometimes in the story books, rarely in real life. Now real life has done better than romance. Will the romance be enhanced if Mr Armstrong-Jones is made an earl, a duke, a prince?'

Then the syndicated life story of Princess Margaret, written by James Leasor, took off around Australia, just 24 hours after the announcement. The first paragraph began: 'At 22 minutes past nine on that stormy August evening almost 30 years ago, the thin, protesting cry of a newly born child rose above the rattle of rain on the ancient castle windows. Through this rain and under flickering spears of lightning, beacons blazed out the news from the hilltops round Glamis, in Scotland. Soon the whole wide sky was afire, announcing to the world the birth of a new princess.'

By heavens, the news spread had not abated. It was still enormous in the morning papers. By now we had switched to the biography of Armstrong-Jones. One article described him as 'slight, rather short — a few inches taller than his fiancee — and has fair hair and blue eyes ... he has few close friends, but those he has are loyal and absolutely discreet. He is gentle and quiet with unobtrusively good manners which fit easily into Balmoral. One of his closest friends describes him as the brightest young genius in England behind a lens or a dark room, but the most hesitant, diffident man who ever worked for a newspaper.'

A 'Sun' reporter rang Buckingham Palace, spoke to Commander Richard Colville and came back with the news, 'The princess is so obviously in love.' 'Obviously' was obviously moving well by 1960.

That same day, the reporters found Mrs Dorothy Bradford 'at her pleasant middle-class home in the Melbourne suburb of Bentleigh.' She was housekeeper to Antony Armstrong-Jones' grandparents, Colonel and Mrs Messel, for six years. Mrs Dorothy Bradford said: 'I feel just as if my own son has become engaged to Princess Margaret.'

By Monday evening we were speculating on the likely venue for the honeymoon. Obviously it had to be Canada or Australia. Canberra correspondents wrote that the Commonwealth would be delighted if the princess chose Australia and Mr Menzies was certain to issue a formal invitation.

But perhaps the most practical royal fervor was caused by a Collins Street jeweller. He had the jewellery scoop of the year. By March 1, and that was Tuesday, he had a replica of Princess Margaret's engagement ring on display — a ruby flower surrounded by diamond petals. Price, $2000.

Came the wedding day on May 6, supplement. So there's the challenge: everyone will have to do the same for Anne and I'll take bets, 6 to 4 on, that if she comes here for her honeymoon I'll be waiting on horseback.
