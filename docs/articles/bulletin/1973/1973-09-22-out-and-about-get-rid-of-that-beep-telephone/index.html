Get rid of that (beep) telephone

5 min read.

First published 22 Sep 1973

The PMG's new 50-mile range paging device prompts Keith Dunstan to declare a pox on the telephone and report with admiration on Gordon De'Lisle, the Melbourne businessman who retired to Buderim, Queensland and refused to have a telephone installed even when the PMG sent in a team and his refusal caused them to be stunned beyond belief.

AT THE MELBOURNE electronics exhibition last month they had on display the latest paging device. You know these beeping gadgets you carry in your shirt pocket? As soon as it starts yelping you go to the telephone and call the boss. Well, this one is backed by the PMG. They run the transmitting station, so doctors, engineers, plumbers, tree surgeons, bottle-ohs, anybody all over town can be subscribers. The range is something like 50 miles. You can get your man on the golf course, down a mine, up a tree, in the pub, anywhere.

Eventually they expect to have these transmitting stations all over Australia, so even if you are boiling a billy 100 miles north of Alice Springs, big brother can get you. So far the PMG is sticking to beeping, no talk-back devices. But they have these in the U.S. and there the list of subscribers runs to millions. A husband can be put on talk-back, and I understand they are particularly popular with mothers for their daughters. Say the little loved one is still out at 2 a.m., mother can order: 'You bloody well come home now or father is coming out with the 12-gauge shotgun.'

A pox on the telephone I say. It is the most loathsome invention ever inflicted on mankind, a totally mannerless, insidious thing which demands attention immediately whether you are eating your dinner, in the bath or sitting on the throne. I have instructed my family not to answer, but they never have the guts to go through with it. 'It might be something important,' they say. Anyway what hope do you have when you possess three daughters.

I have witnessed a rising movement against the jet aeroplane and at last there is a rising anger against the god motor car, but nobody has started an anti-telephone movement. At least not until a month ago and it all started in the advertising columns of The Bulletin. I am surprised that it didn't receive more notice. There was a full single column advertisement for an 'unusually fine painting by Sir Arthur Streeton.' The present owner wished to dispose of it and prospective buyers were asked to contact the De'Lisle Gallery, Panorama Crescent, Buderim, Queensland. They were to do this either by calling personally or writing a letter.

And this was the punchline: 'We regret the inconvenience, but we do not have a telephone.'

Doesn't care to have a telephone. Gordon De'Lisle? I remembered that name. Gordon De'Lisle, a man of courage. He used to have a tremendous showbiz business in Melbourne. One time he personally took on the Taxation Department and accused them of bleeding him white. When the New Arts Centre was built he described it in the Australian alternatively as an obscure grey telephone exchange and a bleak penitentiary hovering above its scungy moat. Well, after some heart warnings, he announced to all he was leaving this wretched Melburnian rat race and he'd go lotus eating in Queensland.

I regret to have to inform you I sent him an urgent telegram and asked him to telephone me, which he did. Mr De'Lisle explained all. He went to Queensland and settled for Buderim with a marvellous view 600 feet over the Pacific. He could lie round in the nude in the sun all day.

At first it wasn't easy. People in sunny Queensland just don't build things for you in a hurry. So it took him about six months to get his house with his photographic studio down below. But the telephone wasn't necessary. He did just as well without it and soon his business was almost back to what it was in Melbourne ... without all the panic. The next step was to open an art gallery so life had never been better.

'Well, when we came here my wife, Cynthia, made it the number one rule. No telephone, no matter what. Oh it didn't go down well at all. You'd be surprised how angry people became, particularly advertising people. They were furious. How dare he not have a telephone ... it was an insult against modern civilisation.'

'If people really want to do business they come to see me. We sit down, open a bottle of riesling and take it quietly. Back in the old days I had four telephones on the desk. Tension-builders I called them. One was for outgoing calls, two were for the two lines for in-coming calls and the fourth was for harassing my staff. It's amazing how abrasive most of the calls I received were.'

Warming to his subject Mr De'Lisle continued, 'Do you know one time we were practically forced to have a telephone. Mr McMahon was prime minister and his personal adviser Don Dobie MHR had a place very similar to ours with a Skylight right here in Panorama Crescent. The PM apparently wanted to speak to Mr Dobie urgently. Do you know what happened? A PMG telephone putting-on team just marched right in here. We pushed them back. Told them we wouldn't have it. They were stunned beyond belief that anyone actually wouldn't have a telephone. They still insisted. It was only after I actually threatened them with violence that they went away.'
