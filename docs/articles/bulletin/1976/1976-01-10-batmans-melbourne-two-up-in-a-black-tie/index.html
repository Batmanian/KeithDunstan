Two Up in a black tie

5 min read.

First published 10 Jan 1976

A visit to the legal Two Up game at Wrest Point Casino in Hobart, where the spinner is in black tie, the ring is padded with royal blue carpet, and Keith Dunstan foul-tossed three times before slinking away from the national game in humiliation.

TASMANIA has always been ahead of us innocent Victorians in sin. They had betting shops half a century before us. They gave us Tattersall's. They have led us from the paths of righteousness with their casino and before Christmas they introduced new drinking laws.

Hotels can open their bars here now 24 hours a day Monday to Saturday and eight hours on Sunday. There were fearful cries from the churches. The Seventh Day Adventists thought it the certain way to ruin, but so far the hotels have not made much use of the facility. The casino keeps its bars open until 4 am and here and there a pub goes to a late hour. However, a genuine alcoholic needs a drink in the early morning and it is still very hard to find a pub that opens before 10 am.

Many are still not opening on Sundays, but one chain is giving us from noon to 8 pm. However, the serious purpose for my visit to Hobart was not to drink but to indulge in the sin of Two Up, which is conducted in polished style in the old wing of the Wrest Point building.

As I entered the area I noted some familiar sights, flickering electric candle lights and ash trays from our beloved old now defunct Regent Theatre. On the walls they had blow-ups of Two Up scenes from the old movie of The Sentimental Bloke. There were also World War I pictures of Diggers playing the game.

Apart from that all was infinitely plush. The ring looked like a fancy cocktail bar out of a Fifth Avenue hotel. It was padded with leather. There was green baize where you put down your chips and in the actual ring itself there was expensive royal blue carpet.

The boxer, ring boss, or whatever you call him, was elegant in black tie. Now this wasn't the way I pictured Two Up. I had hoped they would have reproduced the back of the Williamstown Racecourse, which could have been very nicely done with sheets of rusty galvanised iron. There would have been hard dirt on the floor and the seats naturally would have been empty. See kerosene tins. Jackie Howe singlets would have been in order and guests would have been asked to surrender their ties upon entering.

This is the one legal Two Up game in Australia but even so I would have liked to have seen a cockatoo on duty at the door. What's more, for old time's sake, the Tasmanian police should be encouraged to make an occasional raid.

Most enthusiasts, though, were older, real cases of cloudland revisited. One old boy, on a stick, RSL badge in button hole, came in to have a spin. His wife was there at the edge of the ring and she was furious. 'The silly old bugger's nearly blind,' she told everyone. 'Gawd, why doesn't he just give his money to me instead of throwing it away in there... Come on out Charlie,' she hollered, 'do you think you're back in the war?'

The crowd around the ring is pretty raucous. They shout a lot even using fine old sentimental bloke terms like, 'Come in Spinner.' They shouted 'Come on — give us heads Charlie.'

Charlie, shaking on his stick, actually tossed very nicely. Like riding roller skates it's not a skill that you forget.

The spinners are called in by turn. They go round the ring clockwise and each bettor, male or female, gets a chance. I must confess that with my deeply puritan Melburnian background I was startled by the vision of tender young ladies tossing up coins. It wouldn't have done at Steeny Isaacs.

My turn came. It was explained I had to back myself for at least $5. I was given a kip and a choice of two vintage English pennies. One had to go on the kip heads up, the other tails up. If the coins fell tails then I was out. But if I tossed three heads in a row I won at 7/2 to one.

I tossed the coins high.

'Foul,' cried the boxer. 'They didn't turn over.'

He picked them up, returned them and I tried again.

'Foul.' They still didn't turn over.

There was a murmur around the ring. 'Throw 'em mate, come on.' There's no greater sin than to hold up the flow of betting money.

The boxer was curt. 'Toss the coins high over your head and give a sharp turn of the wrist.'

I tried again. I concentrated so hard on wrist turning I didn't throw the coins high enough.

'FOUL,' he said for the third time. 'You're out. You can have the choice of getting someone to throw for you or take your money and try again next time round.'

I took my money and slunk away. There's no greater humiliation than being a failure at the national game.
