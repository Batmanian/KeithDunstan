A nose wheelie, a tail wheelie

5 min read.

First published 24 Jan 1976

American skateboard champion Stacy Peralta visits Melbourne for a demonstration session in a multi-storey car park, performing nose wheelies, tail wheelies and terrifying downhill slaloms that reveal a sport combining the grace of Nureyev with the daring of a dive bomber.

THERE MUST BE something in the American style or their education system, the American child is always so articulate.

Stacy Peralta, 18, junior skateboard champion of the U.S., arrived in our town. He wore a brilliant yellow T-shirt, with STACY on the front, shorts, gym shoes, and he carried his skateboard around like a wart. He and board were never parted.

Not that it was anything special. Stacy was a purist about that. He said that there were all sorts of crazy skateboards on the market. You could buy them with platforms of curious, of wondrous flexing materials. There was even a skateboard fitted with a hydraulic brake, prices up to $80 or more. Stacy stuck to the basic model, which sold all over America, a board that flexed only slightly, good quality urethane wheels. Price: $38.

Stacy began skateboarding about six years ago. He began with a plank of wood with wheels attached. He made it himself.

'You want to know how it got to me?' he said. 'I didn't go for the competitive bit. I didn't like team sports. You always had to be somewhere at some particular time. Then one of the team would be sick and it wouldn't work out right.

'But on a skateboard board you're your own person, right? You can do it anywhere on any piece of hard pavement. Whenever you are moved to action, yair, even in the middle of the night, you can get out that skateboard, do a few tricks. It's the greatest form of expression the young have ever had.

'The skateboards came on big in California around 1968. They died and now they've come back again, bigger than ever, a $25 million a year industry. The reason, I guess, is better design. Before they used to have clay wheels. Wooden, urethane has been the answer. You can do anything with those.'

Stacy has hair down to his shoulders. He looks about nine stone and he is a restless young man. You don't have to ask him to do tricks on his skateboard. He does them unconsciously like a sort of jitterbug, he goes backwards forwards. And when walking along he ran forward with the skateboard in his hands, put it on the footpath and did a handstand.

There was a skateboard film showing at the Total Theatre and all day Sunday the golden bread people sponsored an 'expression session.'

Now the Total Theatre has one of those awful, bleak, six-storey concrete car parks. It was filled on all levels with the young on screeching, howling wheels.

They had a downhill slalom which was the most awe inspiring ride I have ever seen. You know the angles of the ramps in these car parks? Well, they had witches' hats placed at appropriate intervals all the way down.

A slalom at Perisher, Thredbo or whatever was pansy stuff compared with this. Those kids went down like dive bombers threading the needle of the witches' hats, then when they came to the lower level they went down on their haunches, shrieked around in a big arc, then on down to the next level.

The manoeuvring was done by the hips forward and backward, undulating, and this is something I hadn't been told about. My God, they were beautiful. The style and the grace of the slim, young teenagers was pure Nureyev. One could watch them all day, prettier than skiers and even more spectacular than surfboard riders.

It was an inter-sex thing. There were 30 per cent females, and the girls were as good as the boys. There was one kid aged about four, wearing a crash helmet, and he wasn't bad either.

There were all sorts of events. For example, on the top flat deck there was a two minute expression event, two minutes for your best tricks.

Meanwhile Stacy was doing nose wheelies, tail wheelies. He could jump the bar. That is, race along, leap over a bar and come down on the board on the other side. He could do two-board wheelies. He would do a wheel nose wheelie on one board while doing a two wheel tail wheelie on the other.

He has one famous trick with a swimming pool. For this he needs a free form pool, and naturally he uses it when it's dry. He gets up high speed from the shallow end down to the deep end and then he goes right up on the rim of the pool like the wall-of-death motor cycle people, so that he's almost horizontal.

'People don't believe it,' said Stacy. 'That one scares even the photographers.'

The skateboard is now the biggest of all the crazes. It has beaten the hula hoop, the frisbees, and one wonders whether it might not get a permanent niche.
