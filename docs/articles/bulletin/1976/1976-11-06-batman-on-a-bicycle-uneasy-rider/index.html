Uneasy rider

9 min read.

First published 06 Nov 1976

Keith Dunstan recounts cycling 6700 kilometres across America as one of 4000 riders in Bikecentennial 76, navigating timber trucks in Oregon, dry Kentucky counties, the cruising ritual in Missouri small towns, and the Hoosier Pass at 3400 metres, guided by Sir Hubert Opperman's advice to keep the chamois well greased.

SIR HUBERT OPPERMAN gave us the best advice. He said, 'Look out for maniacs going down hills, eat little and often and keep your chamois well greased.'

Perhaps one shouldn't give indelicate information like this but it does give you a real insight on bicycle touring. If you plan to ride a bicycle for a considerable distance it is essential that you wear riding shorts, no underclothing with chamois leather in the crotch. Then, as Sir Hubert says, you need plenty of Vaseline.

The tender skin around one's behind and the thought that the bicycle saddle was probably designed by some sadist in Belsen were the greatest hazard in our 6700km ride across the U.S. It took maybe a month to harden up for that one. The second greatest hazard was knee-suffering. Toiling up some of the mountain passes I thought at times the knees would come apart like the hinges of an old door. It took five weeks to overcome that one.

The bicycle ride across America was one of the grand events turned on for the bicentenary. And, would you believe, they called it Bikecentennial 76. It was dreamed up by a dedicated bike nut named Dan Burden who believes that it is time flabby Americans got out of their polluting motor cars and had a good look at their own country. He thought maybe he would get 100 enthusiasts and in the end there were 4000, 2000 of whom pedalled from coast to coast.

Twenty-six of us went from Australia, and just getting to our starting point, Reedsport, Oregon, was an experience in itself. For an exercise like this it is pretty essential that you have your own and trusted bike so we took them with us in the plane.

As soon as you arrive in America with anything in a carton terror erupts. We weren't allowed to check them at the airport; we weren't allowed to check them at the terminal. The hotel wouldn't have them, even taxi drivers looked at them with fear. They looked at us as if we were potential terrorists.

Coming home it was even worse. By then we were sun tanned and emaciated. My wife and I really did look members of the PLO.

But from Reedsport on it was beautifully organised. We were mixed up and put into groups of 13 or 14 with a trained tour leader and deputy leader.

You could settle for the fast trip, across America in just over 50 days — 130km to 200km a day. Or you could take it more easily, 65km to 130km a day, across in 82 days. Seeing that we were carrying all our own gear plus sleeping bag the slow trip was plenty quick enough for us.

It's amazing how selective you become regarding luggage when you are carrying it by your own muscle power. I mean, it's marvellous to be able to say to your wife, 'Darling, you'll only be able to take one pair of shoes.'

The folk with the biggest loads were the Japanese. Their bicycles had every conceivable gadget, speedos, mileage gauges, turning lights. Then they had their cameras and their lenses plus big short wave transistor radios.

The biggest contingent was from Holland, 160, and very vain about their cycling skills, some of them. There was one group, called the Flying Dutchmen. They would do their stint of 160km or so, arrive at their destination, take all the gear off their bikes, then go for a sprint just so that they wouldn't get out of shape.

But I remember one Hollander with much affection, Annie Dadema. She was a little old lady with grey hair pulled back into a bun. She looked like she had just come in from some other world. She would work quietly alongside members of a group that seemed to have no idea she was there, until others would notice that this little old lady had just cycled over the mountains and climbed to heights of 3000 metres by her own pedal power.

A particularly interesting rider — age wasn't really an impediment on this exercise — there were sexagenarians and there were even some over 70. One was 70, indeed an old doctor named Clarence Pickard. He was retired and there was a shock of white hair that stood up like tarantula legs. He had the energy and the grin of a 25-year-old.

One day he discovered that this group was doing 250km to our 65km.

So instead of taking a bus he hopped on his bike, gear and all, and rode the 250km in 10 hours. He said later, 'It's a wee bit tiring when you're not sure of the way.'

You would have to describe the route as the scenic trip. We would have saved 1600km if we had gone straight across, but it was two years in planning and Dan Burden sees it as a permanent recreation path across America.

It starts in Oregon, winds its way through Idaho into Montana as far as Missoula, then heads down south through Wyoming, Yellowstone Park, along the ridge of the Rockies in Colorado, then at Pueblo it goes straight east through Kansas, Missouri, Kentucky and Virginia.

We went along back roads and avoided all cities over 30,000 people, so what we got was really a unique view of rural America. We found the people extraordinarily generous, kind with often an almost astounding, naive simplicity.

Take a typical rural town in, say, Colorado or Kansas. It is not over prosperous. Business is dominated by at least two enormous supermarkets. There are at least half a dozen empty shops. There are at least four really prosperous churches and the most expensive of them all is likely to be a Baptist. I saw so many good looking Baptist churches and so much peanut butter in the U.S. I wonder if Jimmy Carter can possibly fail to be the next President of the United States.

There is a bookshop, but almost inevitably it is a religious bookshop. I found it hard to buy a newspaper in many towns and darned near impossible to get a news magazine like Time or Newsweek. These towns were introspective, calm, and often beautiful, almost a world of their own.

As I cycled through I thought, 'These are the sort of towns that sent off kids to Vietnam. They couldn't have had the faintest idea what it was about and when the unfortunate boy came back in a coffin the shock must have been profound.'

The social centre was the Dairy Queen rather than any pub and the boredom for the young was similar to what we endure in Victorian country towns. Often on Friday and Saturday nights we witnessed that strange phenomenon, so beautifully described by Tom Wolfe... cruisin'.

I saw it first on a hot summer night in Marshfield, Missouri. My wife and I were doing nothing in particular when we noticed cars going round and round the city square. There was a girl at the wheel of a Mustang with big fat tyres and she had gone around for the sixth time.

'What the hell's happening here?' I said to our tour leader, Dave Canha.

'Aw, they're just cruisin',' he replied. He explained that this was a regular weekend thing, that went on all over America, a sort of mating ritual. You get out in your Mustang, Camaro, Toranado, Cutlass or whatever and you just cruise, waiting for something to happen. Maybe somebody will try to burn you off, maybe there's a lead to a party, maybe you get a good pickup, maybe you just cruise.

I noticed this night that some cars had two boys, some had two girls. Some had a CB radio and they barked out through loud speakers. Behind them all trailed a police car.

Yes, this rural America was different from what I expected, very pure, very moral. After we crossed the Mississippi we rarely saw a drinking saloon again. Indeed we cycled over 1600km without seeing a pub and that's hard when you're on a bike.

Local option and the churches are the powerful influence. Kentucky was the big shock. Yes, Kentucky, one of the whisky producing capitals of the world. In Kentucky there are 120 counties and 100 of them are dry. One of the reasons is that — I thought there must be a great story in this. Who would tell it to me? Ah, yes, go to see the local newspaper editor, he's sure to be a drunk. So in Hodgenville I called at the local paper and asked to see the editor.

It was a she.

Miss Phillips was tall and grey haired. As I asked questions about the licensing laws of Kentucky she began to look more and more severe until she said, 'Before we proceed with this interview I think there is something I should make perfectly clear. I have been a total abstainer for the past 30 years.'

Oh, there were hazards. The greatest of them all were the timber trucks of Oregon and the coal trucks of Kentucky. Can you imagine what it is like to be on a bicycle on a narrow road, when a vast timber truck, hauling 40 tonnes of logs is bearing down from behind. First there is the monster's elephantine bellow. Next there is the hissing of the air brakes, the sound of the monster's annoyance. You pull over as far as you can to the right, suddenly there is a towering cliff alongside, cutting out the light, and the rich smell of fresh timber mixed with diesel fumes.

This is accompanied by a huge air draught and two seconds later it has gone. You are left wobbling and shaking with fear.

We struck every kind of weather that can be supplied in a devious American summer. We rode two days in snow, went through thunderstorms in the Rockies when the entire heavens seemed to be celebrating the bicentennial. We had hail, we had a cyclone, we had heat waves with temperatures up to 40C and torrential rain and floods in Kentucky.

The highest we climbed was over the Hoosier Pass in Colorado at 3400 metres, but mercifully we were well equipped. Our bikes had three chain wheels on the front and a cluster of five sprockets on the back giving a combination of 15 gears. On the flat if you got into the low gear the pedals went round like a food mixer.

Am I making all this sound unpleasant? If anyone asks me I'll be out there doing it again tomorrow morning. One of the reasons is America itself. I thought I knew the U.S. very well indeed, yet I expected the countryside to be a progression of McDonalds, Colonel Sanders, Humburger, Konner billboards.

For the first 2400km we seemed rarely out of national parks, state forests, wilderness reserves. I thought it the most continuing beautiful country I had ever seen, and on a bicycle it was possible to see it, smell it, feel it and take time.

Actually I haven't been good for that much since. I still dream of the calm and the eternal quiet of those Rockies, the scent of Ponderosa pines and the urgent rushing rivers of Oregon and, my God, Kansas, the wheat and the corn, enormous Saharas of it going on for ever into the distance. Yes, the perpendicular hills of the Ozarks all come back to me and the locals with their funny accents.

Then there were the kids wanting us to come in and drink Seven Up and eat cookies. My mouth waters, too, when I think of the food... American fresh strawberry pie, hot cakes and maple syrup.

Yes, I'd do it again. Give me a chance.
