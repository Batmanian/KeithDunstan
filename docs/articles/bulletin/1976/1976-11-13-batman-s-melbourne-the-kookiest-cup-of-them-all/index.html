The kookiest Cup of them all

4 min read.

First published 13 Nov 1976

Melbourne Cup fashions have drifted from morning dress and earnest elegance into deliberate mockery, with protesters targeting Sir John Kerr in anti-formal gear and recycled denim tails, prompting concern that when a society begins to spurn the tribal rituals handed down over generations, it is a sign of decadence.

DON'T YOU AGREE that it's time the Sociology Department at La Trobe University made an expert study of behaviour at the Melbourne Cup? I know some odd things go on occasionally at Royal Ascot but do they compare with our Melbourne Cup, which has become the strangest, the most bizarre... dare one say... the kookiest event in the country?

Now your pioneer hasn't missed a Melbourne Cup for these past 20 years. It always used to have a noble solemnity about it. As befits our most important national ritual. The only thing Australians have ever genuinely worshipped has been the horse and Melbourne Cup day has been the ultimate religious day when we have crossed ourselves and gone down on our knees.

The men have respected this by putting on special gear for the equine high mass. They have and still do go to the Cup in morning dress complete with top hats.

Even as far back as the 1870s the social Press reported on the immense sums the women have spent on the right clothes for the Derby, the Cup and the Oaks, sufficient to keep some fashion houses in operation for the entire year.

Progressively over the years there has been a change which has developed into a gallop. The desire is to mock and make it the national giggle. For example, on Cup day the Herald, for its principal social picture, chose the Sisters Luxford — Jennifer and Anne. They wore no gowns. They came out in black and red corselettes with suspenders holding up long stockings and they looked like a Mme de Neuville. Everyone loved them.

This year and for the past three years there has been a trend throughout the course to satirise formal gear. Most have bought their tails at opportunity shops, then turned up usually in shorts, T-shirts and occasionally bare feet. The problem has been to get top hats. It is devilish hard to get top hats at opportunity shops so the thing to do has been to make your own out of cardboard or vinyl. There was a whole team of these this year who organised themselves to get at Sir John Kerr. If you were going to hoot Sir John in his magnificent formal gear it was right to be dressed in magnificent anti-gear. And, mind you, some of them aren't all that slapdash. One young man had a set of tails made entirely from recycled denim.

Last year we had a team of women who turned up with ceramic pots on their heads. One of these pots had a propeller on top which whizzed round like an old Lysaght windmill. But if you ask any fashion writers they will tell you that for the past two or three years there has been no theme. The only object has been to shock, startle or amaze.

The VRC used to consider fashion and elegance at the Cup so important that it ran an annual contest called Fashions in the Field, and there were prizes for the best outfits in all the various price ranges. What's more, they used to bring out famous beauties such as the Baroness Thyssen to kick things along.

The greatest year was 1964 when they produced Jean Shrimpton, representing England, and Christine Borge, representing France. Miss Borge had a sensational arrival. I remember she walked down the gangway of the aircraft, swayed and collapsed unconscious on the tarmac.

Tony Lewis of the Wool Board sprinted forward, beat all competing males by 10 yards and carried her off in his arms while TV cameras purred. What's more, the picture made every front page in the country.

Jean Shrimpton didn't arrive until Derby Day. I discussed this with Miss Borge.

'When is the Shrimp due to arrive?'

'I don't think she'll be here until the Derby is almost over.'

Christine smiled a very feline smile. She said: 'All I need is just like the Shrimp to come along and take all the publicity I had.'

You know the rest. That was the year the Shrimp startled every female in Melbourne to the tips of their stiletto heels by appearing in the members' stand at the August Derby with no hat, no stockings and in a plain white shift.

But at least there was a semblance of formality and style. Since then we have had the void and it is up to the sociologists to explain why. It has been said many times that when there is a destruction of social mores, when a society begins to mock the customs of the tribal fathers, when they spurn the religion that has been handed to them over many generations, then this is the decadence that destroys. I'm worried.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The kookiest Cup of them all, The Bulletin, 13 November 1976. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
