Slugging it out with snails

5 min read.

First published 06 Mar 1976

After wine merchant Len Evans boasts of eating Sydney park snails, Keith Dunstan attempts to serve home-grown garden snails to his dinner guests, enduring a three-day purification regime, an escaping bucket overnight, and a first tasting that is leathery before the second snail redeems the whole exercise with a glass of dry suburban riesling.

THAT WELL-KNOWN eater and drinker Len Evans has come over here a number of times and boasted that the plain, ordinary, plebeian snails that you get in Sydney are a delight to eat. One time he even went so far as to suggest that the snails you get on the west side of Hyde Park have a delicacy and a richness.

Frankly, I never quite believed him. It was difficult to pin the man down to the time and date that he ate those snails. I didn't believe him, that is, until a pamphlet was dropped in our letter box. It was from the Hortico Company, the people who produce all sorts of chemicals for the garden. The article in this pamphlet read: 'Did you know that the big brown snails you get in your garden are a delicacy to eat? Put your captive snails — a dozen makes a tasty snack — into a clean box or bucket with a couple of good lettuce leaves. Replace the lettuce as it gets eaten out.'

Now at our place we are from a rich school of snails. At our place we are not allowed to use detergent, not allowed to spray either a fly or an apple. No chemical fertilisers, no plastic: and if we put down bait for a snail there would be a total family walk-out. Instead of poisoning the snails, I thought, how much better to eat them.

I found some marvellous snails deep down among the blades of the iris, and our privet hedge was virtually a seething slithering army of them.

I put them in a bucket, put a nice cover on top, popped in the lettuce as required, and left it under the kitchen table.

Next morning there were snails crawling up the wall, leaving trails across the kitchen table, there were snails in the curtains, on the telephone. The cover had been too flimsy. This little incident placed something of a strain on the marriage.

I have found since then the only answer is to keep the snails in a bucket in the garage. On top of the bucket you put a large piece of three ply and on top of that a brick. Snails are most ingenious when they are trying to escape from snailiverous humans.

You must appreciate how important it is to put the snails through a purification period. Snails tend to eat plants which are lethal to humans. The French put them for days on beds of bran. My Hortico instructor said lettuce. Well, they have to be on this lettuce at least three days.

The bucket has to be cleaned out daily. This is the first occasion that you discover how peculiarly devoted you have to be to proceed with this thing.

So they get lettuce for three days. Then they are starved for another two or three. On your feast day you soak them in a solution of salt and vinegar for two hours. That kills them.

Next you put them in boiling water for five minutes and you further thoroughly rinse them in running water.

What next? Well, you remove the snails from their shells with a skewer. You cut off their heads and you cut off the black bile bit at their tail end. This is testing moment number two. If you can survive this part with a strong stomach you are a genuine snail lover. Actually it is a good idea to get your friends to undertake that part.

Now you put them in an enamel pan with some wine, salt, pepper, garlic, bay leaf and brandy. You simmer until they are tender, maybe half an hour or more. Meanwhile you clean the shells. You boil them in water with some bicarb of soda, for around 30 minutes. Frankly, our privet hedge brown snails are disappointing visually. The French vineyard snails have superb, honey-coloured, almost porcelain shells and are a delight on the table.

But steady on, don't give up yet. Cream some butter with chopped parsley, crushed garlic, and a little chopped shallot, season with salt and pepper and place a little of this in each shell. Add the snail and seal with more butter.

Now you have to put them on a dish, standing up so that all the juice won't run. Unless you have a special snail dish, this is difficult, so place your snails in a bed of salt. Keep the oven at 400 for about 10 minutes to heat and melt the butter.

If you are one of the elite you have all the correct snail plates, claws and forks. It's nice to serve at least a dozen snails to each of your guests.

Well, on the first occasion we had our Escargot Batmani Suburbiï, it was terrifying. Frankly I stood suspended in front of my plate for maybe five minutes, like a poor creature fearful of a cold shower. Finally I took the plunge. The first snail was a bit leathery, rather rank and bitter, I thought. Disappointing. Definitely an east Hyde Park snail.

I was almost giving up. But wait. The second was juicy and splendid. Definitely more tender and succulent, indeed, so good I proceeded to wolf them all, taking between snails sips from an excellent, acid, dry suburban snail riesling.

But to be honest I have had much better snails in Paris.

I am now seeking information. Obviously the snails among our irises are not getting the right tucker. They're not as big as French snails. So what do we do to fatten them up? Do we give them Kelloggs corn flakes and All Bran? Or would they eat Pal meaty bites?

Are we getting our snails from the right area? Should we be collecting them in North Coburg, Upper Fitzroy or North Balwyn? Or would we do better to breed our snails among the wines at some of our better vineyards, say, Chateau Tahbilk or Milawa?

I await your answer.
