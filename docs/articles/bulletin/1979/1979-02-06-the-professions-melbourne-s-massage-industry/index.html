Melbourne's massage industry

7 min read.

First published 06 Feb 1979

Social welfare researcher Bronwen Rumbold lifts the lid on Melbourne's reputation as the massage parlour capital of the world, tracing the city's 300-plus parlours, their links to migration patterns and organised crime, and the Social Welfare Department's cautious attempts at reform.

IT IS NOT easy to discover why rich, snooty, wowser-ridden, sometimes beautiful Melbourne became the so-called massage parlor capital of the world. The massage parlors just crept in, starting maybe 15 years ago.

For a long time most of us genuinely believed they were health giving places entirely devoted to those who had a bad back and that sort of thing.

We don't believe in these euphemisms any more. The Department of Social Welfare has now labelled them firmly as houses of prostitution or, if you like it better, brothels.

For two years now the department has been making its study of prostitution and last November it produced a comprehensive report. Over 1000 copies went out to councils, shires, interested bodies.

The report contains much information, but if you read between the lines there is a definite air of puzzlement and a call for help and comment. Does anyone really know what to do about those who are giving their bodies for hire?

The officer who has had most to do with this is a very pretty dark-haired lady, 27-year-old Bronwen Rumbold. She is personal assistant to Victoria's Minister for Social Welfare Brian Dixon.

She concedes that Melbourne ranks very high on the world massage parlor scene. Sydney is comparable, she says, but then Sydney has a different problem and the pressure is more intense. As for Adelaide that's very quiet.

"I have walked down Hindley Street," she said, "and I know what to look for. There would be four times as many prostitutes operating in various streets in St Kilda."

The police figure for the number of massage parlors here is 154. However Bronwen Rumbold says many of them operate in a very quiet manner. The real figure would be more like 300.

There are 112 escort services, which include call girl services, and visiting massage girls. These employ 300 prostitutes. Again the figure could be much higher.

Then there are 50 who get out and hustle on the street. Again the true figure would be much higher.

Often the police put them almost on a roster so that the thing doesn't get out of hand. One girl might work Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays, another Tuesdays, Thursdays and Saturdays.

But why Melbourne? "I asked this question, too," said Bronwen. "One woman told me if I only used my head then the answer was obvious. Melbourne was a central seaport, and all seaports were the same. But there's more to it than that.

"Melbourne always has been the biggest migrant centre with a strong imbalance in the sexes. Not only that, many of the immigrant groups are very protective of their women. So there has been a need.

"You get migrants of all ages looking for prostitutes, but otherwise, do you know the biggest clients? Usually middle-aged men, over 45, and usually married."

Bronwen started her investigation more than two years ago. What with her gentle family background one wondered how she possibly made a start.

"It wasn't that difficult. A girl I was with at the university was a prostitute. She needed extra money to cover her expenses and she found this was an easy way to do it. She was a great help to me.

"People don't realise this. There is no law on the social background of a prostitute. They come from everywhere. You get the girls who moonlight. They just want to make enough money to go for a trip to England or wherever. They could be school teachers or bank clerks or secretaries, so they do a stint in massage parlors.

"The worst types, the most tragic, they're the ones that work the streets. They could be transvestites, homosexuals, anything. Mostly they are heroin addicts. They've got to get money for a fix, and usually they are very aggressive.

"I've had some very nasty allegations about people who have given girls two or three shots so that they will be forced to do this thing. It's a horrible circle really. It's pretty hard to get the courage to work the streets unless you are on drugs. Once a girl starts there's no way out.

"In St Kilda you see them in Robe Street, Acland Street, Fitzroy Street. I have come across 30 in less than an hour.

"The youngest was thirteen.

"If you go to a very smart place, sex can be expensive, it could cost anything, but in the streets the competition is fierce and the price is coming down. It could be $25 or even as low as $10 or $12."

The problem is not only in St Kilda. There are massage parlors in most suburbs. "You even get them in nice meat and three vegies suburbs like Box Hill," said Bronwen. "The problem is that you have to get some kind of consensus as to what the public is prepared to accept. If you stop it entirely it will only come up in some other form.

"And you have to consider just how far you are going to use up your police resources. All right, you close the massage parlors, you pick up all the prostitutes and you overflow your jails. Society can't afford the expense."

Bronwen quoted the case of San Francisco's new district attorney. He campaigned on a pledge that he would not waste his office's resources on prostitution cases. They found it had cost $375,000 to arrest 2000 prostitutes. It was all to no avail. Once the processes of law had gone through the prostitutes were all back in business again.

Bronwen gave The Bulletin a copy of the paper on prostitution. Here are some of the quotes:

Difficulty in prosecuting: It is a fairly common practice for owners of massage parlors to have no direct involvement in their management. Some, it is said, remove themselves still further from the operation by setting up a company which in turn sets up a massage parlor company. The managers may then be changed and the process begins again.

The amount of money which prostitutes are required to pay to the owner of the brothel varies — sums of between half and two-thirds have been suggested.

The owners of many massage parlors in Melbourne have no direct involvement in their management. One estimate is that about 90 per cent of them operate in this way.

Organised crime: Police and others maintain that organised crime has not been able to move into Victoria's prostitution scene. This relative freedom from organised crime seems to be true of prostitution in other parts of the world and, according to the US Task Force on Organised Crime, prostitution is too difficult to organise and maintain discipline.

The report suggests however that inter-parlor rivalry has caused crime. There is an over-supply of prostitutes and smaller parlors are fighting for survival.

Venereal disease: While the standards of hygiene in the business vary, the knowledge of venereal disease and techniques far outstrip that of the general population. Prostitutes who are fairly experienced in the game usually insist on checking their clients for any sign of infection and require a condom to be worn during intercourse.

Police corruption: The Victorian police force are reportedly less prone to corruption than their counterparts in New South Wales. Involvement in policing prostitution does, however, make policemen subject to the possibility of being offered bribes and there is some suggestion that a certain degree of victimisation takes place — where police prosecute certain operations whilst others are relatively free from police intervention.

The report suggests that the more you drive prostitution underground the more it comes up in other forms, the more it brings in the criminals, the more it encourages corruption.

Just before Christmas the government introduced some tough new penalties. Those engaged in street walking or the famous practice of gutter crawling can be fined $250 or up to $1000 for a third offence. Then there is legislation against massage parlors which are found operating outside the conditions of their licence, and those in breach can be fined $200 or $500 for a second offence.

The legislation is designed particularly against those who you might say "upset the horses" or disturb the peace. One gets the impression that if you carry on your prostitution without upsetting anybody then you will be relatively free.

Clearly the Social Welfare Department in Victoria is gently feeling its way, being as sensitive as it possibly can be to modern public opinion.

Moral issues are not the biggest problem any more. Bronwen Rumbold says the most sensitive are those who are worried about their money interests. A massage parlor moves into the street, property values are at peril, then owners are out with their protest sheets as fast as you bat your eyelid.

Source: Melbourne's massage industry, The Bulletin, 6 February 1979. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
