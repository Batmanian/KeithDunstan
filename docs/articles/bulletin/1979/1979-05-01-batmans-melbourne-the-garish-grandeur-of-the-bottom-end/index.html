The garish grandeur of the bottom end

5 min read.

First published 01 May 1979

The "Chicago end" of Collins Street — the block of William Pitt's exuberant Land Boom buildings including the Rialto — faces a development battle, and Planning Minister Alan Hunt has declared a three-month freeze while a British expert recommends recycling the entire block.

YOU APPRECIATE, of course, that the Paris end of Collins Street has now thoroughly shaken off its Champs Elysees connotations. We call it the Chicago end or in moments of real delirium, the Developers' Delight.

If you want to become nostalgic and dream about Victoriana as it was, then you have to go down the other end, where the developers versus the nostalgers have been waging a noble battle between King and William Streets over some of the splendid works of William Pitt.

Pitt was the architect in excelsis of the Land Boom. Some people call his work Venetian Gothic, maybe a more accurate title would be Gee Whiz Vulgaria. He believed his architecture should match the brilliance of his era and he designed my favourite building, the Princess Theatre.

Lovely it is. Up there in Spring Street it has sculptures of plump ladies, it has two domes with gorgeous, useless little balconies on top. It has figures of characters way up high blowing trumpets, and back in the old days it had a roof which folded back to reveal the heavens.

He designed the old Stock Exchange building, an incredible piece. You would think actually it was designed by Ronald Searle. It has such an exotic design it really made the greed of money seem utterly charming.

The Federal Hotel was his. Alas, now gone to the developers and turned into an office tower. It too was a wild design with an exquisite crazy domed tower that overlooked Collins Street. Marcus Clarke is alleged to have said it looked like a castle built for a Norman earl, by an architect who had been dreaming of Dore's illustrations to Balzac. Actually Clarke was dead eight years before it opened, but the description remains apt.

In the 1880s a smart developer, Patrick McCaughan, came out from New Zealand. He had the idea that business had to develop west down Collins Street and he transformed the whole block with buildings which included the Winfield, Rialto, Olderfleet, South Australian Insurance, the Record and Robb's building.

Well maybe not Robb's — John Robb was a wealthy Melbourne contractor of the 80s — but all these buildings had the same character. The Winfield, the Rialto and the Olderfleet are pure Pitt and the most astonishing of its kind is the Rialto.

Professor Brian Lewis, a former professor of architecture at Melbourne University, has described it as an "aesthetic stinker" and added: "It's so vulgar, it's just a mannerism using debased Gothic forms." He says we are merely confusing age with beauty.

He misses the whole point. It's the vulgarity that makes it so wonderful, so different, say, from the black tombstone of a BHP building. Graeme Davidson, senior lecturer in history at Melbourne University, a member of the Historic Building Council, says it is important to preserve the Rialto because it is such a perfect example of social history. Once down it is gone for ever.

Let me tell you about the Rialto. William Pitt finished it in 1890 right at the dizzy height of the boom. Its Venetian Gothic front was and still is covered in a crazy network of cornices, and fascinating pigeon-loving fiddly bits.

It was designed as the ultimate, the perfect set up for the businessman, a sort of grand selling centre with individual suites set along a line of galleries. These galleries now remind one a little of Alcatraz, but they were terrific then. Every suite was equipped with a telephone, incredible for that time, and they all had access to superb, modern, hydraulic lifts.

I can't remember the Rialto lifts too well, but I do remember a lift in the Winfield building next door, presumably designed by Pitt. Inside it was almost like a boudoir with mirrors, polished cedar and plush seating. It moved with such an immutable, majestic slowness, it was said that a girl could lose her virginity, just in time, between the second and third floors.

The owners of nigh on 70 percent of the buildings in the block are the National Mutual Life Association and the Hammerson group of companies. The National Mutual applied back in 1976 for permission to demolish both the Rialto and the Winfield buildings without success and there's no question the owners now wish they had never heard of these dear old monuments.

Last November the National Mutual applied to the Historic Buildings Council for permission to pull down three-fifths of Williamson's backdrop. The council rejected the application.

Planning Minister Alan Hunt, with an election time, is not changing his mind. Theoretically there is provision for compensation for firms that can prove they would suffer heavily by the building being preserved for posterity, but extracting the money is entirely another matter and this is becoming a fascinating test case.

The government commissioned a British expert, Geoffrey Holland, to make an investigation and he recommended that the block be re-cycled. So, just before Easter, Planning Minister Alan Hunt declared a three month freeze on development for the entire block bounded by King, Collins, William Streets and Flinders Lane. No demolition can proceed during that time.

The beauty about the block is that the buildings are all homogeneous and give great character to the area. Right now it is a fine victory for the nostalgers.

But what happens after three months and who eventually will sign the cheques to pay for this history?
