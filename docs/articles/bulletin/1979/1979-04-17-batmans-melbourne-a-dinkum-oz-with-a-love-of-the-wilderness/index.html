A dinkum Oz with a love of the wilderness

5 min read.

First published 17 Apr 1979

Percy Trezise, racing cyclist, airline pilot, artist and Aboriginal rock art expert, visits Melbourne to promote the Second World Wilderness Congress in Cairns in 1980 and shares his vision for Cape York Peninsula as the world's greatest wilderness area.

PERCY TREZISE has been on a tour of great PR exercises.

Percy is one of Australia's 24-carat indigenous characters. If you really wanted someone to represent us on another planet, such as Venus, as a dinkum Oz type, then Percy would be your man.

He was born in Tallangatta, Victoria, and he has been pretty well everything — racing cyclist, airline pilot, writer, artist, anthropologist, tribal mate of the Aborigines, yarn spinner, and just about the greatest expert there is on Aboriginal rock art.

He has been down south because he wants to plug the Second World Wilderness Congress which is to take place in Cairns during the week June 9 to June 13, 1980. The first was in Johannesburg in 1974 and now Australia has the second.

He wants 2000 people there — 1000 from overseas and 1000 from around Australia. He believes he has to start persuading early.

Why? "Well," he says laconically, "look at the air fares. It's cheaper for you to go to San Francisco than fly from Melbourne to Cairns."

And why Cairns for the wilderness conference? Percy explains that the Queensland Government is just in the process of declaring the entire Cape York Peninsula as wilderness.

"This," he said, "will be the largest wilderness area in the world, equivalent in size to Victoria and Tasmania combined." Percy can hardly believe their good luck. Imagine Joh Bjelke-Petersen doing this thing! Is the Queensland Government really so interested in preserving the ecology?

"I keep looking for other reasons," he says. "I think possibly they were looking for a buffer zone. There are more cattle within 100 miles of Rockhampton than in all Cape York and the Northern Territory combined. They are terrified of exotic diseases like foot-and-mouth, screw fly and blue tongue that could come down from Indonesia or New Guinea."

The turning of the peninsula into a wilderness will not be an overnight affair. It could take three generations. The process started a few months back when the Queensland Government bought Lakefield Station, which actually comprised four stations and covered half a million hectares. The price of the lease was $300,000.

Percy says there are still 30 cattle stations on the peninsula, but it is a matter of time before they fall to the government, because cattle-raising is no way to make money in the north.

"Some people are prepared to carry on and live close to the poverty line because they love the area," he says. "They've been there for three or four generations. But more and more want to get out. The end began when the southern unions forced the owners to pay the Aborigines working on the stations award wages.

"You see, in the old days the Aborigines, mothers, fathers, grannies, kids, all lived on the station. They got their food, clothing, the lot. It was a big paternal thing. I won't say conditions were too fancy, because they weren't. But they were happy.

"There were two big events in the year when they got boozed — Christmas and the week of the picnic races. Come the rains they'd go walkabout.

"When the unions forced the stations to pay award wages they had to order all the hangers-on off the place and use only the employables. This was detrimental to the Aborigines and detrimental to the stations.

"Most of the blacks went into the missions and remained there getting boozed in the canteens. Now they just sit there in a state of hopeless resignation."

And what about the mines — Weipa, bauxite, coal? Percy doesn't see any contradiction in this. He claims only 2 to 3 percent of the wilderness will ever be used for mining. However the tourist potential is immense and there will be many job opportunities for the Aborigines as rangers and guides.

Percy sees Cape York as the world's best area for prehistoric rock art. He estimates there are at least 1000 sites and he has been instrumental in finding 95 percent of them.

"I'm getting good at it now," said Percy, "I can pick the most likely sites just by flying over in my Cessna. Then we go in after them by Land Rover. On a couple of occasions the areas have been so inaccessible we had to be dropped in by helicopter."

His latest find was the Magnificent Gallery, 396 individual figures, just near the top of the Great Divide and 50 kilometres south of Laura. They called it "Magnificent" because that was their immediate comment, 'This is magnificent.'

Percy continued: "Those rock carvings would be 13,200 years old and they have been seen so far by only 30 Europeans. Xavier Herbert was one of them. He's got himself a new pair of plastic knee joints and it was a pretty good test for them.

"A thrill to find it? I'll say it was. If you had been listening you would have heard the yodelling of Eddie and me right down in Melbourne."

So that's Percy's life. These days he's a shaggy, sunburnt character with greying whiskers, a sort of northern Ernest Hemingway.

He retired from Ansett five years ago with no regrets. This is much more fun than the daily back-and-forth airline routine, as dull as driving a tram.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: A dinkum Oz with a love of the wilderness, The Bulletin, 17 April 1979. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
