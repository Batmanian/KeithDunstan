A lifetime of penance

5 min read.

First published 22 May 1979

A self-confessed penaholic traces his obsession from the torment of left-handed steel-nib schoolwriting, through confiscated fountain pens, the ballpoint revolution, cartridge pens, and now the new "floating ball" — with a stop at Taft's pen shop in Collins Street.

YOU MUST have noticed that there is a new type of pen on the market, the "floating ball."

The Japanese people began making it before Christmas and now everybody is into it. Parker and Sheaffer have elaborate models in stainless steel and such.

The blurb in the ads tells us that at last this is the ultimate in writing.

Already I have bought 10 of them, just to be added to the 30 or 40 different writing gadgets I have in my drawer. You must realise I never venture abroad without at least four pens, including my marvellous old Sheaffer snorkel fountain pen.

Could one reveal the true terrible story of the creation of a penaholic? It all began at preparatory school when we were compelled to write with the old-fashioned school-issue steel nibs.

I was a left-hander and the feeling in those days was that any young gentleman who persisted in writing with his left hand was likely to end up as a masturbator or an alcoholic, and certainly no decent bank would ever give him a job. Yes, the opportunity to go into a bank was almost the ultimate.

You can't imagine the horror of this thing. We had built-in ink wells on every desk and this black stuff spread all over my hands, my clothes. Other chaps got gold stars on their exercise for neatness; Dunstan was constantly thrashed.

As my right-handed spidery scrawl spread all over the paper in a desperate desire to acquire the correct thin up-strokes and graceful thick down-strokes, the thought came that if only I could acquire the correct writing instrument then maybe I would also acquire a beautiful hand.

Swan brought out a beautiful lever-fill fountain pen, price seven shillings and sixpence. This seemed the answer. The fountain pen was considered evil, worse than some of the previously mentioned aberrations, and my headmaster confiscated it.

Onoto brought out another, even cheaper, two shillings and sixpence. Headmaster confiscated that, too.

So I became a clandestine pen-user. One of the most beautiful things I bought was a pen with glass barrel. You had to pump the ink into it. The leaking of the ink on to one's clothes was a continuing misery to my mother, but ah, how beautiful the royal blue ink looked in the glass barrel.

Meanwhile, my writing became worse and worse until finally an enlightened form teacher pleaded with me to use my left hand. "I don't care if you write with your feet," he said, "if only you can produce something legible."

That ideal never came about but the hunt for the writing tool which would produce it continued. Maybe it could be done with the right pencil.

One firm, I think it was Eversharp, brought out the push-button propelling pencil. This was the marvel of the age. You had a continuous supply of leads and theoretically it went on for ever. Every time you pressed the button on the top out would come another millimetre of lead.

That's if the damn thing didn't jam.

Then my father brought back from the US a push-button operated multi-coloured pencil. I really loved that one. One would spend hours on homework composing each letter of each word in a different color. That gadget was confiscated repeatedly.

Then came the cartridge-filled fountain pen and the fountain pen with adjustable nib, and the interchangeable nib. I tried all those. The doom came inevitably when you dropped the darned things nose down on the hard school floor. So then you would have to take your pen into Taft's in Collins Street for repairs. Taft's is still there, one of those rare establishments that sells nothing else but pens.

It was after the war that the ballpoint pen first appeared. Biro became a new word in the language. We were told that with a ballpoint you could write anywhere, upside down, downside up and even on a crocodile's back under water.

The occasion for me to use a ballpoint pen on a crocodile's back under water never arose, but I did discover that they would not work upside down, nor were they useful on a reporter's notebook when writing in the rain.

Indeed, some of the early models were difficult to get going. The system to encourage an ink flow was this. You tied them to a length of string then whirled them round your head like a propeller.

No longer was I at school, but my son had to endure this new advance. It was interesting to note that the fountain pen was now perfectly acceptable but the ballpoint was the new sin.

Any young man who learned to write with a ballpoint was due for certain perdition and would end up for sure not with a fine job in a bank or insurance office but as a night soil carter.

Even to this day, Georges — that upper-class store in Collins Street — still has instructions to staff never to use a ballpoint pen when writing letters.

To be frank, I am not over-fond of the ballpoint and I find the fibre-tipped pens messy. As a penaholic I still get satisfaction out of a good fountain pen, particularly the aforementioned antique snorkel Sheaffer.

Taft's tell me they still sell a wide variety of fountain pens but they are used only by a very small proportion of the community. The banks, you will notice, quite outrageously no longer provide blotters at their public counters.

However, the new thing is the floating ball. I have them in red, black and blue, and I must confess they are supremely efficient, if lacking a little in character.

I can only presume that headmasters are confiscating them like crazy and the boy or girl who uses one is bound for certain ruin and will never get a job.
