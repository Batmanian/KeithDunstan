Melbourne gets behind Moomba

15 min read.

First published 06 Mar 1979

On its 25th anniversary, Dunstan traces the turbulent history of Moomba — from its disputed Aboriginal name meaning "let's get together and have fun" to the dramas of sinking gondolas, a cable tram wreck, Rolf Harris and the garbage strike, and Mickey Mouse's pie in the face.

THERE is an old saying around here that Melbourne is like a Kygmy. That is, one of Li'l Abner's favourite creatures. The Kygmy was good when it was being kicked, and it served a valuable purpose because it relieved the tensions of those who kicked it.

Nothing has been kicked around quite as much as Moomba. Even today if you mention the word in polite company distinguished citizens will give a smirk as if you had mentioned something absurd.

Yet Moomba is the great survivor, and believe it or not, a phenomenal success. This week it is a quarter of a century old and most of the previous Kings and Moomba Queens are coming to Melbourne to celebrate for the biggest Moomba yet.

Lots of superlatives are being thrown about. Some of the organisers, like its chairman, Cr David Jones, claim it is the biggest and best festival in the world.

Well hang on, there are some pretty good festivals in South America, and another in California that I could name, but even so, the crowds that Moomba draws are remarkable.

A previous Moomba director estimated the crowd for the Labor Day procession at just on one million. This is nonsense. Even the Ayatollah Khomeini has problems pulling a mob like that, but would you settle for 300,000? This is the regular police estimate. That's a whopping get-together, three times a footy grand final, a Melbourne Cup quadrupled, in short, the biggest regular crowd on the continent.

Moomba takes place at the one time when Melbourne can guarantee sunny, benign weather. It lasts 11 days with shows every night in the Music Bowl and on the Yarra. Total crowd estimates for the whole festival are over four million.

But don't think it has been easy. The history of Moomba is packed with rows, personal jealousies, explosive committee meetings, sackings, walk-outs, and the possibility of cot death seemed almost inevitable in its second or third year.

There have even been rival committee meetings and one chairman resigned after being in office only half an hour. It has had to survive the most consistent campaign of classic Australian knocking that we have seen. The quotes come easily to mind. It was a natural to talk about the annual Doomba procession, everybody did that. There was the Sydney columnist, who, as a hardy annual, revelled in his stories about the Bacchanalian knitting competitions by the Yarra, and the blood-pulsing cat competitions in the Town Hall.

Who started the whole thing and why? There are different opinions about that. There's no doubt in my mind that the man who gave birth to Moomba was Sir Maurice Nathan with mid-wives Cr Bill Comeadow and Bob Gardner, director of the City Development Association.

Cr Maurice Nathan, a tough, blunt, get-things-done character, was managing director of Patersons, the furniture chain, and there was considerable fear at the time that the city was losing business. Maurice Nathan went on a world tour and came back enthusing over the Edinburgh Festival. "Let's face it," he said. "What can a tourist see or do in Melbourne? The place is bloody dull. It needs livening up." On May 12, 1954, he put the idea to a gathering of councillors, businessmen and newspapermen and in those dim days it was just the "Melbourne Autumn Festival." There has been a little bit of fun over the name ever since.

Sir Maurice recalls that he had a telephone call from Eleanor Knox, who was Town Hall roundsman for The Argus. She said: "I know you're looking for a name for this festival. I think I've got it — Moomba."

The astonished Maurice Nathan could only say: "What?"

"Yes, it's an Aboriginal word meaning 'Let's Get Together and Have Fun.'"

The more he thought about it, the more he liked it. "I still think it is a good name," said Sir Maurice Nathan several weeks ago.

The Sydney Morning Herald commented: "This is a dangerous word to let loose among the multitude. It doesn't suggest carnival but rather a moaning at the bar. Moomba could be a deadly snake, a funeral dirge or a new kind of saveloy."

The name was not in any of the Aboriginal dictionaries but eventually a scholar did discover an obscure Moreton Bay tribe and to them Moomba meant "Thunder."

In December, 1969, Mrs Lorna Lippman, an expert on Aboriginal affairs, wrote a letter to The Age. She explained that in southern Aboriginal languages "Moom" meant the human "behind" and "ba" meant "and."

The first Moomba was full of trauma. One Labor councillor called it a Bourke Street joke. The money should be spent on the slums of Carlton. Ballarat was furious because it interfered with the Begonia Festival. Ballarat was in Henry Bolte's electorate and never as Premier did he show much love for Moomba.

But that first Moomba, with Tom McCaw as secretary and Cr Bill Comeadow as chairman, was a big success. There was Shakespeare in the gardens, car racing around Albert Park, bicycle races in the streets, band leader Spike Jones came to town, Henley on the Yarra was switched to Moomba weekend and The Argus gave the Moomba procession crowd at 300,000. The Sun News Pictorial was rather more enthusiastic, "nearly a million."

The years from 1957 to 1959 were the most turbulent in the festival's history. There were two rival factions. In the left corner there was the City Development Association, originally inspired by Sir Norman Myer and backed by the retailers. In the right corner was the Melbourne City Council.

The CDA thought the council was dull, inept and lacking in ideas. The council thought that CDA was a bunch of sharks, which, to put it mildly, was trying to take over the city.

In 1957 Moomba looked as if it would expire altogether. Bob Gardner, a very efficient operator, moved in as the secretary of the CDA and he virtually ran the show.

The Adelaide Festival did not exist then but this was precisely the type of festival he had in mind. Get rid of the hundreds of little events and get Malcolm Sargent and the full London Symphony Orchestra, get the Shakespearean Company from Stratford. In May, 1958, Bob Gardner went on a world tour seeking such big-occasion visitors. However, while he was away the Melbourne City Council decided to take over completely.

Bob Gardner, who now has his own business, remembers the events. He had been to the big Rose Bowl parade at Pasadena and he was in a hotel in Pittsburgh. He said: "If I remember correctly the time was about three in the morning. I was sound asleep. There was a ringing on the telephone and groping in the dark I heard this voice: 'Mr Gardner? ... Yes ... It's the Melbourne Sun ... We hear you've been sacked as chairman of Moomba, have you got any comment?'"

It was a struggle between those who believed it should be an eye-opening cultural festival and those who believed it should be a grass-roots popular festival. Bob Gardner to this day believes it was his groundwork that gave the ideas to those who took over.

There was a new festival with a series of chairmen. E. B. Coles, later Sir Edgar, resigned after half an hour. Kenneth Myer was chairman for a fortnight and went on an overseas trip. George Griffith remained for a few months, then Bill Comeadow was back as chairman.

But Moomba did settle down. It had a long period of stability with Don Ingersole as director. Don had been an executive with Peters and he ran the festival with almost frenetic energy, insisting on doing everything himself. He could even be seen on a ladder, personally putting up the street decorations.

One of the great early creations was Billo the Bunyip. There was this great, shaggy shapeless thing of canvas and papier mache. He was 40 feet long and 15 feet high built on an army Duck. He looked splendid moving down Swanston Street, but down the Yarra Billo looked majestic, an extraordinary primeval thing. There was just one problem. Once in the water there was no escape for the Duck's exhaust gases and the Army men very nearly perished.

I remember one year the organisers brought out a superb gondola. A lovely thing, it was. Radio, Press, TV and all, came to the initial floating ceremony. There was a problem here, too. The boards had opened out during the long trip from Italy. The gondola went bubble, bubble, bubble and quietly sank.

Then there was the late Fred Asmussen, of Myers. Now Fred was a display genius with few peers anywhere in the world. He spent months every year working on his Moomba float. It was never revealed the vast sums he spent, but his floats were perfection, works of art. He loved gold, Nubian slaves and things like the vast Cleopatra barge.

One year he decided to bring back the cable tram to Melbourne. He scoured Victoria for tram plus dummy, then he set to work restoring them in minute detail. I doubt if Melbourne's cable trams ever looked as beautiful as Freddie's re-creation.

He mounted it on special wheels so it could move through the city streets. Well, the procession was under way and they were just turning into Swanston Street when there was a fearful noise of rending metal. The cable tram wheels had been caught in the tracks of the modern, vulgar electric trams. The undercarriage was ruined beyond all repair and the marshals ordered Freddie's cable cars out of the parade. He wept that day.

Or how about 1962, the year Bullen Brothers decided to put their whole darned circus in the parade. There was a swamp of protests. "Why advertise the misery of the animals and our shame?" was one cry.

So there were no animals, no lions, in the parade, but there was one marvellous float. It carried a large cage. Inside behind bars was Stafford Bullen, dressed in his ringmaster's gear, tails, top hat and all. Outside was a magnificent mock gorilla, frothing at the mouth.

Right from the beginning there were Moomba Queens, but it is very hard to get excited about beauty queens. Much more interesting were the Kings, like Sir Robert Helpmann who was so disgusted with the gear. King Helpmann in 1974 looked at his Moomba crown of artificial pearls, fake rubies and synthetic diamonds and said: "It's the most horrid thing I've ever seen." As for his red velvet "ermine"-lined cloak he fingered it as if it had come from Steptoe and Son, then said: "I shall wear it once — and you will never see it on me again."

The first King was Robert Morley and the idea of Moomba King came from The Herald back in 1967. Everyone at the Moomba office thought it a great idea. That night Don Ingersole rushed up to the Comedy to sign him up. The whole place was in darkness, he got through a trap-door at the Lonsdale Street entrance, walked into a concrete pit and fell 12 feet. He was there on his back for an hour and a half before he was found. Morley wasn't performing at the Comedy, he was at the Princess, and Don Ingersole performed Moomba that year hobbling on a stick, the result of a dislocated knee.

As for Morley he adored being King. I think he would have taken it on as a permanent job. He claimed there was nobody anywhere more fitted for the task. After all he had played Louis XII, Louis XIV, Louis XV, several of the more ghastly Georges ... he was a professional. He was hilarious. He liked being called His Majesty and he took the job seriously. The photographers asked him to kiss the Moomba Queen and he was shocked. "Certainly not! Royalty does not kiss in public. Who do you think we are? — Tony and Meg?"

Another spectacular King was Rolf Harris, King for 1975, and he got himself into real trouble. Right at Moomba time the City Council was having a garbage strike. By the end of the week it was becoming serious, garbage was piled high in the streets all around the city, Swanston Street, Elizabeth Street, everywhere. Worst of all was the smell and Melbourne was in danger of being condemned as a health risk. Unless the strike was solved by the Monday there would be no Moomba procession.

Alan Murphy was general manager of Moomba. His secretary Jill Crawford had an idea. Why not get King Rolf to call on his subjects to clean the streets as a grand patriotic gesture. The Lord Mayor, Ron Walker, called an immediate Press conference and His Majesty read out a very charming Royal Decree, which he composed in a taxi on the way to the Town Hall.

All hell broke loose. Rolf received one telegram which labelled him bluntly as "King Rat." The Municipal Employees' Union was furious and so were the wharfies. The strike looked like spreading all over Melbourne. Rolf even received a threat that he would have no hope ever again of putting on a show in Australia, the unions would see to that.

Rolf was devastated, almost in tears. He apologised and withdrew. But still the problem was not solved and it looked as if the Moomba parade would have to be cancelled.

Yet come Monday morning, as if by some strange magic, Melbourne was sparkling clean. Nobody could explain why and only a few weeks ago did Ronald Walker tell the true story.

He said suddenly that fatal weekend he began to receive calls from all sorts of service groups and voluntary organisations — Lions, Apex, Rotary, Boy Scouts, Girl Guides and just ordinary individual people.

They started the clean up at 2 am on the Monday morning and went right through until just before first light around 4.30 am. They brought old tip trucks, they hired trucks from city rental firms and many just used their own cars. They stuffed the rubbish in their car boots.

Ron Walker admits it was a monumental risk but they got away with it. One unionist put it to Walker that he had used council equipment to clean up the city, but he was able to say, honestly, he had not. It was a superb parade and Rolf travelling in a 1925 open Rolls-Royce was a triumphant King.

The most controversial King was Mickey Mouse in 1977, which The Age described as a middle-aged plastic rodent from California. There were demonstrations, and a patriotic team from Carlton even set up a rival king, Blinky Bill. Mickey had a pie squashed in his face during the Moomba Parade but, according to Alan Murphy, all this did no harm. It helped Moomba receive record publicity.

The present Moomba general manager, Alan Murphy, has some dramatic memories. One of the features of Moomba until recently was the Queen of the Pacific contest. Twenty beauty Queens or more came from India, South America, USA and so on. One year Miss Canada, considered by many to be the most beautiful girl in the contest, tried to kill herself when she failed to win the crown. The final judging was at the Melbourne Hilton. First she tried to take a flying leap through a closed window. Next she took the lift up to the top floor with the intention of throwing herself off the roof. There was a most spectacular scene with the Hilton's PR officer, Ian Evans, sprinting after her. Later the sobbing girl explained that her father had told her if she didn't win the contest "don't bother to come back."

The newspapers never got on to that story.

Another event which Alan Murphy remembers all too well was the visit of Neil Diamond in 1976. Diamond was giving three performances at the Sidney Myer Music Bowl, huge sell-out affairs with crowds of more than 30,000.

Diamond opened on the first night and Alan Murphy made arrangements with the Diamond management for their show to end at 10 pm at which time the traditional Moomba fireworks display would begin.

The Diamond Show was very late starting. Neil Diamond didn't even appear until 8.30 pm. Murphy said: "I had 40,000 people on the Yarra, many of them children, waiting for their fireworks. I couldn't hold them after time. Out of courtesy I rang the Music Bowl at 9.50 to say my fireworks were going off on time at ten. They blew up. Diamond's manager said: 'You can have your fireworks in the Music Bowl, Mr Murphy, because tomorrow night there won't be any concert. We'll get out of here. I'll see Mayor Walker tomorrow morning and I'll see that you're fired.'

"I argued that the concert made its own noise and would not be affected by the fireworks. My argument was blown out immediately. I could even hear the first bangs over the telephone. Diamond was singing a tender number from Jonathan Livingston Seagull, very quiet and tender. I'd ruined the concert, said the manager."

Alan Murphy said he had a terrible night. He knew it was far from unusual for American entertainers to stamp off and go home. He waited anxiously for the morning papers, but nothing happened and Mayor Walker did not send for him.

The present Moomba general manager, Alan Murphy, is the youngest of the Moomba directors and his chairman, David Jones, is the youngest of the Moomba chairmen. The battle of 1958 — culture versus popular entertainment — still goes on. Both David Jones and Alan Murphy argue that if you go through the program you will find just as much culture in Moomba as in the Adelaide Festival and very often the same events.

But there is no getting away from it, Moomba is a mass appeal show, the difference between a tabloid and a broadsheet.

The budget for the first Moomba was $40,000. Budget for the Moomba of 1979 is $600,000 — that includes $130,000 from the Melbourne City Council and $100,000 from the Victorian Government. Seven Moomba Kings are coming back, Oleg Popov from the Moscow Diamond's Circus, Johnny Famechon, Barry Crocker, Bert Newton, Mickey Mouse, Johnny Farnham and Sir Douglas Nicholls. The reigning King is Graham Kennedy. Twenty-one of the surviving 23 Queens will be there. Among the entertainers there will be Count Basie, Peggy Lee, the Royal Fiji Police Band, and the Royal Papua New Guinea Constabulary Band. For the Labor Day procession, March 12, there will be a new Chinese dragon, just imported from Hong Kong, 300 feet long, biggest in the world.

One feature, deeply shocking to some St Kilda Road lovers, is the sign. Claude Neon has made it for the 1979 Moomba and Moombas to come. It cost $45,000 and according to Alan Murphy it is the largest free standing sign ever made in Australia.

It goes right across St Kilda Road just before Princes Bridge. So there it is, the sparkling, blazing, gateway with just the one word MOOMBA. It leaves no mistake whatever that something is taking place.

So it has all survived 25 years. There is the water skiing, the fireworks, the famous bands, shows every night in the Bowl, ice show, water pageant and the big boast is that most of Moomba is free, 90 percent of it, according to David Jones. What's more, when it comes to putting bums into hotel beds and on to aeroplane seats it is way ahead of the football grand final and only just second to the Melbourne Cup.

So, maybe it is aptly named after all.
