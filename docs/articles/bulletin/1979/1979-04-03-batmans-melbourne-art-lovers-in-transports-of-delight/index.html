Art lovers in transports of delight

5 min read.

First published 03 Apr 1979

Melbourne has fallen in love with its trams and commissioned six leading artists — including Mirka Mora, Clifton Pugh and Andrew Southall — to paint them as moving canvases; Dunstan explores the resulting "tramurals" and their unlikely origin stories.

WAS it 10 years ago or even less that Melbourne used to hate trams? We had stirring leading articles: "Get rid of them." The former tramway chairman, Major General Risson, told everyone who would listen that they must be kept at all costs. Here was the ideal, non-polluting machine, the vehicle with no equal for the shifting of crowds.

Now that they are practically unique, Melbourne has developed a mystic love affair with its trams. You can have your Westgate Bridge, your Arts Centre, even the Melbourne Cricket Ground, the true symbol of Melbourne is the tram.

The love is manifested in the development of the tram as an art form. We have six trams which have been turned into "canvases" or as Dr Eric Westbrook calls them, "tramurals." We have trams painted by Mirka Mora, Andrew Southall, Michael Brown, Clifton Pugh, Less Kossatz and Peter Corrigan.

Moving along St Kilda Road through the trees they look enchanting. Andrew Southall's tram, for example, features a girl in a bikini, a yacht and a character sitting on a sofa. The Mirka Mora tram is absolutely marvellous. It is now an exotic, baroque thing, covered in cherubs, geese, flowers, angels. The ventilators on the roof are transformed to heads of geese and on the advertising panels inside she has written messages.

Sadly we do not have pictures for you of Peter Corrigan's tram — Rennie Ellis was banned from taking pictures of it and it has been temporarily grounded. It was a very bright tram. It featured a rising sun, lots of brilliant colors, and the message "MOTHER KNOWS." The Japanese Embassy complained, and now the rising sun is being changed for something else.

Dr Westbrook, director of the Ministry of Arts, said: "Peter Corrigan was quite innocent. He wasn't really trying to be offensive. The Embassy was just a little over-sensitive. Before he painted the tram he was speaking to one of the girls in my office. He was looking for a non-hurting symbol, and he said he was slightly scared about the whole thing. She replied, 'Don't worry, I'll be with you. Mother knows.' So he thought, what a great idea, and he put it on the tram. Now people think there is some strange hidden obscenity."

Clifton Pugh said: "I was having lunch with Irvin Rockman, and Ian Baillieu. We were trying to think of ways we could brighten up Melbourne and I was talking to John Olsen. We thought wouldn't it be a good idea if Australian artists painted up Qantas aircraft. It would be terrific around the world. So I rang the Premier, after a good lunch. Actually I rang the Premier immediately after the lunch, through the switchboard at the Town Hall, and Rockman liked the idea immediately."

So Eric Westbrook put the idea to the Premier, after a good lunch. "Of course, I was in it then," said Clifton Pugh, "there was no backing out. They gave me the most detailed sketch of a tram, outlines of the wheels, bogies, everything. I couldn't work them all out, but I've kept them still as souvenirs. Then they asked me to put in writing exactly what I proposed to paint on the tram.

"Geez, they've got no idea how an artist works. I tried to explain that I didn't really have any idea until I had the tram in front of me and I got some idea of the thing. But they kept insisting.

"Finally I said 'All right, there won't be any copulating nudes or anything like that. I'll probably paint birds and give a feeling of spring.'"

One gets the impression that the chairman of the Tramways Board, F. D. Snell, is pleased but not carried away with his tramurals. Sadly a painted tram does not result in increased patronage, but he does concede that they provide splendid publicity, and the public response has been enthusiastic.

He would like to see trams decorated for specific routes. Why not kangaroos, lions and giraffes on the tram that goes to the Zoo? Why not bathers, sea and sand for a St Kilda tram? Or even massage parlors?

Then what happens to the trams in the future? The paint out there in the rain, sun and carbon monoxide has a fair chance of lasting five years. But will an original Mirka Mora or Clifton Pugh tram become progressively more valuable? Will the time come, say in 1985, when a Pugh tram goes up for auction at Christies?

Or will international gangs of art thieves come along with oxy-acetylene torches and carve pieces off our trams?

Eric Westbrook has no doubt that the art trams is one of the best things done by the Ministry of Art. More money is being squeezed out of the Treasury and soon another six artists will be handed their trams and asked to get to work.

Snell says Pittsburgh and Chicago — what a great idea, and he put it on the Bicentennial and there is a beautifully painted articulated tram in Vienna, yet one feels Melbourne is doing something unique — a gallery trundling round the streets at around 10 miles an hour.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Art lovers in transports of delight, The Bulletin, 3 April 1979. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
