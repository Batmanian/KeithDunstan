All out for National Strike Day

5 min read.

First published 05 Jun 1979

With Melbourne deep in its annual strike season, railways, power workers, teachers and bank employees all walking out, Dunstan proposes a National Strike Festival in mid-March and outlines his business plan for the Melbourne Strike Emporium, to be headquartered at the Regent Theatre.

I HAVE come to the conclusion that we need a National Strike Day. Except for possibly the Melbourne Cup, a holiday given for a horse race, we do not have a day which fits the national ethos.

We do have Australia Day, which commemorates white settlement and the birth of the nation but which leaves most of us indifferent. We have the Queen's Birthday, which is not on her birthday, and we have Anzac Day, which raises little emotion for anybody under 40.

Even Labor Day has little to do with labor any more. In Melbourne it is the Moomba holiday and some unkind people have even construed that the whole thing is a plot organised by the Bourke Street capitalists.

I have been driven to these thoughts because clearly we are heading into the annual strike season. Melbourne is moving into its fine rhythm of railway strikes. We have just endured one day when practically all public transport stopped, then there was a "spontaneous walk-off of 100 gantry operators and forklift drivers," which was followed next day by an off-peak stop-work meeting of drivers and guards.

Seven power workers went on strike at the Hazelwood power station, which was enough to put 150,000 out of work. The teachers are restless, the bank workers are mutinous. We have had full page ads in our newspapers from the Australian Bank Employees' Union with the message "FULL INDEXATION OR ELSE ... stop work meetings will be held in capital cities and provincial centres throughout Australia."

Oh, I could go on and on and tell you about the building industry strikes, the oil refinery strikes, the truckie strikes.

In New York you can use the dial-a-strike service which, if the telephones are working, will provide instant information on the day's strikes. Here it is not so easy and a strike can be upon you before you are aware of it.

The Queensland Government is about to introduce legislation which will outlaw serious strikes in the essential services. This, of course, has been tried before and it never works. The result is only industrial unrest beyond belief. It will be a challenge to strike in the same way that they have challenged the citizens to engage in protest marches.

Why couldn't we get all our strikes over in the one day? No, perhaps that's asking too much. Let's make it an annual strike festival spread over three days. Naturally our strike festival would start on the Monday so that there could be a superb long weekend.

Naturally we would want it when the weather is at its balmiest and most perfect. I would suggest mid-March, after the intense heat. Maybe it could tie in with the Sydney autumn racing festival.

There is an excellent basis for a strike festival because the feeling for torpor and unwork fits well into the Australian character. And the idea of a national strike festival will deeply move everyone.

Donald Horne in The Lucky Country has written of the national contempt, loathing and distrust of authority. It began way back on the goldfields when many of the inspectors and police were no more than ex-convicts.

It continued on through the wool sheds and in two world wars Australian soldiers were renowned for the disrespect they had for their superior officers. The servile English Tommy saluted his officers without fail. It was almost an automatic reflex. The Australian had to be forced into saluting; he hated it.

So you see a National Strike Festival will be a lovely way of exercising this feeling. The whole nation will stop dead, utterly; a unique affair in the entire world — no trains, no theatres, no taxis, no banks, just sloth.

You are going to suggest that this would inconvenience us all. I don't think it would. I have noticed, particularly in Sydney, that the people are so tuned to strikes that nothing can faze them.

If it is transport, everyone has an alternate method of travel. If it is petrol, there is barely a house in Sydney that does not have a secret supply in a can at the back of the garage. If it is power all housewives have their survival kits of candles, kerosene lamps and primus stoves.

I mean, my wife has some kerosene lamps which are so charming she rather looks forward to a good power strike and often it is a relief from television.

If we know that the National Strike Festival is coming in mid-March we can all prepare for it and enjoy it.

For some time now I have been thinking of going into business and setting up the Melbourne Strike Emporium. I am hoping to get hold of the still largely disused Regent Theatre, which is right beside the City Square. The location is ideal because the City Square will continue to be the headquarters for most of our demonstrations.

My emporium would produce signs and banners for street marches. We would have a team of top copy writers who would provide witty slogans. (Banners here display a lamentably low standard.)

We would have a team of marchers, available for protests anywhere, any time. If you wanted a group for Queen Street, Brisbane, or Rundle Street, Adelaide, we would have them on the train in a jiffy, complete with the most up-to-date banners.

During the National Strike Festival we would have them all on roster. We would have marches to Parliament House, at 10 am, noon, 2 pm and 4 pm for various unions and we would make bookings for the Willesee Show, Nationwide, Four Corners, etc.

There is one trifling detail which as yet I have not solved. During the National Strike Festival my entire staff, as good, decent, slothful, disrespectful Australians, will be on strike.

Do you think I would have to pay them quadruple time?

Source: Batman's Melbourne: All out for National Strike Day, The Bulletin, 5 June 1979. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
