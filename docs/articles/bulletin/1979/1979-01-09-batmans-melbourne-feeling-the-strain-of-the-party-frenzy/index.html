Feeling the strain of the party frenzy

5 min read.

First published 09 Jan 1979

Keith Dunstan surveys the exhausting social demands of the Christmas party season, from the strain of remembering names after the divorce revolution to a handy list of conversation-starter topics for the desperate guest.

IT HAS been an extraordinarily difficult party Christmas. One would have thought that with the economic downturn, life would be becoming easier. The reverse is true — the harder the battle for the dollar, the greater the frenzy.

Let's take Christmas 1973 as the base year. That's the last Christmas before the miseries set in. I would suggest that partying is up 173.25 percent.

The bubbling drinks are served under the name of champagne. Two, Melbourne used to be a good Sabbatarian town in which Sunday was sacrosanct. Now, along with the playing of cricket, tennis and such on Sunday, the Sunday party is all the rage. You can have booze coming out of your ears from 9 am until midnight.

The strain began to tell on me this Christmas. It wasn't so much the drink and food, but the problem of remembering names and appearing to be awake still making conversation.

My wife gets very upset when I go to sleep at parties. She doesn't realise it is the greatest possible compliment to the host, that you are, really, in the greatest state of relaxation. Many people even have to take pills to go to sleep. So she says: "Look at him, he's asleep again."

As for names, we usually have a deal. I remember the husbands' names, if only she will remember the names of the wives.

That's broken down utterly. This year hardly anybody we know is still married to the people we used to know. Husbands are escorting different females and vice versa. So our chances of getting two names right on the one evening are about two out of 10. This adds to the terrible strain.

The ultimate strain comes in conversation. All through the holiday period you are at dinner parties, cocktail parties, brunches and so on. Suddenly you are introduced to six people you had never met until that moment. Or maybe you are left with Mrs Dougall, wife of the local bank manager.

She, too, poor creature, is in exactly the same agony. She looks up with appealing eyes: "Talk to me about something, anything. Just get it going."

Actually it is not easy. I am an introverted type. Conversation has never bubbled. I can stay broodily silent for as long as 20 minutes.

Our newspapers this year have gone berserk for supplements called "Leisure," "Cue," "Weekender," and such. They carry with the most minute detail every tiny item of amusement that is going on in town, where to go, what to do. We're never at a loss where to go. There are too many places. These papers would be much better advised to give quick summaries of conversation starters. You could pencil chapter headings on the back of your wrist with a biro.

I'm not very good at it, as I have already admitted, but here are a few ideas which might carry you through during parties at Portsea, Palm Beach, Rottnest, Kangaroo Island or wherever.

(1) As a child I remember the Christmases always absolutely divine in January. Do you agree that this planet is rapidly coming to an end because of the over-use of aerosol sprays?

(2) I think it would be rather nice if the Queen became a Catholic. After all this is the year of the Pope. Think what it would do for religion in general if the Queen, instead of sending a child to Geelong Grammar, sent one to Xavier College, Mandeville Hall or the like.

(4) A general air of pessimism is splendid for starting any conversation. I call this the "We'll be rooned" theory. It starts, "You know the downfall of this country? — The sheer lack of desire for work. As long as we have men like Halfpenny and Norm Gallagher this country doesn't have a chance."

You judge your market here. Should Halfpenny and Gallagher be present at the party, as well they might, you change the names to Fraser and Street.

(5) UFOs? I have spotted at least six of them. We have never been able to do anything with our back lawn because one of them landed right there. Look, we've got vehicles from half the universe invading our air space and what does Jim Killen do? He puts the three aeroplanes and four tanks that we actually possess into mothballs.

(6) "It's rather an intriguing red don't you think? A little light in body but full of generous, luscious flavor. What are you doing about the great red revival? I want to be able to tell my children that I went in there fighting at a time of crisis. I believe that it is the duty of us selfless creatures to reduce the great red lake. Personally I am trying to drink two bottles of red wine a day."

I just toss these topics in as a guide. What I am really suggesting is that your favorite newspapers should have a list of topics like this every day to go with the bridge column and the crossword.
