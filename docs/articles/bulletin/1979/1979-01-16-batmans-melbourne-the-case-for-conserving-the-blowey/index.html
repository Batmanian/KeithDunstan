The case for conserving the blowey

5 min read.

First published 16 Jan 1979

Dunstan surveys the curious world of indigenous Australian contests, from fly-swatting championships in Eaglehawk to rabbit-skinning at Moyston, before making a tongue-in-cheek case for protecting the blowfly as a uniquely Australian creature.

ONE OF THE most curious things which has happened during the past 10 years is the spread of strange, indigenous, Australian contests.

For example, at Cooee Bay, 48 kilometres from Rockhampton, they have the world coo-ee calling championships. Why they should be the world championships is a matter for conjecture. It is unlikely that any rival show takes place in Omsk or Valparaiso. Competitors' calls are registered on sound equipment, and I understand that one female champion recently hit 360 decibels. Imagine the power with which she is able to call her husband. As a sideline they also have cattle-calling and kookaburra-imitating.

At Wycheproof there is an affair called the "King of the Mountain." Competitors have to carry a 70 kg bag of wheat on their shoulders and run 1000 metres to the top of Mt Wycheproof. You must understand that Wycheproof is in the Mallee and anything slightly larger than a bump out there is classified as a mountain. Even so carrying such a bag is a Herculean effort, and the last winner did it in 6 minutes 43.2 seconds to win $1000.

At Moyston in the Western District of Victoria there is the world rabbit-skinning championship. Again there is an odd lack of competitors from Omsk and Valparaiso and they have just held the tenth of these annual events. However there is gloom hanging over this festival. The man who catches the rabbits and has them nice and warm, ready for skinning, is Ron "Killer" Crawford.

This year he admitted that there was a sad shortage of rabbits, all because of myxomatosis. He told The Age: "I didn't think I would see it in my time, but they are disappearing." All he could find in three days for the contest were 40 miserable specimens, not up to world championship standard.

One time if you fell over in a Moyston paddock you were likely to kill half a dozen.

Oh, I could go on. At Bendigo they have the national gold-panning championships. At Albury there is the boomerang-throwing championship of the world and recently at Lakes Entrance there was an affair called the Kinkuna Festival. This included the world scallop-splitting championship, the Australian damper-making championship and the Victorian billy-boiling contest. Regrettably I did not get to that affair, but one presumes that the winning damper was nicely scorched with a classic ceramic hard skin and that the billy champion swung his billy with a sweep that would do justice to Rodney Hogg.

I am telling you all this because we may have discovered the ultimate indigenous contest. Eaglehawk near Bendigo is now staging the world's fly-swatting championships.

According to Ian Mackay of The Sun this has developed into a local art form and it takes place at the back of Jack Taggart's Railway Hotel. The local champion swats under the professional name of Geronimo, but he lost his title this New Year.

He was defeated by a blow-in from Gippsland. A gentleman by the name of Mac Bragg is the new champion. Using a swatter with something of the overhead style of Guillermo Vilas he belted 100 flies in 25 minutes. The contest is now an annual one and they are expecting hot swatters from all over the country.

All this brought to mind a charming essay on the house fly and the blowfly written by the late Robin Boyd in a somewhat under-rated book called The Great Great Australian Dream.

He pointed out that the harbinger of spring in Australia was the song of the blowfly. Now the beauty of the blowfly's song was entirely lost on the early settlers. Their ears were not attuned to it. They were too used to the effete whine of the bee, which was, of course, a symbol of the northern hemisphere's lack-lustre outdoors.

Boyd in his very erudite piece listed all the varieties of flies and blowflies in Australia and he quoted entomologists on the point that we had never shown sufficient appreciation or understanding of these creatures.

He said the three well-known blowflies had these poetic zoological names — Lucilia cuprina, Calliphora stygia and Calliphora augur.

He mentioned these particular blowflies because he claimed that they were distinct members of a Pacific family unavailable in other countries. They were true Australians, strong, independent, free-swinging creatures. And if they occasionally attacked sheep, this was natural, an assault against creatures, which were not, like themselves, native to the country.

He went on a little about their beauty — "wings marked by delicate black veins ... body, a subtle amber with forward patches of olive grey-green reminiscent of the bluegum's leaves ... legs and hair of a moist brown verging on red ... large eyes black and deceptively expressionless."

He bemoaned the extensive use of insecticides and in a clever satirical piece he saw the creation of a special society formed to protect the blowfly. The president of this society was a gentleman named Colonel Reneally, who in his augural speech said: "No other country in the world with anything approaching our standard of living can boast anything like our Blowey, not in size and certainly not in number. To understand Australia one must first learn to love the blowfly."

The message was that if the present madness went on our unique and wonderful species of blowey would become extinct. Therefore the only thing to do was to protect the blowey in the same way that we are protecting the tiger snake and the crocodile.

The Eaglehawk championships do, in a way, pay tribute to our flies, but later we might have to rectify the violence of the affair.

Perhaps they should use nets, allow specimens to be studied for their beauty, and then release all captives at the end of the day.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The case for conserving the blowey, The Bulletin, 16 January 1979. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
