Passionate paintings by an old commo

6 min read.

First published 24 Apr 1979

Noel Counihan, at 65, holds a retrospective exhibition at Realities Gallery in Toorak, the converted church hall where some of us went to kindergarten, and talks about four decades of social-conscience painting, his communist years, street-meeting arrests, and why showing your work is like being naked in a room.

NOEL COUNIHAN, at 65, is a grand old commo. His appearance belies the passion, even the violence of his paintings. You could mistake him for Canon Counihan or maybe Archdeacon Counihan. He has grey frizzy hair which takes on aspects of a halo. He dresses in dull corduroys, battered suede shoes and he has a marvellous gnomish grin.

Some of the old hands are shocked out of their minds. Noel, the old working-class fighter, is having a great retrospective exhibition at Marianne Baillieu's Realities Gallery in Toorak. Realities is the converted church hall of St John's Church where some of us went to kindergarten.

And if that isn't enough, Gryphon Press has brought out a limited edition of the superb linocuts he did in 1950 with poems by Jack Lindsay. The books are quarter bound in leather, every one of them signed by the authors, price $150 a copy.

The nice people of Heyington Place, George's Road and the parish of St John's prove of the rightness of Noel Counihan's work. I found him, Archdeacon-like, at the gallery last week. In the left hand corner were the paintings from the 30s and 40s, borrowed from private collectors. Best of all were those of Wonthaggi and Broken Hill miners. They had metal tags on them in Russian. Counihan explained they went to Leningrad for an exhibition in 1960 and it was rather nice to hang on to the old Russian tags.

On the other side of the hall are pictures of men working on the Melbourne Underground. "I've always been fascinated by miners," he said. "You ought to see how they've changed. They wear these fancy plastic helmets. They have beards and pony tails. They look more like poets than miners."

Central left are the pictures taken from the Boy in the Helmet series. "For eight years I was pretty consumed by the Vietnam war," he said. He certainly was. The most striking picture shows a skull-like head in a helmet with a strained, agonised body, and the symbolism of the body crucified is clear enough. The possibility of a purchase by the Toorak RSL is unlikely.

Over in the right-hand corner there is a picture of Christ himself in the crown of thorns. This has caused some profound shock here and there. It is a mocking, leering Christ with a big display of teeth.

Counihan explained: "I wanted to get away from the usual saccharine picture of Christ. Both the Americans and Australians had Christian troops in Vietnam, both had their chaplains. How can you explain the hypocrisy and cant?"

The big picture right at the end of the hall showed the city building skyline and in front a line of eyeless, empty heads. I don't think the City Council will make a purchase.

There are pictures of dole queues, a deadbeat, a haunted naked mental patient and, as soon as you enter, you are struck by one astonishing painting — a startled, frightened nude young man.

Counihan is probably the only true social-conscience, political painter that we have and you wonder whether it is possible to sustain an anger for more than 40 years. He insists that he has not changed his views since the 1930s. He has mellowed a little perhaps, but his views are as strong as ever. He publicly opposed the oppression of Czechoslovakia. He is neither Peking nor Moscow. He believes in the Australian form of communism. "I take a pretty grim view of contemporary society," he said, "and I tell you, we are not going to find the solutions in Toorak or South Yarra."

Counihan is a marvellous talker and he is very hard to stop: "I was a cathedral choir boy at St Paul's for five years. Cathedral choir, that's the best school for atheism. You learn about the hypocrisy of it all. As a gesture of protest I set fire to the place once. I filled all the ventilators with dried leaves and put a match to them. Smoked them all out.

"I went to Caulfield Grammar for one year, that is until my parents ran out of money. I was always at the bottom of the class. We had one teacher there, a terrible temper. One day I spent the entire lesson doing sketches in my exercise book. This teacher crept up behind me and I thought it was the end. He said, 'Hey, that's not bad. You should get some training and take it up seriously.' The next year I went to the National Gallery Art School.

"My father was a commercial traveller. He never read a book in his life and the first time I ever saw a painting was those grim old paintings of archbishops in the Chapter House. My father said straight out, 'All artists are poofters.'"

He said, "I have always hated attracting attention to myself, and I was terribly nervous about going out to address street meetings. I had to build a sort of shield around myself."

One of those affairs was the Brunswick Strike Fight of 1934, where his rostrum was bolted down on to a lorry. This enabled him to address the crowd unmolested and it took the police 25 minutes to bash their way through to arrest him.

He was sentenced to three months jail but he won his case on appeal, and this had a significant effect on the rights of speakers in street meetings.

"Things improved when I got work as a cartoonist," he said. "I used to do cartoons for The Argus. They paid me a guinea a time. Then I did them for The Bulletin and all sorts of other papers."

He did not paint until he was 27 years old: "I was terribly nervous about it. I didn't know how to start."

Indeed he still admits to being nervous. One asked him how he felt this time. The National Gallery had honored him with a big retrospective exhibition in 1973 and now here was another.

"It's like being naked in a room with everyone looking at you," he said. "And all the critics want to piss on you." So, just as at the old street meetings, he builds a shield around himself to survive.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Passionate paintings by an old commo, The Bulletin, 24 April 1979. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
