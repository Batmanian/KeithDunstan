Pro Hart: a painter as busy as a bull ant

17 min read.

First published 15 May 1979

Dunstan profiles Pro Hart — the Broken Hill mining artist whom critics spurn and the public adores — tracing his childhood correspondence schooling, 15 years underground, the discovery by Kym Bonython, and his current plans for a 15-metre steel bull ant and a salt-pan canvas dropped from a balloon.

One of the characters in Keith Dunstan's recently-published book Ratbags was the Broken Hill artist Pro Hart. Here are excerpts from his profile of the man the critics and artists love to hate, and who — fortunately for him — the public love to buy.

BROKEN HILL is a mining city in the arid far west of New South Wales, 1125 kilometres from Sydney, 515 kilometres from Adelaide and just over 900 from Melbourne. It is an island, a civic island in the desert, as isolated as a desert atoll. The umbilical cords to civilisation are long unbending strips of bitumen across a landscape that consists of red ochre, sand, salt bush.

The mallee grows in weird twisting shapes, always struggling for survival and its wispy leaves give no shade from the blinding sun. The population of Broken Hill is 30,000 but the sense of isolation and clannishness is intense. It is hardly the place one would expect to find Australia's top-selling artist.

Top-selling? Well, his sister Margaret says so. Pictures by Pro Hart rarely, if ever, go unsold. In 1978, all at the one time, he had exhibitions in Hong Kong, Grand Rapids, Michigan, and Sydney. He is a Prince Charles artist, which is to say that the Royals have bought his canvases. Prince Philip bought three of his paintings at an exhibition in 1973 and Australia's union chief, Bob Hawke, is a fan. He bought a painting for $1100.

Pro Hart is represented in most Australian galleries and he has had shows in New York, Los Angeles, London, Singapore and Tokyo.

Pro Hart has been called the heavyweight champion of the naive painters. He has been spurned consistently and viciously by the critics, but the racketeers continue to pay him the ultimate compliment: the Pro Hart forgers are at work from here to Hong Kong.

There is not the faintest sense of arrogance or self-satisfaction about Pro Hart. At first sight he seems an extraordinarily simple, uncomplicated character. One writer suggested that he looks like the president of the local Rotary club on his day off.

Pro Hart is short, he wears big horn-rimmed glasses and he is distinctly tubby about the middle. He wears thongs, a T-shirt or a windcheater, plus pale cotton pants. As he paints he wipes his hands or his brushes on his stomach and occasionally on the side of his pants. So always there is a brilliant abstract Pro Hart across his stomach with lesser Pro Hart originals on his thighs.

He is renowned for his enthusiasms, or should one say, his passions. They cover motor cycles, religion, weight lifting, playing the organ, pistols, cameras, sculpture, oxy-acetylene torches, inventions and gadgetry of all kinds, anti-communism and talking.

It would be a great mistake to underestimate the importance of Broken Hill in the creation of Pro Hart. It is the bricks, the mortar and the roof. When you ask him why he lives there, he says, as if you had asked a particularly foolish question: "It's a great place. It's the centre of everything isn't it? Look it's got great bitumen roads. I can tear off to Melbourne, Sydney, Adelaide, to Brewarrina, back to Bourke, Wentworth, there are roads in all directions.

"Sometimes when I want to relax I throw my leg over the bike and I just ride off, go say 100 miles, straight road, no traffic, probably not a human being.

"The heat, no, that doesn't worry me. I like it. In the summer time your paint dries quicker. You mightn't understand, but it's half the problem in being an artist. While you're waiting for the paint to dry the inspiration's gone."

The enthusiast who seeks him out drives up Chloride Street until the bitumen runs out, turns left at the water tank on the top of the hill, drives past the splendid mansion once occupied by the millionaire grazier, Sir Sidney Kidman, and there it is, the Pro Hart Gallery.

Walk inside and the garden is a dashing extrovert clutter, every item a symbol of the enthusiastic Pro life. Oh, there are sculptures everywhere. One is four or five metres high and composed of steel boxes. It is called Metal Crystals. But he has a sense of humor, this man. On the left is an irresistible thing composed of antique fire alarm boxes and he has welded a garden tap on the side. Rising out of the earth there are very odd misshapen bits of metal. There's a story here — Pro Hart is always experimenting with new art forms. On this occasion he took some metal sheets from car bodies, placed them on a mine site and blew them up with gelignite. He was thrilled with the abstract shapes that came out of the blast.

On the right is the Pro Hart dwelling. Here he lives with his wife Raelee, his three sons and two daughters. It is white and classic Broken Hill, acres of galvanised iron, which in the desert dryness is almost rust-proof. Straight in front is the Pro Hart Gallery, built of concrete blocks on three levels and emblazoned, almost like an ad for detergent, in enormous black letters, are the two words ... PRO HART.

It costs a dollar to go in and it is a very busy scene. You can buy copies of The Art of Pro Hart, by Eugene Lumbers, copies of the poems of Lawson or Paterson, illustrated by Pro Hart, or you can buy Pro Hart drawings or prints. In the midst of all this there are three organs; there's a large pipe organ down the back, but in the front just behind the counter is an electronic organ with a console imposing enough for the grand old days of the Regent Theatre or the Majestic. Actually it was used at the opening of the Sydney Opera House and it cost $35,000.

But it is the pictures that take the eye. This is a gallery that has less spare space than a flea market — pictures, pictures, pictures. He has more than 600 of them, but what a collection — Herman, Tucker, Perceval, Fairweather, Dickerson, Pugh, Looby, Heysen, Blackman, Nolan, Conder, Roberts, McInnes, Whiteley, Streeton, the whole tribe of Boyds, plus Chagall, Dali and Picasso.

How does one insure, how does one care for such a collection? Some years back he did try to insure them all for a million dollars, but the rates were too expensive. He has a complicated series of burglar alarms and a panel of lights in his bedroom which gives him the security story in every corner of his gallery.

Interspersed, side by side with the odd Picasso, Tom Roberts or Arthur Boyd, there is a retrospective exhibition of Pro Hart going back to the 1950s — they are all there: grasshoppers, desert flowers, haunted Dobell-like miners deep in the underground, blue-grey Mallee scenes, those naive paintings of his such as the miner's wedding, full of boozy figures, an old pub and, of course, dogs, mean hungry dogs. You take it for granted that a Pro Hart dog is always lifting its leg. It is practically a signature.

There are pictures of dole queues, a deadbeat, and there is a large imposing painting by the door and every visitor ponders over this. It looks like a modern Tower of Babel, a very rickety Tower of Babel. It is still under construction and everywhere in this picture there are cardinals, bishops, priests, grandiloquent church processions, the clergy looking very fine indeed, but at the same time ridiculous. The Pro Hart message is obvious: the traditional formal church is trying to build expensively into the sky, but what for?

"You don't need all that nonsense," according to Pro Hart. "You can find Jesus in a rubbish tip if you are prepared to look for him."

Another picture shows Christ crucified out among the red sand and the sun-blasted mallee. The message is just the same.

Pro Hart was born at Larloona on May 30, 1928 and he was christened Kevin Charles Hart. Raelee and sister Margaret both call him "Kev" but to everyone else he is Pro and Pro Hart is the signature on his paintings.

It is short for "Professor," the old-fashioned term for anyone who was slightly off-beat, ingenious or eccentric. He told John Lapsley of the Australian: "It goes back into the dark ages. When I was about 14, I think, it just started. You know, one of those things, a bit like calling Murphys 'Spud.' It was through inventing things. I used to work on explosives. I'd do anything that I thought I could sell — I was interested in making money but someone else always put it on the market first."

It is not easy to persuade Pro Hart to talk about his youth. For one thing he says he has a zero memory. "Shocking it is," he said. "I can't remember a name for two minutes. What's yours again?" For another he said: "Look, I'm not interested in the past or tradition. I'm a NOW man. What's going on this minute is of importance to me."

He does admit that his art, "as he calls it, started very early. Like many an outback bush boy his early schooling was by correspondence and his lessons came from Blackfriars School in Sydney. Eugene Lumbers writes that unasked he used to illustrate his correspondence lessons. Busily he drew everything in sight — the drunk sitting under a tree, a duckshooter stalking the edge of a swamp, the outback house ants and dragonflies with their diaphanous wings. The teachers were impressed. They asked Pro's mother to keep her son drawing and asked if they could keep the pictures for their records.

In 1941 Pro Hart's parents moved to Broken Hill so that they could tackle the problem of how to educate their children. Pro for three years went to the Marist Brothers College. Desperately he missed the kindly Blackfriars Correspondence School. He found the discipline of the Brothers tough, unbending and life was tedious. He even tried unsuccessfully to leave school under the legal age. He was so unhappy that for years he deserted both art and religion.

His first job, at the age of 15, was driving a baker's cart. He then did other things such as labouring at a cordial factory and working on the grape vines at Mildura.

At 19 the inevitable happened; like most young available men in Broken Hill, he went down the mines. "I worked in the mines 15 or 16 years. It was bloody terrible, mate, horrible. I hated every day of it. I was scared all the time I was there."

He told Eugene Lumbers: "I've worked with blokes who've been killed later. I've had lumps of ore fall off the roof and I've been hit with a piece of steel. I've run over gelignite with the train and it hasn't gone off. Miners used to amaze me. They have their smoke and go underground and walk around. They don't seem to worry they could get hit with a dirty big slab of rock or something. I'm damn sure I used to worry. My blooming oath I did. I used to show it. I'm sure the blokes I was working with didn't worry like I did."

All the years he was working underground Pro used to paint. He said: "I used to live opposite the hospital and sometimes my paintings would be hung over there. I never thought about making money out of it. My paintings would go for five bob.

"I remember back around 1955 I had never heard of Jackson Pollock, but I did a picture Pollock style, dropping paint on the ground where they have tea. I suppose some bugger has got it now.

"Oh yes, they'd rubbish you. They don't like a bloke to be different. They thought I was a bit of a twit, a crackpot. Of course, it was the thing at the mines when your shift was over to go to the pub. I didn't like doing that so I didn't fit in. I had a great time on art. The first painting I bought was a George Winnen. The second was a Sellars. It cost me 150 pounds, a fortune then, but I was single at the time.

"Next I bought three old masters, then I plunged. I bought a John Perceval for 350 pounds. You should have heard the howl. Fancy paying all that for a thought. The same old tugboat is now worth $30,000 or $40,000. People are easily led, they can't see in front of their noses."

Life changed very dramatically for Pro Hart in 1962. Kym Bonython, the Adelaide art director, jazz lover, racing car driver, was putting on a show of 60 paintings in a Broken Hill lubritorium. "I don't remember much about it now," said Pro. "All I can remember is that Kym had one of his underwater photography submarines on show. Isn't it funny? I can't remember anybody who was there but I can remember practically all the paintings."

None of Pro Hart's paintings were in that exhibition, but wandering around hotels and cafes Kym Bonython spotted Pro Hart after Pro Hart. He became fascinated and he knew that he had discovered a naive painter of considerable talent. Soon after he put on a one man Pro Hart exhibition in Adelaide and sold every picture.

This was the great beginning. There were exhibitions all around Australia. The days underground were over and he was a full-time artist. The paintings which sold in pubs for five shillings rose in value to over $3000.

When I found Pro he was busy on a large canvas which was to be the pub at Menindee. This was his new style, he said. He was laying on acrylic paint in big dobs. The colors were heavy and dark and the road outside the Menindee pub was like a great blue river.

"I didn't have any art training," he said. "An art school will only ruin someone with talent. All they can do is teach you how to mix paints. To me painting is a happening. It's a NOW thing that comes into my head. If I lie in bed at night and worry about it and work it out, then it's not good, hopeless. The natural inspiration has to flow. Sometimes I overwork my paintings. Then I've ruined them. I've thrown away over 200."

He had a color picture of the Menindee pub on his easel. "Yes, that's what I do. I take a color picture first. Well, why not make use of modern techniques? One time I used to make a sketch, now I take a photograph. The photo kills the natural inspiration in the process. I do better with what I've seen and what comes into my head. I do a bit of everything that comes into my mind."

Pro Hart has just had a "Balloon Happening." Only two days before he had taken his "Balloon Happening" for exhibition at the opening of a new Broken Hill gallery, the Frieburg. The "Balloon Happening" was Jackson Pollock in the grand style. He put three sheets of fibre board on the ground, six feet by four feet, then up he went in the balloon. We positioned ourselves between 60 and 100 feet then he had all the paint tins ready in the proper order. And he had the consistency of the paint just right for droppings.

"There were reds, blues, browns and I had a special tin of yellow at the finish to give a lovely sprinkle right across."

We went across to the Frieburg Gallery to inspect the finished work. It was vast and much more imposing than one expected. The yellow flecks of paint look like lights on an earth-seascape. Pro looked at it with beaming admiration, like Rolf Harris admiring British Paints. "Acrylic paint — that picture will last longer than the Pyramids."

He has another painting in mind which he will do on a majestic scale. He will put paint down on one of the outback saltpans. Maybe he will use a crop duster and as they fly across the white dry lake jets from the aircraft will release paint on the biggest canvas ever.

He is also looking for a sponsor for his bull ant. He wants to build a bull ant 15 metres high, something which will tower over Broken Hill. He has had the idea for five years and he is still working on it.

"I have built two models," he said, "one small, one about a metre or so high. I have had structure problems. When you build the thing 15 metres high in steel those legs aren't strong enough to take the weight. I think I've overcome it. I'll put the tail on the ground and I may put a support under the centre. I want it to be safe enough for kids to climb on. I intend to build it all right — I might even pay for it myself."

He has studios all around the back of his gallery. They are an amazing clutter of bits and pieces — tubes of paint, brushes, cameras, tripods, a Honda lighting set, oxy-acetylene equipment, a radio-controlled model aeroplane, spare engine parts, and as we walk he insists that he is a very poor talker with nothing to say, but he talks all the time jumping from one subject to another. "See that screen there, the one with the hole in it. I love to get behind that and take pictures of little kids pushing their noses against the window, trying to see what I'm doing."

He interrupted that idea with; "Look at that!" He grabbed an imposing Nikon camera, ran outside, pointed the camera at the ground and started taking pictures.

As he came back he looked triumphant: "Marvellous. A circle of blowies. Look at them, see how their green backs shine in the sun. That'll make a good shot. I'll send it to the camera club."

Every so often he receives merciless treatment from art critics. A criticism, far from rare, appeared under the name of the Australian art critic: "He illustrates country folklore with a series of repetitious color schemes, syrupy and pretty, which are thinly washed over an 'ocker' drawn scene. Hart's technical handling of the paint and compositional keyboard is unerringly mechanical and excruciatingly academic." That criticism, on the other hand, is directly aimed at the city slicker's nostalgic view of the bush and his country brothers.

Does that sort of thing worry him?

"Yes, it used to worry me, but I find it doesn't make any difference what they say. I've just had an exhibition in Hong Kong and they gave me very good reviews there. Except one got my name wrong and called me Pop Hart.

"These critics think you should do the same paintings all the time in the same style as when you started. Can't bear anything different. I did a series on the Battle of Trafalgar. I got interested in that, went to England, inspected the Victory and did a lot of research.

"The point that moved me was this. None of the paintings that ever had been done of Trafalgar depicted the pain and the suffering of the sailors. That's what I wanted to bring out. Critics didn't like the shift of pace. 'Pro Hart is all at sea,' said one. 'He should stick to bull ants.' I thought it was my best series and I've sold all but one of 27 of them."

He talks a great deal about God, about Jesus. His faith constantly comes into the conversation. "People say I'm a man of many parts. I'm not really. I believe God gives every person a gift — it just has to come out. My gift is painting. My other gift perhaps is weight-lifting. I don't have any others. Motor cycle racing? No, I always come last. The organ? No, that's just playing on Saturday nights for my own amusement."

Pro said he had a surfeit of religion when he was a boy and lost his faith. Then started a search to find some meaning in life. He read philosophy, studied Buddhism. Then one day down the mines he had a revelation, a belief in Jesus.

"So I discovered Jesus. From that moment my entire life changed. Kym Bonython came to Broken Hill, all my pictures began selling."

Pro and his family belong to a group called the Christian Revival Crusade, a hearty outgoing church group, which believes utterly in the word of the Bible. Pro himself believes the signs are there, taken from the Bible, that the Second Coming is very near, that within a year or two Christ will return to earth.

Religion, he says, keeps him humble, stops him getting big-headed and one must give it credit for the disarming, remarkable self confidence the man has. He knows the inspiration will be there for the painting he will start tomorrow.

As I left him he was getting ready to go pistol shooting. "We do it on a range out in the Gash," he said. "I haven't shot with the club for months. We have a 50-metre range. You are supposed to do 40 shots in about two hours. I do mine in 10 minutes and I'm gone."

That's Pro Hart. He hates to waste a second, even when he's pistol shooting.
