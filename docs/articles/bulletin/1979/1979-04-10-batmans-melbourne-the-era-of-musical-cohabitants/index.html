The era of musical cohabitants

6 min read.

First published 10 Apr 1979

The cohabitation revolution has swept through Dunstan's social circle — from the Baptist mother making quilts for her daughter's new arrangement to Californian "palimony" cases — and he proposes the newspapers add Cohabitations and Dehabitations to their classified columns.

SOME friends of ours announced the engagement of their son last week. There was an engagement party and there was a formal "engagement" notice placed in The Age. We look forward to the wedding in July.

Everyone was delighted with this. It's jolly hard to get a wedding these days let alone an engagement, complete with party.

Cohabitation is the modern term for people who live together without actually being married. It is not long since even Time magazine struggled to find the proper euphemism. If Time wanted to imply that people were actually indulging in sex outside of holy wedlock then it said they were "great and good friends." That was the code; when Time used this, you knew what they meant.

There is no need for such niceties any more. Aren't you fascinated by the legal cases that have been taking place in California? The ramifications are immense. I've always found that most things which happen in California inevitably turn up here within two years.

In infinite detail our newspapers have been covering the Los Angeles case of Lee Marvin who is being sued by Michelle Triola who wants $1.8 million. They lived together as unmarried cohabitants.

Oh yes, in California cohabitants do have rights and more than a thousand cases have been fought. It is the new beautiful bonanza for the legal profession.

So I ask you what will happen here? We have a situation where almost an entire generation is cohabiting. This has come about only within the last six or seven years. I won't say it wasn't always there; the dramatic change is the public acceptance.

Just take the ladies of the Mothers' Union, the tennis group, the ladies who make the marmalade at the school fete. Back in 1970 or 71, if one of their daughters became a cohabitant they would have swooned out of their collective minds.

We had one respectable Baptist mother around the other day. Five years ago she would have pulled out a shotgun in fury. Her daughter has moved in with a Monash student, and glory be, she is rushing around with cakes and marmalade and she is even making a quilt for their double bed.

Then there's a local Anglican vicar. He has a son living in sin. Back in the 60s I doubt if he could have survived the shame and remained in the parish. Now he can chat about it openly and everyone feels he is a terribly up-to-date, with-it, vicar.

We know a lovely commune up in Carlton. There are five of them, two teachers, an artist, a nurse and a secretary. It is terribly hard to work out who cohabits with whom. They seem to move around.

But I do know that a lot of furniture came from Rosemary's mum. Ted's father footed the bill for the car. Charlie actually bought the fridge and the washing machine. Clare painted most of the pictures and George at least once cut the grass — once. And I've a feeling that when a group like this breaks up it is the crafty, thick-skinned ones who get off with most of the possessions.

A court case here under Californian law would be particularly interesting. Alice and Robin in Prahran are both on the dole, but Robin has been on the dole three years longer than Alice. If he walks out and leaves her is she entitled to half of those three year dole earnings?

Then there are a couple of gay professional gentlemen who live in South Yarra, tremendously skilled. One of them is a top doctor. If their beautiful relationship breaks up, does the offended party earn compensation?

I am not worried by the morals of all this, I am even rather envious. The problem is the difficulty of putting people in their right compartments any more. Once upon a time if we asked Reg to dinner there was a reasonable chance he would bring Angela. Or if we asked Maurie it was likely that he would bring Nancy. Not any more: 30 percent of our friends have had divorces and if they are under 30 they have changed girl friends at least three times. It is difficult enough to remember the names of your friends' wives and vice versa, without all this instability.

For some time now, without success, I have been trying to urge Melbourne editors to make some readjustments to the departments of their classified ads.

In view of what is happening to Marvin the possibilities are even more interesting. I would suggest that the categories now should be Births, Deaths, Engagements, Marriages, Cohabitations and Dehabitations.

Our Cohabitation and Dehabitation column would go something like this:

BILL BINKS who was living with Roma Pecksniff is now at Jill Rockingthwaite's house in Carlton.

ROSEMARY COMELY who was with Bill moved in with Jack Fotherington at Syndal.

CHARLIE WHISTLEWORTH has broken up with Lou Fosdick, of North Yarra, and has moved into Albert's flat at Camberdale.

HARRY MACY, formerly of Footscray, has been living temporarily with bus conductress Sally Fosgig in her North Fitzroy flat but indicates he will be there at least until November.

FRED HOTCHKISS is no longer with his former partner. The court granted an order divorce on Thursday. Fred is presently with Millicent Del. He may be contacted through his solicitor. Fred will marry Millicent Del by Saturday.

Don't you agree this column would be the best read in the newspaper, but more than that you could pin it on the refrigerator with one of those little magnets and at least there would be a reasonable chance of keeping track of who was cohabiting with whom.

The future though will be interesting. One of the competitive games at our place has been identifying the babies. Rival camps of in-laws always like to point out that the child definitely looks like Uncle George or Aunt Millie.

They won't be so sure from now on. You could see mother saying to her daughter. "Yes, I do recognise the little darling's nose. I really do. Let's see, Eileen, who was that nice fellow you were living with last November? You know, the fellow with the curly shoulder length hair in the Brunswick commune?"

"Oh Mother, it was ... ah ... oh dear, I can't remember his name."
