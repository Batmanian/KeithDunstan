Jerilderie gives Ned a fair go

7 min read.

First published 27 Feb 1979

Dunstan reports on the centenary celebrations at Jerilderie, the small New South Wales town Kelly held at gunpoint in 1879, where 1200 revellers danced until dawn and the people showed no self-doubt at all about honouring the outlaw who robbed their bank.

JUST LATELY I have been doing some research into Ned Kelly. The amount of material available is beyond belief, over 60 publications, including half a dozen full-length biographies, plus plays, novels, personal experiences, poems, full-length films, diatribes, symposiums ...

I am beginning to suspect that more has been written about Kelly even than Michelangelo or Napoleon. When you think of great Australian figures like Redmond Barry and George Higinbotham, who have been practically ignored, one feels sad.

The Ned Kelly buffs are fascinating people — their intimate knowledge of Kelly, Byrne, Sherritt and the whole tribe goes into such depth, you have only to toss in a word like Fitzpatrick or Skillion to start a magnificent full-blown debate.

The Jerilderie Letter is a priceless document of around 700 pages. When it was re-published by Heinemann about eight years ago it cost over $70. The dedicated Kelly buff knows it by heart. Some even remember the clause numbers and you can get a conversation — "It all comes out perfectly clearly in clause 1205." ... "Completely rebutted in 6304."

Naturally one could not ignore the centenary celebrations. The Mansfield centenary last October was positively pro-police, the Euroa centenary in December was a mixed affair, ridden with guilt. Was it right to celebrate the centenary of a bank robbery?

Ah, but Jerilderie had no self-doubts. Clearly you got the impression that Mr Kelly had honored their town by holding it at the point of a gun for nigh on three days and relieving the Bank of New South Wales of 2140 pounds.

The Royal Mail Hotel has a Ned Kelly Bar with a Kelly in armor at the front door. The radio station has a sign out on route 39 welcoming you to Kelly Country and the official Jerilderie sign, offering you welcome, has a Kelly hat on top.

There was no end to the souvenirs available, Kelly T-shirts, Kelly wine glasses, Kelly mugs, Kelly belts, Kelly posters, including a special Jerilderie job which depicted Ned with the message: "My Visit To Jerilderie Was Profitable — Good Luck To You, signed Ned."

You could buy special Kelly riesling, Kelly centenary port and even Kelly centenary Billabong Creek water. Yes, dinkum stuff out of the Billabong Creek, complete with brown sludge.

Alec Brierley, who recently did a little illustrated book on Kelly, was there, so too was Ian "Against the Wind" Jones, undoubtedly the greatest Kelly expert of all.

Ian, a great purist, was disappointed in the re-enactment. For example, Kelly paraded the street in armor. The armor was never worn until the last stand at Glenrowan. Then they dressed Constable Richards in the uniform of a 19th century soldier, when they had plenty of NSW Police trooper uniforms available.

If you are going to feel any warmth for Mr Kelly, Euroa and Jerilderie were the most charming episodes because he staged both robberies without a shot being fired. Those involved wrote nice things about him later, for example Scott, the bank manager at Euroa, and even Richards, the constable who was paraded through Jerilderie with a gun in his back.

The women particularly were captivated. Ian Jones pointed out that Devine, the policeman in charge at Jerilderie, pleaded with Kelly not to frighten his wife, because she was in an "interesting" condition. Thereupon Kelly made sure she didn't strain herself and lifted all heavy loads for her.

When Mrs Devine said she was in an awful predicament, she had to convert the court house into a church for morning Mass, Ned sent Joe Byrne to help her. Joe even arranged the flowers.

I would have liked to have seen on display at Jerilderie the famous Jerilderie Letter. Actually, it is an archival mystery as to the whereabouts of the original Jerilderie Letter. It is not in the Melbourne archives.

It is a full statement of the Kelly philosophy. It runs to 8300 words and one of Kelly's main objects in visiting Jerilderie was to get it published in the local newspaper. Gill, the newspaper proprietor, was in no position to receive the document. He was hiding, terrified, down in the local creek.

If for no other reason we should remember Jerilderie for this extraordinary piece of writing, which at times goes on in a vast unpunctuated James Joyce-like stream of consciousness. Take for example his comments on the police ... "I am reckoned a horrid brute because I had not been cowardly enough to lie down for them under such trying circumstances and insults to my people certainly their wives and children are to be pitied but they must remember those men came onto the bush with the intention of scattering pieces of me and my brother all over the bush and yet they know and acknowledge I have been wronged and my mother and four or five men lagged innocent and is my brothers and sisters and my mother not to be pitied also who has no alternative only to put up with the brutal and cowardly conduct of a parcel of big ugly fat-necked wombat headed big bellied magpie legged narrow-hipped splay-footed sons of Irish bailiffs or English landlords which is better known as officers of Justice or Victorian Police who some calls honest gentlemen but I would like to know what business an honest man would have in the police as it is an old saying that it takes a rogue to catch a rogue and a man that knows nothing about roguery would never enter the force ..."

It was century-hot in Jerilderie with the sun blazing out of a stainless blue sky. At night the air was still luke warm with a big moon in the sky. Ian Jones said, with satisfaction, it was a night just like this on February 8, 1879, when the Kelly Gang rode into town.

It was a brave venture by little Jerilderie, population 800, to turn on the big celebration. They had a Ned Kelly race meeting with every event named in honor of the original cast back in 1879. For example there was the Sgt Devine Maiden Handicap, in honor of the policeman whom Kelly locked in his own cell.

The only failure was the big shearing competition sponsored by Tooths Brewery. The shearers pulled out because of their strike, which seemed to me particularly pedantic. After all, Ned Kelly could have been a symbol of their struggle for better wages and conditions.

The climax was the centenary ball, held two miles out of town, on the site of the Woolpack Inn, the pub first visited by the Gang when they came up from Yarrawonga.

This was a beauty. It cost nigh on $12,000 to put on. There were four big marquees pitched together, two bands, unlimited supplies of beer and food for 1200 people. The citizens from all around came in period dress and the Kellys made a dramatic armed arrival at midnight. It was hardly according to history but it pleased the revellers.

It was one of those occasions that show the impressive stamina of people — they didn't arrive until almost midnight and they went through to the dawn.

They had to prepare for the great procession of 40 floats through Jerilderie. Most of them had Kelly themes.

For example, there was Joe Byrne cutting down a telegraph pole, to cut communications out of Jerilderie. Then there was Mr Tarleton, naked in his bath. Mr Tarleton, manager of the Bank of NSW, was indeed in his bath when Ned called at the bank.

Eddy Cully, president of the celebration committee, believes that towns like Jerilderie desperately need publicity to survive. He said Four Corners did a show on Jerilderie and literally "tipped the bucket on the place."

Oddly enough this had such a good effect it kept them going for two years. "If crook publicity keeps us going for two years," he said, "imagine what good publicity will do. I reckon it will keep us going for five."

That's the point, you see. He's hoping that from now on they will have Ned Kelly celebrations every two years.
