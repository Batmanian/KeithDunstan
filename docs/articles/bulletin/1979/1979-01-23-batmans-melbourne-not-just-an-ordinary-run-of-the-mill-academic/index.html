Not just an ordinary, run-of-the-mill academic

5 min read.

First published 23 Jan 1979

A tribute to Monash University historian Ian Turner, who died on Erith Island on 27 December 1978 — former communist, carriage cleaner, jazz lover, Richmond football fanatic, and one of the most gifted lecturers Australia produced.

THIRTY years ago our newspapers took obituaries most seriously. Indeed most newspapers had a permanent team of feature writers who did nothing else but write loving obituaries about people before they perished. These obits were kept warm and up-to-date in the office morgue, to be called out on the appropriate day.

Obits have gone out of fashion. Your demise are slim, unless of course, you are an absconded financier, a great artist or an absconded financier who was a great artist.

But it is important to say something about Ian Turner, Associate Professor of History at Monash University, who died of a heart attack last December 27 on Erith Island.

He was not an ordinary academic, he was a very gifted lecturer with a sense of humor. Monash used to have a magnificent team — Turner to teach the undergraduates and Geoffrey Serle for the post-graduates.

I first came across him when he was one of our more celebrated communists. He was working for the railways as a cleaner of railway carriages.

This was just after Cecil Sharpley's famous expose and there was no more infamous creature in the eyes of my newspaper than a communist.

An account of his rise as a young communist appears in the spring issue of the 1974 Overland. He grew up in Nhill, the son of a stock and station agent. He says the conversion to communism for a bush boy was the Depression and the Left Wing Book Club. One of the books that had an impression was John Strachey's Why You Should be a Socialist.

The war temporarily interrupted his academic career. Like Spike Milligan, his effect on Hitler was not over-damaging and at one stage in New Guinea he was demoted from lance corporal to private for insubordination.

He gained honours degrees in arts and law and meantime held minor office in the Communist Party. It was around 1950 that Ralph Gibson told him that he should get a job in the railways for industrial experience and he began work as a carriage cleaner at the West Melbourne depot.

He found it dull, repetitive and unrewarding. As he pointed out in Overland: "I flaunted an old army greatcoat and shabby clothes in a stupid and hurtful attempt, born of frustration, to convert myself into a proletarian by changing my external trappings. Once the word got around (spread by the Groupers in an attempt to isolate me) that I was the only double-degree carriage cleaner in the business, I was the object of some curiosity and suspicion."

Then there was the problem of interests. Turner's interests and those of his railway mates were rarely on the same plane. He confessed: "It sounds silly and snobbish, but I could not suppress a shudder when I heard a workmate describe a copy of Women's Weekly or People as a 'book.'"

Turner and his oldest friend Dr Stephen Murray-Smith left the Communist Party at the time of the Hungarian uprising. Murray-Smith resigned and the flamboyant Turner, of course, was expelled.

His range of friends particularly amongst artists, writers, poets and potters, was huge. Hal Porter on his Street, Richmond. Fred Williams, recalls his passion for football. "He was a fanatical Richmond follower. He went to every match, he really did. I used to go with him twice a year when Richmond played Collingwood."

Ian Turner's Ronald Barassi Memorial Lecture was justly famous. I went to three of them. He gave it every year, and like a performance of Hamlet it was always the same, except that perhaps he honed it and made it a little more beautiful every year.

And the audience grew. It started in a small lecture theatre but it became so popular with the students, the last time I attended it was in the Robert Blackwood Hall.

He would arrive always in a Richmond football sweater and wearing a Richmond beanie.

He married three times and his present wife Leonie Sandercock was one of his former students. They were both at Erith Island at the time of his death.

Erith is an uninhabited island of about three square miles out in Bass Strait. It reminded him of the Outer Hebrides. Stephen Murray-Smith first discovered it in 1962-63 and he and Ian Turner have been going down there for summer holidays ever since. It is a battle to get there, eight hours by launch from Port Albert.

In 1974 they took down Fred Williams and Clifton Pugh and both artists painted it. Fred Williams remembers there was just one building made of driftwood and stone. Legend had it that a refugee from Tasmania, who had run off with another man's wife, had built it 80 years or so before.

At Christmas on Erith Ian Turner celebrated the 12th anniversary of a severe heart attack. Two days later while playing cricket he fell over and died. He was 56 years old.

There was a memorial service for him at Springvale Crematorium. As requested, the music was by Jelly Roll Morton.
