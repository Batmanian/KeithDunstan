The art of being nostalgic

2 min read.

First published 01 Feb 1975

Keith Dunstan finds nostalgia in a Thomas Records shop selling old 78rpm discs, in Tarzan comic books illustrated by Burne Hogarth, and in a 1923 Foy and Gibson catalogue, and argues that in an anxious present the past has never seemed more attractive.

NOSTALGIA is the great modern commodity. Everyone seems to be selling it and everyone seems to be buying it. It has become more than a pastime; it has become an industry.

Thomas Records in Melbourne is one of the most remarkable shops in the city. It sells old records — 78s, the great heavy shellac discs of another era. Here you will find the Ink Spots, Vera Lynn, Joe Loss, Billy Cotton and the whole wonderful catalogue of popular music from the 1930s and 40s. You will find Bing Crosby in his prime, Rudy Vallee, the Andrews Sisters.

The shop is always full. There are old men who look for the records of their youth with the intensity of treasure hunters. There are young people there too, perhaps looking for the music of their parents or grandparents. There is something deeply touching about it.

The Tarzan comic books by Burne Hogarth are having a revival. Hogarth was the great illustrator of the Edgar Rice Burroughs hero. His Tarzan was magnificent — a figure of extraordinary power and grace, swinging through trees drawn with baroque splendour. The trees themselves were works of art. The villains were magnificently vile. The collection now costs serious money.

Then there was the Foy and Gibson catalogue of 1923. Someone found a complete copy. It was sent round for us to look at. Here was 1923 Australia in all its glory. Fashions, prices, values — everything that characterised an era. A woman's coat, beautifully illustrated, for 59 shillings and sixpence. A man's suit for four pounds ten. The prices suggest how modest salaries were then, but also how modest expenses were.

There is something very human about nostalgia. We know the present is good in many ways, but it is also anxious and uncertain. The past, even if it was not so wonderful when you were living in it, seems reassuringly clear and simple now.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The art of being nostalgic, The Bulletin, 1 February 1975. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
