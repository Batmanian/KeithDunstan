The Monopoly game

2 min read.

First published 14 Jun 1975

Keith Dunstan revisits the history of Monopoly, tracing the board game's invention by Charles Darrow during the Depression, while noting the unexpected cultural battleground between British Airways and Lufthansa whose advertising war echoes Monopoly's property-acquiring logic, and the export of the Australian television series Number 96 to Belgrade.

MONOPOLY is one of the great board games. It was invented by Charles Darrow during the Depression in the 1930s. He was an unemployed heating engineer in Philadelphia. He invented it, took it to Parker Brothers, and they declined it. He produced it himself, sold it, and eventually sold it to Parker Brothers for a large sum.

Darrow named his streets after Atlantic City, the New Jersey resort. Boardwalk and Park Place are Atlantic City streets. Mayfair and Piccadilly are London streets, introduced for the English edition. Melbourne has its own version with Collins Street, Bourke Street and so on. The game has been adapted for every country in which it is sold.

Monopoly has just experienced one of its great moments. British Airways and Lufthansa have been fighting an advertising war. Each is claiming to be the premier airline on the European routes. They have been buying full-page advertisements in newspapers of several countries. This is very much in the Monopoly spirit — buying properties, dominating the board.

Meanwhile, Number 96 is going to Belgrade. Number 96, the Australian television series, is to be shown in Yugoslavia. This is an interesting cultural export. The Yugoslavs presumably have no idea of the Australian social context — the flat, the neighbours, the particular Australian way of living — but the producers believe the universal human elements of the series will transcend this.

The game of Monopoly has always had a cultural adaptation problem. When the American streets are replaced with local streets the game works. When American cultural products are exported intact they sometimes don't. Number 96 in Belgrade will be an interesting test.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The Monopoly game, The Bulletin, 14 June 1975. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
