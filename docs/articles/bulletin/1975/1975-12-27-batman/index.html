The best time

4 min read.

First published 27 Dec 1975

Keith Dunstan recalls his best times, the girls' school dance where he danced every dance with Priscilla, only to fail to recognise her at a ball twenty-five years later, alongside a Christmas Day 1950 flight with Prime Minister Robert Menzies from Singapore to Calcutta, where the BOAC steward announced that time had become irrelevant and proposed a Courvoisier before dinner.

THE BEST time ... ? I don't know, really. Memory is a strange emotional force — sometimes luxurious, often dangerous.

But ahh, let's see. Some years back my old boarding school went co-educational. It still seems something absolutely beyond belief. Imagine in those stark carpetless corridors, imagine in the quadrangle, in the swimming pool even, girls that you could actually see and touch. Impossible.

In my day it was as cruelly monastic as any institution in the land. The place was utterly female free. We were allowed to be visited by our parents three times a term and that was our only contact with other creatures — fleeting. I used to try and make up for it during the May and September holidays.

One's idea of heaven then was the Glaciarium. Girls were there, in short skirts and fast on their skates. But what chance did one have? The demons from Melbourne Grammar, Scotch, and even Xavier, swishing on their racing tubes, had the year-round advantage.

We were taught dancing. Miss Lascelles came to the school on Thursday evenings. She was solid and she had a booming voice. 'Slow, slow, quick, quick, slow.' We would all be stationed round in a big circle and we would do our slow, slow, quick, quick, slows, in unison.

The school leavers were allowed a school dance, with the female school leavers invited over from our sister school, of appropriate religion. The dance didn't take place in the school hall but in the dining hall. This was a softer building with leadlight windows.

I had every dance with Priscilla. Utterly the best time ever.

Then a nagging, awful fear crept into my stomach. Dare I kiss her goodbye? What if she said no and all was ruined? In the shadow by the door, I took Priscilla's hand. Noses got in the way. But there was a quick fiftieth-of-a-second kiss.

We corresponded every week. We exchanged school pins. I saw Priscilla once more at a picnic, then never again.

A quarter of a century later we were invited to a very illustrious ball. During supper a distinguished lady came up and said: 'Hello, Fred.' 'Uh... uh, hella—' 'FRED!!! I'm Priscilla.' My wife smiled and smiled all the way home in the car. 'You didn't even know her, did you?' she said.

ON THE regrettably infrequent occasions when I encounter Sir Robert Menzies he is liable to point a finger at me and inform anyone present that this McNicoll person led his daughter Heather astray by introducing her to claret and the popular dice game of craps.

Strangely enough, it's true. Claret and craps in a near-empty BOAC Constellation flying from Singapore to Calcutta — on Christmas Day, 1950. The PM was on his way to a Prime Ministers' Conference.

We piled aboard our BOAC plane about 7.30 am. Mr and Mrs Menzies, Heather Menzies, secretarial staff, a handful of Prime Minister's Department officials headed by Sir Frederick Shedden, and two Press representatives — about 12 in all.

The plane was festooned — a Christmas tree, stars, tinsel streamers, snowpowdered branches. As we took off, the cabin was a-ding with bells hanging from the empty luggage lockers.

We had been in the air about two hours, when an immaculate BOAC steward confronted Menzies.

'Prime Minister,' he said, 'we are aloft.'

'So I perceive,' said Menzies, giving the man a pretty keen look.

'Time has become irrelevant,' said the steward. 'It could be anytime.'

'So?' said the PM.

'What I am getting at, Prime Minister, is this,' said the steward. 'Who knows exactly where we are, or exactly what time it is? In other words, although back in Singapore it might well be only 9 am, in other parts of the world it is much later.'

'A fair assumption,' said the Prime Minister. 'Where does it leave us?'

'My proposal, sir, is this,' said the steward, 'that we pronounce festivities under way and have a Courvoisier before dinner.'

Mr Menzies looked at him for some time. Then muttering something about 'madness' he nodded — and drinks were served.

Christmas dinner came on an hour later. Delicious baked ham, turkey, mince pies, Christmas pudding, fruit salad, ice cream, fresh fruit. To the gurgling accompaniment of Montrachet Nuit St Georges, and Heidsieck champagne.

Menzies was kept cheerful by cabled progress of the second Test match taking place in Melbourne.

Looking back over a 25-year gap, I recall trying to converse with Anna Magnani in schoolboy French about 4 am at the Ambassador in Rome on New Year's Eve. It had been quite a festive season.
