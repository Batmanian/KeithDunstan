Men who cry

2 min read.

First published 11 Oct 1975

The emergence of emotional displays in public — Gary Dempsey crying on the football field, North Melbourne supporters weeping at their premiership victory, cricketers hugging each other — prompts Keith Dunstan to examine changing expectations of male emotional expression, contrasting this with Leonid Brezhnev kissing Henry Kissinger.

MEN cry in public now. This is new. Or rather it is not new — men have always cried — but it is newly permissible, newly acknowledged.

Gary Dempsey of North Melbourne cried on the football field. This was noted in the newspapers. Some years ago it would not have been reported. Now it was not only reported but discussed. Was this appropriate? Was it admirable? Was it a sign of sensitivity or weakness?

Lou Richards, the former Collingwood footballer turned television commentator, is known for his emotional nature. He has been known to cry. He is not ashamed of it. He cries because he cares.

North Melbourne won the VFL premiership. The scenes at the MCG were extraordinary. Players cried. Supporters cried. There were hugs and embraces. This was quite unlike the restrained manner in which Australian men were traditionally expected to receive good news.

The cricketers have also changed. When a wicket falls, the Australian team forms a cluster. There are hugs. There are arm-around-the-shoulder moments. The English cricket teams of the 1950s, by contrast, shook hands.

Leonid Brezhnev kissed Henry Kissinger on the lips and embraced him in a bear hug that lifted him off the ground. This caused considerable comment in the Western press. For Russians, apparently, this is normal. For Americans and Australians it is still remarkable.

These are changes in the culture of masculinity. Men are allowed to be more openly emotional than they were. Whether this is entirely good is an interesting question. But it is happening.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Men who cry, The Bulletin, 11 October 1975. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
