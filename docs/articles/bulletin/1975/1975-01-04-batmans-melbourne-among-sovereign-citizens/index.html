Among sovereign citizens

3 min read.

First published 04 Jan 1975

Keith Dunstan travels to the Hutt River Province, where former wheat farmer Leonard Casley has declared a principality on his 18,500-acre property, issuing passports, stamps and a national anthem, and profiting handsomely from the tourist trade.

THE Principality of Hutt River Province is one of the most remarkable sights in Australia and I believe the must, the absolute must, for the tourist this year. The Principality is situated on the North West Coastal Highway, about 300 miles north of Perth.

His Royal Highness Prince Leonard I is 50 years of age. He was Leonard Casley, a wheat farmer. Some years back he had a dispute with the Government over wheat quotas. The Government, he believed, was doing him in. So in 1970 he read the Colonial Laws Validity Act, the Treason Act and various other musty documents and decided there was only one thing to do. He seceded from the Commonwealth of Australia.

This he did by proclamation. He declared that the Hutt River Province was an independent nation with himself as its ruler. He has 18,500 acres and a population of 20 — family and farm hands. Australia now contains two independent nations, the Commonwealth of Australia and the Principality of Hutt River Province.

This is not a joke. The Prince has passports. He has postage stamps. He has a national anthem. He has his own flag. He has a Privy Council, a Cabinet and a Chamber of Deputies. The Governor-General of Australia acknowledges the existence of the Province. The Prince issues travel documents, charges fees for entry, and the whole operation is a going commercial concern.

The tourist trade is particularly good. Up to 1,000 people a week pass through the Province. They buy stamps and visit the Province museum. Each visitor is greeted and stamped into the Province by the Prince or one of his officials, who include his wife, Shirley.

The road to the Province is a good bitumen road. When you arrive there is a sign saying 'Entering the Principality of Hutt River Province, another country.' There is a flag flying. The buildings are of the utilitarian outback kind, surrounded by bush and wheat.

The Prince is a very courteous man, serious, sincere. He is convinced that he has acted entirely within the law. The Commonwealth, he says, could remove him any time by passing legislation in Parliament but, as he is doing no harm, there is a certain reluctance to do anything about it.

He says the Province has been visited by dignitaries from 25 different nations. He has exchanged letters with Prince Charles and Queen Elizabeth II and while they have not recognised his principality he is in correspondence with them. He shows you copies of these letters.

The Province has its own coins, struck in silver. The five-cent piece bears the face of the Prince. The Province has issued an enormous number of stamps to collectors all over the world and this, combined with the tourist trade, keeps the Province solvent.

It is all a very Australian sort of situation. Australia is a land of eccentric independence. We have always been a land where you do it yourself, do it differently and argue with authority. The Prince, however, is entirely serious. 'We are a nation,' he says. 'We will remain a nation.'

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Among sovereign citizens, The Bulletin, 4 January 1975. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
