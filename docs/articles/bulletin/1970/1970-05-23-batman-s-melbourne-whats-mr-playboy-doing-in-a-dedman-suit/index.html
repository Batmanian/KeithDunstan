What's Mr. Playboy Doing in a Dedman Suit?

5 min read.

First published 23 May 1970

Playboy's men's fashion director Robert L. Green visits Melbourne to show how the magazine covers male style, but arrives wearing a dull grey Dedman suit and a wide paisley tie, confirming that Australian fashion lags three years behind New York.

WAS IT the Duke of Bedford who said that the sign of good British tailoring was for a suit to look better after fifteen years than when it was new? I'm sure Mr. Harold Macmillan still has. He has revealed that some of his grouse-shooting suits date back to 1916. This was one of the beauties of pre-war living — the security of the tailoring. One needed only two suits, a grey flannel, a blue serge, and they were indestructible.

Since World War II, male fashions have been caught in an ever-tightening cycle, more devastating than the fashion cycle for motor-cars and equally as devastating as the female fashion cycle. Unquestionably there is a summit Men's Fashion Council with representatives from London, Paris, Rome and New York. One suspects that it meets every six months and conversations would go like this:

'Nah, Nah, Pierre, forget the stiletto toes. We're still on the square-toe kick for '71. We'll phase in the points for '72.'

'How about ze turn-ups, zen?'

'Right. Absolutely right. How about we phase in that rill...'

Every pair of bags in the country...

It was then that we were to be honoured with a visit by the men's fashion director of 'Playboy,' Mr. Robert L. Green. It has sometimes occurred to me that 'Playboy' had a men's fashion editor, and for the first time I went out to buy this magazine for the men's fashions.

Sure enough, on page 118, there was an article with pictures by Robert L. Green. There was a picture of a chap in a $315 sueded pigskin suit by Pierre Cardin, and the copy read: 'Next summer at St. Tropez and other strands in the South of France, expect casual wear to reflect an increased air of studied insouciance. Indian silk tunics, crushed velvet slacks and tight body shirts open to the waist will be the coming sun season's fashion fillips for the au courant sons of beaches over there.' Well, one could hardly wait for the arrival of Mr. Green to see the glory of his studied insouciance.

Back in '45-'46, when the Government was issuing goodbye indents from the park and elsewhere, there was a special goodbye indent from the Government. It was the latest version of the Dedman suit. It was a dull grey fine worsted. It had lapels like the spinnaker on an America's Cup yacht. It had no turn-ups on the flapping trousers. It was almost short enough to be a bum-freezer and it had a nipped-in waist. I don't know where he got it, but Robert L. Green was wearing that suit. Furthermore, he was wearing a black-and-white paisley tie, four inches across from east to west, and he had on a dull grey shirt.

'Are you wearing everything that is right now for New York?'

'Yes,' said he. 'Your fashions are about three years behind us. It takes that long for them to seep through. No harm in that, but that's the way it is.'

'You're not wearing any jewellery? No brilliantly coloured shirt?'

'No. The deep colours have gone. Subdued colours are back. I think in 1971 that plain white shirt will be top again.'

Mr. Green, 53, with longish grey hair, is a bachelor, and he is a very forthright man. 'The food in your hotels and restaurants is terrible, absolutely terrible. The worst in the world. I've never had such indigestion in my life. Nobody in New York knows anything about this place. All I knew was from the Expo in Montreal. I thought Australia was a sort of big Aboriginal camp, full of boomerangs, and all the women were like drovers' wives. But, look, the women are as beautiful and as fashionable as any in the world and your cities are so sophisticated.'

Somebody asked him what is the difference between a fashion director and a fashion editor. 'Sixty-four thousand dollars precisely,' said he.

He put on fashion shows in the Ford Pavilion at the Showground in Sydney and at the Masonic Hall in Melbourne. These were seen by 300 gentlemen of the trade in each city. The shows included a mixture of Australian and American fashions. None of the men's-wear gentlemen who saw either of these shows will ever forget them. On stage Mr. Green was a sort of combination Shelley Berman, Sammy Davis Jr. and Malcolm Muggeridge. He danced, pranced, cavorted, goose-stepped, all the time giving a marvellous running commentary.

He left us in Melbourne to go to Adelaide, and he left with little enthusiasm. Somebody had told him Adelaide wasn't very lively.

It all reminded one of a Green story about his visit to a small town in Texas. As he was leaving, a radio interviewer thrust the microphone under his mouth:

'Please, Mr. Green, can you give us just two words.'

'Two words?'

'Yes.'

'Are you sure?'

'Yes, only two words.'

'NEVER AGAIN.'

Source: Batman's Melbourne: What's Mr. Playboy Doing in a Dedman Suit?, The Bulletin, 23 May 1970. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
