Not So Much an Airport, More a Lovely Nightmare

5 min read.

First published 28 Nov 1970

Five months after opening, Tullamarine Airport receives a detailed architectural verdict, with the Victorian chapter of the Royal Australian Institute of Architects finding outstanding individual elements undermined by poor co-ordination, while the new TraveLodge tri-arc hotel next door already looks lonely in its green paddocks.

AS YOU KNOW, Tullamarine to Melbourne has been like the growing of a first beard, our symbol of greatness to show off our manliness to the world. The airport opened on July 1, so naturally you are eager to know how it is faring.

It is being used regularly by five airlines — BOAC, Alitalia, Qantas, Air New Zealand and Pan American. Four others have landing rights — Garuda, Air India, Cathay Pacific and Malaysia-Singapore. KLM and Lufthansa are in the process of negotiating.

There is an average of 64 to 66 arrivals and departures a week. So the eager rubber-neckers who look out the mauve-coloured windows are lucky to get ten take-offs a day. Last week there were 74 arrivals and departures. Sydney International Airport, on the other hand, is being used by 18 airlines and last week there were 347 arrivals and departures.

The domestic airlines move in next April and the Department of Civil Aviation believes the whole traffic situation will improve rapidly. One organisation that hopes so is TraveLodge. They've just opened what their chairman, Mr. Alan Greenway, described as a $3 million experiment — a 213-room hotel-motel at the gateway to the airport.

It is one of 12 built or being built by TraveLodge to the same design around the world. It is a nine-storey triangular tower with concave sides. It is very handsome. The conference rooms are decorated with Blackmans, Boyds and such. The Galaxy Bar, as dimly lit as outer space, has blow-ups provided by NASA. The décor is space-age enough to make guests' minds wonder whether to sleep on the beds or the floor. Then, of course, there are views out on all three sides.

Your correspondent asked a housekeeper whether she found the design exciting. 'Yes,' she said. 'It's like living in a triangle — charming, but it takes atoms before you find your sense of direction.'

The grand opening was performed by the Governor of Victoria, Sir Rohan Delacombe. The theory was that the Southern Command Band would play outside, and at the stroke of 11 a.m. two helicopters would land in front of the tri-arc and dramatically disgorge a bevy of air-hostesses from the airlines of the world.

Regrettably, it was a typical Melburnian spring day, not actually raining but the heavens constantly leaking. Two helicopter companies had been invited to participate and one refused to fly. So, not only did the ceremony have to be performed inside but some of our international air-hostesses arrived by taxi.

If all goes well at Tullamarine, TraveLodge will build tri-arcs at other airports around Australia, but at the moment, with green fields all around, this tri-arc looks terribly lonely. It is, in effect, a large city hotel built out in the paddocks, although no doubt there will be Sydney businessmen who will enjoy flying to Tullamarine, conducting all their business in front of Blackmans and Boyds and returning to Sydney without ever having to go to Melbourne.

Aesthetically, we have waited a long time for this. At last we have been able to persuade the Victorian chapter of the Royal Australian Institute of Architects to submit the new airport to the critical gaze of 'Architect.' The journal handed over the job to four architects and the most critical is David Watson. He says: 'Unfortunately, due perhaps to the organisational method of the Department of Civil Aviation acting as head client, co-ordination of various designers' skills was virtually non-existent, resulting in a number of powerful over-design elements all competing for first place in the spectator's eye. The individual designers must have worked in isolation one from another, and this is clear in three outstanding cases.

'There is the famous and, in my opinion, very nasty ceramic wall by Tom Sanders, pierced by two doors which, by careful planning, could have been located elsewhere. There is the elegant copper-ceiling cocktail lounge, carefully nurtured by George Kral, destroyed by the clumsy hand which decided to give a touch of Toorak elegance by introducing those ugly, garish and altogether unnecessary table-lamps. Then, on the arrival floor there is the seating by Aristoc, upholstered in a purple too appalling even for the Funeral Directors' Guild. It is difficult not to be rude about the catastrophic colour scheme and furniture design of Sonia Preston's Aerojet restaurant and cocktail bar, which surely has reached the nadir of Australian design.'

The gift shops, too, selling the same tired koalas and papier-mache ducks, are transplants from Essendon Airport, squat, crude and hideous.

The architects are not unkind to the functional qualities of the building, although Neville Quarry doesn't enthuse over the art works. The pictures depicting the history of flight, he felt, were inspired by such films as 'Those Magnificent Men in Their Flying Machines.'

The freeway is splendid. The terminal is a good, if not a great, building; the interiors and landscaping are not too bad if not too good. The sadness is that a rare and wonderful opportunity to create a co-ordinated air-town on practically virgin country has been lost. The sadness is in the lack of conviction and direction and vision. The terminal building is already ringed by a haphazard scatter of appendages. The approach is dominated by an oatmeal America-design motel and white gimmick-centre.

'Only weeks after opening, Tullamarine begins to look almost as disordered as Essendon — and this happened under a planning control system as tight as any operating in Canberra or Moscow.'

Does this worry Melbourne? Probably not. It is still our best weekend sideshow, an attraction well in front of the Arts Centre, the MCG, Phar Lap, the crematorium and Chloe.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Not So Much an Airport, More a Lovely Nightmare, The Bulletin, 28 November 1970. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
