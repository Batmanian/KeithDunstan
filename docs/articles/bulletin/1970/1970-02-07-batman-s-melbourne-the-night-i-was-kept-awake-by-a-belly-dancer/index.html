The Night I Was Kept Awake by a Belly Dancer

6 min read.

First published 07 Feb 1970

A TV nightmare about automated television broadcasting leads into a genuine account of surviving an HSV-7 movie marathon from midnight to 8 a.m., taking in Humphrey Bogart, Mel Ferrer's possessed strangling hands, Modigliani's death scene, and the redeeming glory of Laurel and Hardy in "The Blockheads."

I HAVE JUST had an appalling nightmare. The time was mid-January and I was a panellist on one of the TV shows. The TV station could have been 0, 2, 7 or 9, but I had to be on air at 8 p.m. As soon as I turned into Television Court I sensed something was afoot. The building was all in darkness.

I went to the front door and it was locked. Not only that, but it had not been opened for some time. On the ground were piles of papers, letters and milk bottles. I pressed the door bell. No answer. I pressed and pressed. It was 7.55 p.m., long after the time for make-up. I began hammering on the glass and kicking at the door.

At last I could hear footsteps. The door opened and I could see a little old man in overalls. He was the caretaker, and he asked what was all the fuss.

I said I had to be on air in five minutes as panellist on 'Tell a Lie.'

'Don't be absurd,' he said, 'we are showing "The Best of Tell a Lie" tonight.'

'The channel hasn't gone out of business then?' 'No, mate,' he yelled. 'Come in and I'll show you what happens. Just a moment while I turn on a few lights.'

He explained that really there were no live shows between December 24 and the end of January, so there was no point in keeping on staff. 'But I was watching the show last night.'

'Simple,' he said. 'Come in here. What happens is this. I put a switch at 7 a.m. and all the commercials, re-runs, movies, etc., go through automatically.'

But what about announcements, news, the smiling faces of our favourite comperes?

The caretaker put on a resigned look as if he were dealing with a child. 'Mate, we're running "The Best of the Weather Girl." She's OK, because nobody ever listens to what she says. TV clock, Epilogue, the nice warm goodnights . . . all taped, and . . .'

Just as I was marvelling at this miracle of automation I woke up. I must have had this nightmare because I get to watch more TV than usual during the holidays. For example, the other night on ATV-0 I saw 'The Petrified Forest.' It was made in 1936. Bette Davis looked a dear, sweet, gorgeous little girl next door, about 16. Humphrey Bogart as Duke Mantee looked like a member of the Young Gangsters' League, maybe about 19, and the star, of course, was Leslie Howard. He was at his beautiful, soulful, romantic best. He was penniless, broke. He read François Villon and in the most glorious piece of passion he had Bogart shoot him — 'take my worthless life' — so that lovely young Bette could collect his life insurance. Beautiful, it was.

But it is a fascinating time. HSV-7 ran a movie marathon. Starting at midnight, they ran movies right through until 8 a.m., so there was the pure joy of never having to turn off one's TV. I made myself comfortable in bed with half a dozen pillows, put the TV set in position and arranged a bottle of scotch in a handy spot. Remember the Sultan Shariar who slaughtered his mistresses unless they could produce the right entertainment? HSV-7 was like the fair Scheherazade trying to do her stuff.

The first film was 'Balcony,' in which Shelley Winters was a madam in a brothel. Her clientele was never referred to as the customers, but as the 'patients.'

At 1.20 a.m. we had Mel Ferrer in 'The Hands of Orlac.' Ferrer was a great pianist who had his hands destroyed in an air crash. But wait, an extraordinary surgeon gave him a hands transplant and grafted on the hands of a convicted strangler, Louis Vasseur. You can imagine what happened. It was at 2.05 a.m., just when nice Mel was playing Rachmaninov, that his hands got out of control and he started to squeeze his potent thumbs on to the soft white neck of a beautiful blonde. As I reached for the bottle of scotch I could hear ambulance noises way off in the distance, and the house seemed very lonely.

At 3 a.m. we had the third film. There was no waiting, no compere. HSV didn't even tell us the time. We went straight into 'Foxhole in Cairo' with James Robertson Justice as a British intelligence chief. It was most interesting to note how much more intelligent a British intelligence chief was than the unfortunate Germans. He outwitted Field-Marshal Rommel and made the winning of the Battle of Alamein an easy affair.

There was also the belly dancer, Amina Bedr el-Estre, who, as a spy, took British officers into her bed and stole their plans. This film was made in 1961 and it was most intriguing to note the progress we have made in nine years. Luscious female spies when they were seducing British officers wore an awful lot of clothes in bed in 1961. Only Amina's nice tummy kept me awake.

We went on, on. At 4 a.m. there was 'Monparnasse 19' with Gerard Philipe, Anouk Aimee and Lilli Palmer, the story of Modigliani.

My worst time was 4.50 a.m. Modigliani had been wandering the streets wretchedly trying to sell portraits for five francs a time. Now he was lying on his bed dying of consumption and malnutrition. Anouk Aimee was sobbing and starving, Lilli Palmer was crying her eyes out. The only other sound was that of the milkman, and the first miserable light of dawn was coming through the window.

I think I would have expired like poor Modigliani except for one touch of sanity, a beaming young chap in big black spectacles who kept coming on talking about 'cared-for cars' and Holdens that were better than new.

At 6.05 a.m. we had an Abbott and Costello, a cultural death, far more horrifying than the death of Modigliani, and at 7.15 we had Laurel and Hardy in 'The Blockheads,' unquestionably the best film of the marathon. You may remember the opening. In 1918 Stan is fighting the war in the trenches. He is told to stand guard over the trench while his unit advances. Nobody ever tells him the war is over. In 1938 he is still there pacing up and down the trench while he sits down to lunch, opens a tin of bully beef, then tosses the empty can on to a pile about as high as the Arc de Triomphe.

At 8.20 a.m. I was in a daze, waiting for the lights to come on at the old South Yarra Saturday matinee. It cost 1s. 6d. and one bought a Violet Crumble and an Eskimo pie.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The Night I Was Kept Awake by a Belly Dancer, The Bulletin, 7 February 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
