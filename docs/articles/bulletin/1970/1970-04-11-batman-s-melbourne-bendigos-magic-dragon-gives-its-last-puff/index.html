Bendigo's Magic Dragon Gives Its Last Puff

6 min read.

First published 11 Apr 1970

The centenary of Bendigo's Easter Fair Parade brings two dragons into the streets, Old Loong making his final public appearance after nearly a century, and a magnificent new 200-foot dragon, Sun Loong, arriving from Hong Kong to take his place.

ACTUALLY, Bendigo is so perfect it should be preserved under glass. There's the dear, old, monstrous Shamrock Hotel. There's the marvellous Alexandra Fountain in the Mall. The main dish is supported by four early Victorian maidens, each about 16 stone. They are superbly topless and they have shoulders like Primo Carnera. Then there's Fortuna, the gargantuan mansion of the quartz king, George Lansell, who died in 1906 worth £3 million. As an architectural atrocity it even outranks Melbourne's own Federal Hotel.

But, like all architectural atrocities, give them enough time and they become lovable. All round the parapets it has dancing cupids, hunting Dianas, nymphs and even a fisher boy standing proudly aloft like Nelson on his column. There are hundreds of yards of balconies, an ornamental lake, an indoor swimming-pool and acres of gorgeous stained glass. One of the biggest stretches carries the important message: 'East west — home's best.' This was probably quite accurate in George Lansell's case.

Fortuna is now occupied by the Army Survey Corps and the commandant has his office right beside George Lansell's private bathroom. This is somewhat similar to the Baths of Caracalla. The bath is 4ft. deep, solid marble and the shower has a series of ingenious squirts designed to reach the most interesting places.

One must mention the Bendigo trams. They are smaller and much more charming than ours. What's more they have four Birney cars. Hundreds of Birney cars were made in the United States during the Depression. They are very small and designed for one-man operation. Bendigo has the last four in the world. So American tram-lovers come to Bendigo on special pilgrimages. They come on tours, they come alone. There was a purser of a Matson ship who flew to Melbourne, hired a car, drove to Bendigo and then chartered a Birney car just for himself. In his very own tram he toured Bendigo in bliss.

Then there's another point about Bendigo: it has Victoria's only genuine festival, the Easter Fair Parade, and this year was its 100th anniversary. There aren't many dragon cities around the world. There is Singapore, Macao, Hong Kong, and San Francisco. They don't carry dragons in China any more — it's considered a far too bourgeois practice. Ever since the goldrush Bendigo has had its Chinese population and it has possessed the world's oldest dragon, always awakened for the Easter Fair. Its age is uncertain, but it was on the streets in 1882 and it could have been around long before that. This dragon is called Old Loong. Over the years he has suffered from moult, arthritis and he has shrunk from a magnificent 150ft. to less than 100ft.

Last year five Bendigo businessmen got together and formed the Loong 100 Committee to bring out a new dragon for the centenary. They raised $13,000 and went to Lo On Kee, in Hong Kong, the world's only dragonmaker. Qantas flew out the new dragon in cartons, two and a half tons of it — 5000 scales, 65,000 mirrors and all sorts of extraordinary ingredients like pig skin. It demoralised the entire Customs Department. It took 30 days to assemble and when finished it was 200ft., a magnificent dragon.

Dragons are sensitive creatures. They have to be awakened with a great ceremony and put to sleep again in absolutely the correct manner. Old Loong, in deference to his age, had the honour of going first in the parade, and he was awakened on the Sunday. The other dragon cities had sent gifts and greetings in honour of the occasion. Macao sent a new banner, which failed to arrive in time, plus 100lb. of fireworks. In Melbourne the inspectors decided the fireworks were too powerful and Bendigo wasn't allowed to have them. So many Chinese came up from Melbourne, three Chinese restaurants there had to close down over Easter and the rest ran on a skeleton staff.

The weather on the Sunday was appalling, rain drenching down. The Loong Committee prepared two umbrellas of plastic, one 100ft. and the other 200ft. long, to protect their dragons. Yet just as Old Loong was awakened, incredibly the rain disappeared and the sun came out. Ron Wing, the official Chinese interpreter, explained it all. Dragons after their long sleep have a voracious appetite; they immediately swallowed all the rain.

The new dragon, Sun Loong, was awakened the next morning. James Lew, a 101-year-old Chinese, did the job by dotting his eyes. Traditionally one does this with a small brush, dotting each eye with chicken's blood.

The carrying of the dragons was a marathon operation, done by the Chinese, the YMCA and members of the Young Christian Workers. It needed about 40 people to handle each dragon. Sun Loong's head weighed 100lb. One man would carry this for about 30 yards, swaying, dancing, heaving. Then when he was near collapse he would hand it over to one of the six men on the head shift. Carrying the tail was even worse. What started as a peaceable movement became an unbelievable lash by the time it went through 200ft. So tail men were constantly sprinting from side to side of the street.

There is ceremony with dragons. They were kept formally apart until 1.30 p.m. on Easter Monday. Old Loong came first and made a circuit of the Alexandra Fountain. There were Chinese, dressed in ancient warrior style, on horseback. There were characters carrying fish, bees, crabs, birds. There were men in grinning masks who had gongs on bamboo poles. Then, to the beating of gongs and the firing of crackers, Sun Loong arrived.

Old Loong went around one side of the fountain, Sun Loong came around the other, so there was the confrontation, head to head and tail to tail. There was the moment of recognition and the ceremonial dance. Actually, they both came around the fountain too close and for a terrible moment there was a possibility of a collision — the world's first head-on dragon smash.

The next time they met was on the way home. Old Loong was going back into his lair. Dragons always go into their lairs backwards and only his head was now showing. The young dragon looked upon the old for the last time. It was sad, because Old Loong won't be awakened again. His fate is undecided. Probably he will go into a museum.
