The Way to Make a Million in Australiana

6 min read.

First published 02 May 1970

Melbourne goes mad for Australiana-themed restaurants and bars, with the Hotel Australia's Wool Shed, the Commodore Motel's Eureka Stockade complex, and the Branding Iron all opening within weeks of each other, leading Keith Dunstan to announce his own Instant Australiana Mart.

AS YOU KNOW, this page is always giving kindly advice to the Australian tourist industry. For years it has been suggesting the all-Australian restaurant. It would be run by a good shearers' cook known as the bait layer. His specialty would be damper cooked in a camp-oven and witchetty grubs done Dover style. On the menu there would be boiled mutton and white sauce, braised goat, and dessert would be steamed pudding with golden syrup. Drinks would be billy tea.

There would be sawdust on the floor, a little cattle grid at the door, and the tables would be covered with copies of the 'Weekly Times' and 'Pastoral Review.' On every table there would be a can of strawberry or apricot jam with the jagged edge of the lid bent back. There would be hessian on the walls, decorated with 'Women's Weekly' pictures of the Queen.

There would be Billy Tea drunk out of pint pots and cook would swing the billy at 1.30, 6.30, 7.30 and 8.30 daily. Entertainment would be a character playing a gumleaf and the girls waiting on the tables would be plump and beaut. They would wear no bras under their Jackie Howe singlets.

If you're still thinking of following this advice, you had better be warned. It has all been done. Suddenly this city has gone mad for Australiana.

Last week the Hotel Australia opened the Wool Shed right in the heart of Collins Street. The ceiling is cork, bark, and over the servery there is galvanised iron. The bar is like a wool press and the footrest is thick logs. There are long benches with tables just like wool-sorting tables. There are seats, too, like miniature wool bales. It is an air-conditioned woolshed, of course. And it is a woolshed with wall-to-wall carpet, of course.

Now wait a minute, we're just starting. The Commodore Motel people have bought Woolworths in Bourke Street for around $25 million and in a week or so they will open a joint called the Eureka Stockade. It will have one of Melbourne's first tavern licences. For a mob that has just rejected Courage and decided to use Carlton beer only, it is interesting to see them flying a flag of revolution. But that's by the way. This place is really going after the gravy trade.

Downstairs they have the Barbecue Pit. This is an enormous pit lined with bluestone blocks taken from Melbourne's gutters, and in the middle there is a roaring fire. Here you barbecue your own steak. Around the pit there is an old veranda complete with homestead-style veranda posts and weatherboards. Overhead there is utterly authentic galvanised iron, so true it almost brings a tear to the eye.

Next door is the Shearers' Shed, not to be confused with the Australia's Wool Shed — originally they both had the same name. This has rough hewn timber on the walls, bare beams overhead, wagon wheels, farmyard junk everywhere. What with the air-conditioning it is all a little too pure. I think they will have to bring down a bag of Mallee flies each day to give it the authentic atmosphere.

Then, a bit farther over is an exclusive little restaurant, the Lamb Inn. Actually the Lamb Inn was one of Melbourne's first pubs. It was on the site of Scott's and it used to be a fine place for brawls, duels, cock fights and its drinking hours were practically unlimited. I don't think Mr. George Frew, of Commodore, is planning quite that sort of thing. There are marble-top tables, red velvet seats, little canopies over each little table and lots of good panelling.

Up on the street level there are three bars — the Eureka Stockade, with a real stockade separating it from the Peter Lalor bar. One trusts that revolutionaries who will drink at the Eureka will not become too imbued with rebellious enthusiasm. One of the more exciting things they did at the original Eureka uprising was to burn down the Eureka Hotel. The other bar is the Bull and Mouth in honour of the Bull and Mouth pub which was on the site before.

Don't think I've finished yet. Opening this week, we have the Branding Iron, a restaurant above the Oriental Gourmet in Little Bourke Street. It is lit by hurricane lamps. They have thick pine tables, rough-hewn timber, ornamental posts for leaning against, aged weatherboards and, overhead, beloved galvanised iron rusted and holed with a beautiful authenticity. All over the place they have harness, saddles, horseshoes, old bits of pump, and lots of branding irons. One iron, by golly, is said to have come from the Overflow property of Clancy's day. Upstairs they have a genuine hayloft, where the customers will dance to Country and Western music. They also have a large blacksmith bellows. However, to complete the atmosphere, I think they need an air-conditioning machine set to blow in a nice, dry 110-degree north wind and a thin layer of Mallee dust.

Finally, for the grand-scale pub business, Commodore have more than half completed their new hotel, which they are building on the Ma Dalley junkyard site at North Melbourne. This is built around a courtyard and it is downright remarkable. The outside they are designing to look like an old Melbourne tenement, or, if you like, even a section of Paddington. Inside, the whole place is faced with bluestone taken from a demolished church. It has 240 units and the fancier suites will have genuine brass bedsteads. It will be called The Old Melbourne.

Now, don't you think all this is fascinating? Until six months ago we had to be the Miami-Las Vegas-Sunset Boulevard-Finger Lickin'-Melbourne. Now we have gone completely the other way.

Organisations like Commodore have their staffs combing the countryside for old harness and choice galvanised iron. As soon as Mr. Whelan puts up his board on an old building these gentlemen are standing outside, eager to make transplants, bidding for various sections of the cadaver.

Meanwhile, your correspondent is building a little factory out in Clayton. It is called the Instant Australiana Mart. We have our own ingenious aging processes. We will turn out on order, bark huts, weatherboard shot with borer, harness, horse collars, wagon wheels, steam-tractor parts, cast-iron lacework done in plastic, old water tanks, hurricane lamps suitably battered, ant heaps suitably consolidated and ready for restaurants. Furthermore, we will have artists working round the clock making nothing but antique bar-room mirrors. I expect to be a millionaire by Christmas.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The Way to Make a Million in Australiana, The Bulletin, 2 May 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
