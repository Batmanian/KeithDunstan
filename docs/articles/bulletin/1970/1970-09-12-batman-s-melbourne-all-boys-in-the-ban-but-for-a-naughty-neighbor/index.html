All Boys in the Ban, But for a Naughty Neighbor

5 min read.

First published 12 Sep 1970

Victoria's struggle to maintain public decency is undermined at every turn by South Australia, where Don Dunstan allows 'Portnoy's Complaint' to be sold, wears shorts to Parliament and presides over the longest drinking hours in Australia, prompting Keith Dunstan to quote at length a Victorian preacher's Victorian-era pamphlet on the perils of self-pollution.

IT IS NOT generally realised what appalling problems we have keeping Victoria decent. Councillor Duggan of St. Kilda demonstrates local purity by drinking seawater out of pewter pots, and the other day Sir Henry told us that the high crime-rate was due to NOT the permissive society and the activities of Dr. Cairns and Mr. Bob Hawke. Indeed, he has warned us off those idols of permissiveness, Sweden and Denmark, by sensibly describing them as 'nonentities as countries.' Some critics were sufficiently offensive to say that both these countries had a much lower crime-rate than our pure Victoria. Naturally we did not believe them.

Life would be easier for us but for our neighbours. We managed to clean up 'Boys in the Band,' deleting all four-letter words, and we managed to keep out 'Hair.' But what is the use when Sydney continues to stage the show. Harry M. Miller even has the effrontery to advertise it in Melbourne newspapers. Special 'Hair' excursion tickets are available and I understand at least 200,000 of us have seen it.

Well, we always knew Sydney was corrupt, but we used to have unshakeable faith in our other neighbour, South Australia. Under marvellous Sir Thomas Playford it was a wonderful, unshakeable State. I have watched the decline since his departure as Premier with a vengeance. They have barmaids, with a vengeance. In some hotels they even wear bikinis. They have a State-sponsored lottery, and not being satisfied with the abandonment of six o'clock closing, South Australia now has the longest drinking hours in Australia.

The Premier, Mr. Dunstan, appears to be completely permissive. I understand such things as Friday night shopping are on the books. He wears shorts to work, and word has reached us that he even proposes to allow this scanty dress in Parliament.

So, you see, our faith in the purity of South Australia was shaken, but what now? Just when we are trying our utmost to keep dirty books out of the hands of Victorians, this Mr. Dunstan permits South Australian adults to read a book called 'Portnoy's Complaint.' I haven't read this book, but it is dangerous. My old headmaster used to talk vigorously concerning the very same complaint which used to worry Mr. Portnoy. Persist in it for any length of time, he would say, and the most appalling things would befall us, making it quite impossible to pass exams.

Fortunately I still have a copy of the remarkable pamphlet by the great Victorian preacher, Henry Varley. He, of course, was unaware of Portnoy and never actually used the word 'complaint.' However, in 1893 he wrote 'A Private Address To Boys — And Youths — On An Important Subject.' It cost twopence.

He declared: 'The object aimed at in the following pages is the protection of the youth from the terrible ills that attach to the sin of self pollution. The moral and physical effects of this sin are appalling.

'Indigestion, bad temper, pain, drowsiness, and idleness are results distinctly traceable to this guilty practice. Large numbers of young fellows who have committed this sin in boyhood and who are now not more than 18 to 20 years old, constantly lose their strength during the night by what is known as "involuntary emissions." For this condition cure is exceedingly difficult. The memory becomes permanently impaired, mental indolence becomes chronic, often accompanied by a marked want of moral integrity in the character. It is only true to remark that more youths and young men have become insane through self pollution than from any other cause.'

Oh, what to do? Well, Henry Varley had the answers: 'Let every father and mother banish intoxicating drinks from their houses, and one of the chief incentives to lust and mental excitement. Simple and wholesome food, regular hours, and outdoor exercise such as cricket, tennis, boating, football and swimming are among the best preventatives against these vices.'

I have reproduced that section of Mr. Varley's important pamphlet just to give you some idea of the menace created by Portnoy.

Temporarily the crisis of innocent people buying the book in Melbourne book shops has passed. But what on earth are we to do about South Australia? Legally there is a problem. Mr. J. V. Dillon, our Under-Secretary, said a Victorian could go to Adelaide, buy the book there and actually bring it back. He is not permitted to show it to anyone in Victoria. But we have to consider that every one of us who goes to Adelaide and reads the book there must return.

The 'Age' made the suggestion that the only thing to do is to round up all those who have read the book. A good idea. Perhaps we could even have a Portnoy inspection gate. This may sound like a drastic step, but I believe it is the only answer to preserving our flinty purity. Meanwhile, I am taking long draughts of water, taking cold showers and indulging in long walks. I hope you do the same.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: All Boys in the Ban, But for a Naughty Neighbor, The Bulletin, 12 September 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
