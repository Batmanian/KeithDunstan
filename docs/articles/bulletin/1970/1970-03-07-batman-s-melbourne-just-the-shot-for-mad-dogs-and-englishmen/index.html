Just the Shot for Mad Dogs and Englishmen

5 min read.

First published 07 Mar 1970

A war-correspondent account of joining the opening day of the duck shooting season in the Riverina near Hay, where 100-plus camouflaged gunmen mobilise before dawn through swamp mud, with pre-battle keg, army-surplus logistics, and a dog so faithful it kept retrieving the one protected water hen someone shot by mistake.

FOR THE MAN who has survived his first duck season opening there should be a campaign medal, and for the scarred and the walking wounded a general issuing of Purple Hearts.

Your man went on a public relations venture, rather in the manner of a war correspondent in the field. The word was about even in early January that after the big wet the lakes and swamps of the Riverina were infested with duck, the best prospect in 20 years.

But it is very interesting. You have to go to the Riverina, somewhere near Hay. We received printed movement orders and we had to swear not to reveal the location of the property. All the way north via Kilmore, Bendigo, Echuca the road was bumper to bumper with superbly equipped vehicles and amphibious craft. Meanwhile, the innocent birdlife had no conception that this huge invading army was moving north.

The security system on these Riverina properties is remarkable. You go by invitation. We were told we had to be at the gate by 12 noon or it would be too late. This proved to be correct; precisely at noon the gate was chained shut and secured with three padlocks.

'We used to have an open go here,' said one property owner. 'Not any more. The bastards'll do anything. We've had cattle shot. We had a stallion killed with five bullets in it. We've had watertanks holed right through. One time we went out to drive the tractor, five minutes later it blew up. Yeah, bullets through the radiator and sump.'

Even then, resourceful green-beret types managed to get in. More than 100 shooters were in the camp. Immediately one noted that it was important to wear the right gear. Army disposals jungle greens and giggle hats were absolutely right. Waders were utterly out. For speed of movement through the swamp one wore ripple-soled gym sneakers. It was best to have the 'I've-suffered-the-green-hell' look. Many gunmen were bearded. I was impressed. They looked like Fidel Castro guerillas with all the courageous outdoorsmanship of a Marlboro advertisement.

Pre-battle equipment also was important. There were large caravans, beautiful tents. One gentleman had an engine-operated deep-freeze on the back of his vehicle. Three hydroplanes were available and the duck hunters had acquired several small outboard motors. Two of them had large Volkswagen motors and the third was magnificently equipped with a Porsche. Their great advantage was that in draft they could virtually skim on wet mud. They kept the duck aloft and circulating.

At 8.30 p.m. there was an assembly of all shooters for an address by the owner. Here there was atmosphere, gum trees, the river nearby and a gun-shot through the trees, with barks and rumoured squeals. There were noises like a harangue before Agincourt, and we had the feeling that Englishmen, still comfortable in their beds, would be accurs'd that they were not here this day.

The owner said we would rise at 4 a.m., leave the camp at 5 a.m. His car would be the first out and not a shot would be fired until 6 a.m., sunrise. Again the organisation was remarkable. On the back of a truck was an 18-gallon keg, complete with pluto taps.

At 4 a.m. alarms went off, the noise searing through 100 hangovers. At five precisely, according to the movement order, a long crocodile of cars moved out toward the swamp. At 5.25 just as one was slopping through the mud, feeling accursed that this day one wasn't in a comfortable bed, the explosion started way ahead of schedule. This was awe-inspiring. The noise was comparable to the Battle of Ypres. 'Run. Run,' shouted the commander, 'we are already five minutes too late.' We ran, ran, for what seemed half a mile, until at 5.40 I was standing out in the middle of swamp with frigid mud up to my knees. It was deathly cold. The scene was beautiful, the water scarlet with the first light of dawn.

The sky was filled with the clatter and whirr of wings and the duck were dropping with a thwack on the water. The big swans came through low and straight, not even bothering to take deviating action. Swans know they are protected, sort of noncombatants under the International Red Cross agreement.

Your correspondent actually shot at duck and in the excitement of the moment, laden with bandoliers, 200 rounds, duck holders, mosquito repellent and gun, there was the awful danger of never again being able to rise out of the ooze. 'Don't worry about that,' roared the commander, 'Blackies on your left. Fire.' Oh, I fired, but I had committed the sin of not having reloaded.

Of course, other people had dogs to fetch their duck, but then you must understand that on the first day dogs are unreliable because the gunfire unnerves them. However, one gunman became so frustrated with his dog he swam out and returned with the duck in his mouth to show the dog what to do. Another gentleman, in error, shot a water hen, a protected bird. Desperately he threw it away, but every time he did his faithful hound galloped back, water hen in mouth.

Yet the area lived up to expectation; clearly almost every shooter gained a full bag of ten duck, at an average cost of, say, around $10 each.

Your correspondent retired quietly. It was interesting to note that the local pub had a true shooting atmosphere. There was a sign in the bar:

WILL SWAP NAGGING WIFE FOR GOOD PIG DOG.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Just the Shot for Mad Dogs and Englishmen, The Bulletin, 7 March 1970. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
