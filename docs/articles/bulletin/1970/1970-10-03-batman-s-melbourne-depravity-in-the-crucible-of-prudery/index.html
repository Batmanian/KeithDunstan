Depravity in the Crucible of Prudery

5 min read.

First published 03 Oct 1970

A review of two recently translated nineteenth-century novels set in colonial Victoria, including the memoirs-turned-fiction of French courtesan Celeste de Chabrillan, whose lurid account of the Melbourne gold rush depicted the colony as a place of rape, murder and depravity.

SOMEHOW ONE can't help but feel that Melbourne lacks the glamour it had in the 19th century. A point often overlooked in our history books is that in the 1850s Melbourne attracted perhaps the two greatest courtesans of the 19th century, the Countess von Landsveld, better known as Lola Montez, and Countess Celeste de Chabrillan.

Celeste de Chabrillan was born in 1824. By 1840 she had acquired some very distinguished clients, and by 1844 she was the star of the Paris Hippodrome. Her nickname was La Mogador — a town in North Africa that had been bombarded by the French. To put it briefly, she fell in love with Lionel de Moreton de Chabrillan, who came to Melbourne in 1854 as a sort of French consul.

Regrettably, Celeste wrote her memoirs in five volumes before she departed and they were available in Melbourne. So the nice people of Melbourne left her off their invitation lists.

But our Countess de Chabrillan became famous as a novelist. She wrote three novels on Australia, including 'Les Voleurs D'Or.' Imagine one's excitement upon seeing that it has been published in English by Sun Books with an introduction by Professor A. R. Chisholm.

The blurb on the back of 'The Gold Robbers' makes it sound at least equal to 'Portnoy': 'Even before being translated into English, this outraged expose of Australian depravity at the time of the Victorian goldrush was written by ... the most notorious courtesan in France. Alexandre Dumas, reviewing it and a new novel called Madame Bovary for the Paris papers, raved that it kept him awake all night ... with murders, rape and a public execution, Victoria is hardly the place for a shy girl. But Celeste de Chabrillan has taken her revenge ...'

We are told that Celeste took her revenge against Australia with this book. Her opinion of the area around St. Kilda sounds curiously allied to that of Prince Charles: 'Round St. Kilda the vegetation was wretched; not a tree, not a blade of grass which did not look sick. This particular place where, on account of the gold which lay near, people had settled and built their town is the least attractive country in the whole State of Victoria.'

Her description of the Ballarat goldfields is splendid: 'Thousands of tents inhabited by groups of men, black and white, from all over the world; these immense holes in the ground from which people came out drunk with dirt, their hair dishevelled, digging all the earth with that fever which comes from the thirst for gold; here and there the carcasses of cattle or horses which were rotting on the ground. It looked like a vast cemetery with everyone digging his own grave; the sick men whose sufferings made them groan; the drunken men who sang; others that were found dead in their digs; the criminals who roamed round night and day; each evening a thousand explosions giving warning of armed men, each night crimes, thefts, fires which devoured great acres of virgin forest.'

Just like Alexandre Dumas, I found myself sitting up all night. I counted at least 11 murders, one attempted murder, four miserable deaths.

The unfortunate heroine of the story, Melida, is raped by the villain Max, who gives out vampirish expressions of hellish joy. Rape scenes in historic novels are a little different. Melida gets raped after she has fainted. Not until she becomes pregnant does she or the reader realise the awful thing that has befallen her. 'My God! Emerald, kill me, or I shall die of grief and shame ... I can only remember one thing, and that is when you arrived on that horrible night and the brute tried to kill you, I lost consciousness. Oh! she continued, hiding her face in her hands, 'I believe that when I was coming round, two hot, burning lips were pressed to mine and prevented me from breathing. I struggled. I regained consciousness only to continue the struggle. Why didn't that man kill me? Emerald, I want to die, I can't live, I can't carry within me a murderer's child. Emerald! I want to die.'

And she does, too, eventually, after witnessing the terrible death agonies of Max in a public execution outside the Melbourne Supreme Court.

If that isn't enough, Sun Books has also issued a companion title, 'Mistress Branican,' by Jules Verne. Here the plot concerns a Paris woman. A hitherto unknown novel by the author of 'Around the World in 80 Days,' 'Mistress Branican' opens in Paris. Jules Verne has concocted a wonderful mixture of melodrama, crazy natural history and genuine excitement set in the deserts of South and Western Australia.

The heroine sets off to the South Seas in search of her lost husband. Indeed, he is a prisoner of the ferocious cannibal tribe of the Indas, who live somewhere up in the Kimberleys. Mistress Branican purchases a few dozen camels and with a horde of weird friends, enemies-in-disguise, and Aboriginal troops (the Aborigines are the only people called Australian in the novel) strikes off to find her husband.

'Does she save him? Is she eaten by Australians (whose favourite dish is white talking)?'

So, you see this is a very exciting publishing time in Melbourne. What is more, they also translate La Mogador's other two Australian novels, 'Miss Pewel' and 'The Plum of Australia,' and we await them with enormous interest in Victorian depravity.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Depravity in the Crucible of Prudery, The Bulletin, 3 October 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
