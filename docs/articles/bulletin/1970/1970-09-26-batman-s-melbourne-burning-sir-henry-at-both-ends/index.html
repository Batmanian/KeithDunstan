Burning Sir Henry at Both Ends

5 min read.

First published 26 Sep 1970

Melbourne's 16 major electricity stoppages since May 1969 have turned candle-shopping into a civic pastime, with the most popular model being a 99-cent effigy of Sir Henry Bolte's head, while Keith Dunstan traces the city's industrial action tradition to the great Victorian Railways strike of May 1903.

AS I WRITE this, here I am surrounded by all those accoutrements of the civilised life. My Tilley lamp is primed and ready for use. After purchasing it I've had a kind of nostalgic feeling. What a boon they must have been before electricity.

We cook the dinner on our Tuttle Cobber barbecue range and after the chops are done we use the last coals to heat a bucket of water. This can be a little untidy for washing the whole family but, after all, we are all in the same boat, aren't we, even Sir Henry.

It is interesting to tour the shops. The other day Myer's looked so romantic, the entire lighting provided by gaslight. Then our local bank was lit by just one electric bulb. On this gloomy spring day one felt how difficult it was for bank robbers, one couldn't even see what one was taking.

The shops also are specialising in emergency gear. Some have a magnificent range of candles, including ones that will burn for 30 hours and candles in the shape of nudes which glow, in quite a remarkable manner. However, the most popular candles are designed in the shape of Sir Henry Bolte's head, with a wick on top. These are being sold all over Melbourne for 99 cents. For those who are a little fed up with strikes, apparently there is much satisfaction to be had in burning Sir Henry while being illuminated by him at the same time.

Impartial observers are irritated by this. They feel there should be a political choice in candles. After all, at this stage there are no union candles, and there are many who would rejoice at the opportunity of burning a Bob Hawke candle.

If you don't like candles, primus lamps are around $12.95 and for the same price there is a bottled gas lamp with a frying-pan attachment. You can alternate between being able to see and cooking your dinner.

Particularly fascinating, though, are the home-generating kits that are coming on the market. In the newspapers recently there was this ad which read: 'Get your own strike-proof, all-purpose generator. Provides 300 watts of electricity for five hours on one tank of petrol. Price: $196.' It didn't seem quite powerful enough for our nearest family, but one noted it was possible to buy a 2500-watt machine for $560 or a 6000-watt for $1,300.

Three years ago during the great drought many Melburnians were forced to sink their own wells and provide their own pumping gear for water. Isn't it interesting? Have your own well, have your own generator, have your own gas frying pan, your bicycle and you can be independent in this town.

But wait a moment, perhaps you haven't heard everything that is going on in Melbourne. This year we have had continual rail and electricity stoppages. Altogether we have had 16 major stoppages since May, 1969.

The Municipal Officers Association branch of the SEC Union has been satisfied with terms offered by the World Conciliation Commissioner, and Sir Henry has made a series of his classic quotes. Quote One: 'They can strike from now until doomsday. I won't make any difference.'

The electricity workers did just that. They accused Sir Henry of being provocative and staged a strike almost the next day. The electricity men threatened to cut off power to the Bolte farm which produced Quote Two: 'That's all right. I have plenty of candles.'

Then the railway guards, who have been involved in claims that have been going on for many months, produced Quote Three: 'Why have guards? It seems to me they only have a ride for nothing.'

At the time of writing we have an electricity stoppage of four hours proposed, and a complete stoppage for Show Day, 'September 24.' Last year special trains took 202,808 people to the Show. Even if Melburnians hadn't been on trains it would have made the news nationwide, the railway make news.

We have been so thoroughly warmed over lately that your correspondent was interested to discover when this great tradition started. The launching date was May 8, 1903, when, contrary to the understanding of the union's leadership, the men of the Trades Hall Council went on strike. The Government refused their claims and they went on strike.

The 'Argus' was stunned. It said: 'The news that the servants of the State employed on the Victorian Railways were actually on the eve of leaving their posts fell upon the city like a thunderclap. Like a spark on a train of powder the news travelled from mouth to mouth.'

The newspapers advised how people could get to the city — by horse, bicycle, travel down the Yarra by boat and perhaps even the new automobiles could tow people by trolley, locomotive-style. The 'Argus' solemnly advised: 'Thousands will improve their health by an enforced walk of a few miles in this bracing autumn weather.'

But in 1903 solidarity was poor. Not all the firemen went on strike. Strikebreakers, too, were hired. The 'Argus' praised the non-strikers and the strikebreakers as being heroes of the community. It even started a public fund so that they would be 'adequately rewarded.'

The strike collapsed on May 15, with the strikers accusing the Minister for Railways, Mr. Tommy Bent, of being 'insolent,' 'brusque,' and 'high-handed.' Apparently there were no Tommy Bent candles to burn.

Meanwhile, citizens of other cities think of us taking our bracing spring walks. We console ourselves with a line from Christopher Morley: 'Genius is an infinite capacity for catching trains.'

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Burning Sir Henry at Both Ends, The Bulletin, 26 September 1970. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
