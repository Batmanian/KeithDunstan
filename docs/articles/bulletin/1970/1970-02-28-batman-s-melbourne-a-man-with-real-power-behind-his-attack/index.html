A Man with Real Power Behind His Attack

5 min read.

First published 28 Feb 1970

A profile of William O. Bourke, managing director of Ford Motor Company of Australia, who has launched a public campaign blaming politicians rather than car makers for Australia's road toll, and who proposes a 10-point plan including better roads, tougher licensing, real punitive measures for drunk drivers, and the abolition of trams.

WILLIAM O. BOURKE is managing director of the Ford Motor Company of Australia. At 42 he has the overwhelming salesman smile of a Robert Young and the matinee good looks of a Robert Taylor. He has a gift for the spectacular. Last November he went to Vietnam to see his son. From Saigon he hitch-hiked in an air freighter to Da Nang, from there he did the next 20 miles by jeep. The remaining eight miles was Cong territory and mined. However, the resourceful Mr. Bourke conned a passing Vietnamese out of his Honda motorcycle for $10, pushed on and saw his son.

In Melbourne he has no need to ride a Honda. His personal stable consists of a Lincoln Continental Mk. III, a Capri GT and a Falcon GT special. 'The Lincoln,' he says, 'cost $17,000 and has just about everything, including hot and cold water, but I prefer the Falcon, it's a safer and more interesting car to drive.'

There is a great air scoop in the middle of the bonnet and it has, perhaps, the largest engine ever put into a production car in Australia. It is a 'Cobra Jet' 428 cubic inch with a horsepower rate of around 350 and a top speed of around 160.

It has power brakes, power steering, tyres wide enough for Mr. Brabham and all sorts of interesting gadgets. Bill Bourke says he puts all the new ideas into his own cars so that he can try them out before he gives them to the public — 'See, my sunroof. That's available now as an extra, I tried it out first' — and he goes to great pains to point out all the safety features in his car, even to the flat dashboard knobs which wouldn't penetrate one's skull like sharp toggle knobs.

Over the past fortnight he has started attacking in public. He said: 'I've kept quiet for two years. Now I've had enough. I'm sick and tired of politicians who don't know a God damn thing about road safety, blaming the road toll on the car makers. Do you reckon we don't care? Do you think we spend a hundred million dollars a year just to keep them alive?'

Mr. Bourke is very eloquent indeed. One suspects that in full cry he could even sell a semi-trailer to a housewife. At the suggestion that it seems odd for a man who drives a 160-mile-an-hour projectile to push road safety, he took off:

'That's an example of how the God damn politicians don't know what they're talking about. In Australia we have the highest death rate in the world. It is precisely twice the death rate of the United States, which has infinitely worse weather conditions than we have here. You want to tell me that Australian cars have anything like the power/weight ratio of American cars? There is an American authority, Professor Solomons, who has produced a paper which proves the better the power/weight ratio the greater the safety. It stands to reason. It's the car with go that can get out of trouble.'

What of the fast-driving young? 'That's another thing. Sure young guys in sports cars have accidents because they're inexperienced, but don't forget, cars are their lives, their recreation, they are on the roads three times more than older people.'

Bill Bourke has worked out a 10-point plan for improving our road safety: (1) Better roads. (2) More police. 'You can drive all weekend and not see one cop.' (3) More effective licensing and regular relicensing every five years as in other countries. 'Hell, I could be a drug addict and cripple with one arm, but there'd be nothing to stop me keeping my licence.' (4) Real punitive measures. 'Yair, 70 percent of road deaths are caused by drink. Our courts are soft, real soft. In the U.S. there are "Drunk drivers go to jail" signs all along the highways.' (5) Power in motor-cars, a help to safety not a hazard. (6) Give driving licences at 16. 'Here kids get their licence to drive and their licence to drink all on their 18th birthday. In the U.S. it's drive at 16 and drink at 21. In Australia 18 is that lovely time — birds, beer and motor-cars, all mixed.' (7) The give-way-to-the-right rule. 'Was there ever a greater menace? In the U.S. you give way on only unmarked intersections. Here anyone can barge straight into a main highway without looking. Yair, you pick the spots by the mounds of broken glass.' (8) Get rid of trams. (9) Improve the signs so people can understand them. (10) Don't go our own way on vehicle safety standards, but adopt plans which are standard in Canada, the U.S. and England.

Mr. Bourke is very, very eloquent on this last point. He said that in Australia there is an NIH syndrome, that is, 'Not Invented Here.' As from January, car manufacturers would be forced to include safety measures, 'no matter what the cost, no matter how ridiculous and . . . no matter what the duplication, without one thought to what they cost the public — and they cost the public $100 million.' 'The worst of them,' he said, 'is that a car must be crash-tested in Australia, regardless of the fact that hundreds of them have been crash-tested overseas. Say I bring out a special $17,000 Lincoln, the same as they sell in the U.S. I have to bring out two, so that I can crash one against a brick wall. Did you ever in your life hear anything so ridiculous?' No, never did.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: A Man with Real Power Behind His Attack, The Bulletin, 28 February 1970. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
