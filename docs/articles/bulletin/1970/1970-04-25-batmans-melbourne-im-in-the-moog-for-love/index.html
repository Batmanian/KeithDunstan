I'm in the Moog for Love

5 min read.

First published 25 Apr 1970

Melbourne jingle-maker Bruce Clarke demonstrates his newly acquired $12,000 Moog synthesiser, the electronic marvel capable of creating any sound on earth, from Arctic blizzards to canaries singing Twinkle Twinkle Little Star with an elastic band accompaniment.

THE INVITATION posed a series of important questions: Can a Moog Synthesiser create a violent thunderstorm with winds up to 100 m.p.h. and raindrops as big as marbles, inside a 10ft.-square room? Can a Moog Synthesiser create the sound of a canary singing 'Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star'? Answer: Yes. Actually the Moog can perform 'Twinkle, Little Star' with an elastic band accompaniment.

A Moog is the latest electronic marvel, a sort of horrid vocal computer. It was invented by Robert Moog, 35, a Cornell engineering Ph.D. It is a mixture of amplifiers, oscillators, mixers, filters and tape-recorders and it can create, electronically, any sound on earth. The most spectacular application of Moog was the electronic creation of 'Switched On Bach' which sold 150,000 LP copies in four months.

Now Bruce Clarke of Melbourne has bought a Moog. It cost him $12,000. Your correspondent has known Mr. Clarke for a long time. He runs an organisation with the extraordinary title Bruce Clarke's Jingle Workshop and in his time he has composed some of our most exciting and melodic singing commercials. Indeed, one can remember some deeply moving cigarette commercials, which must have cost nigh on $10,000, done to the full accompaniment of the Melbourne Symphony Orchestra.

He has been the power here behind the International Society of Contemporary Music. At one recent performance they did a number entitled 'Music for a Closed Piano.' Instead of playing it on the keyboard, the piano was kept closed and used like a drum. The resulting noise was most interesting.

Two years ago for the Adelaide Festival Mr. Clarke created a 93-minute electronic composition, 'Of Spiralling Why.' The score in traditional electronic language was 300ft. long. At times it had a sort of science-fiction animism, but often it was too chilling, like a psychotic computer going through the last stages of a mental trauma.

On the Tuesday morning we called at the Jingle Workshop. Moog was in one corner, looking rather like a control panel out of the Peter Sellers film 'How to Give Up Worrying and Learn to Love the Bomb.' There were thousands of lights, sockets, and things which looked like radar screens. On top of Moog was a silver candelabra. This was a nice touch. It seemed to indicate that deep down in Moog there was really a Liberace trying to get out.

Bruce Clarke apologised. He had possessed Moog for only a fortnight. It would take him years to exploit it to the full. It would be necessary to live with it for hours daily, practising to gain the virtuosity of the violinist or guitar player.

But we could see what he was up to. 'You're going to hear a lot of this machine within the next six months. It's going to take the advertising business by the ears.'

He explained how Moog could shape or colour any sound. The other day a client had been in. He wanted Arctic wind noise. It just so happened they did have an Arctic wind noise on tape, but the client didn't like it — not enough shrill wind. Well, it's kind of expensive going back to the Arctic for more 'shrill.' Then Bruce showed us how he could colour or blend Arctic noises. It could be delicate, a car noise on a wet pavement, a blinding, screaming blizzard, or he could reduce wind power to a jogger breathing his last, or cut his sound into bits. His Arctic winds suddenly could become crisp, eloquent little farts.

He gave us all the promised sounds, including the canary singing 'Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star' to the accompaniment of an elastic band. Personally, I felt the canary could have had just a little more soul.

Mr. Clarke insisted that Moog didn't destroy the need for existing musicians. One of the exciting things they could do was to put sounds through the machine and moogise them into something entirely different. A flute sound could be blended into anything from a grand organ to bongo drums. 'We could put through a Beethoven symphony and come out with something new. Completely different. Instant composition. No need to compose any more.'

One gentleman present was terribly shocked. 'I call this a new kind of pollution,' he said. But Bruce didn't see it that way. Obviously Ludwig van Beethoven needed freshening after all these years.

Bruce Clarke began to demonstrate the real application of Moog. Take radio time in the morning, he said. Your listener is wandering round the house, getting dressed or something, and the disc jockey is running on about this and that. Your client's commercial has to be pretty startling to cut through and be noticed.

He then gave a series of sounds all laid over each other but appallingly sharp: a man running, a Darwin deluge on a tin roof, the awful haunting cry of the sexually awakened crocodile. He demonstrated how sound could be given an utterly new brilliance and clarity. 'Moog,' he said, 'can give you four times as much information — and you will understand it — in that 25 seconds.'

Then he demonstrated a cigarette commercial that's 'all the rage in the U.S.' First the sound was shuttered, unintelligible, like a man with the most frightful stutter. Slowly the broken pieces of sound were brought together, until suddenly the whole commercial was brilliantly understandable and one thought of the pure beauty of hearing this commercial while groping for the toothpaste at 7.30 a.m.

Bruce was particularly keen on what Moog would do for electronic compositions. 'Using old circuits, I have hunted for eight hours just to get one sound. After working for eight hours, you're terrified of giving up all that work. "Sure, that's good," you try to tell yourself. With Moog you can get the sound you really want instantly. A piece of music which took me six months to create before, I can now create in a day. Say I wanted a melody of bursting car tyres, or poor drills, I could do that. Anything.'

As I went away I wondered how I could learn to give up worrying and learn to love Moog.
