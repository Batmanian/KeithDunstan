Down in the Depths About Going Up in the Air

5 min read.

First published 22 Aug 1970

A world tour of airports, culminating at Boeing's vast Everett factory outside Seattle, finds the jumbo jet age transforming air travel into an exhausting exercise in walking while making the aircraft themselves look like ocean liners coming in to berth.

YOUR CORRESPONDENT is just back after a prolonged walk through the world's airports. Often during sleepless nights looking down at my little red Qantas bootees I would meditate on the shape of things to come.

What would happen when there were 57s, 74s, 87s, when the behemoths would spew out their passengers at the rate of a thousand a time? It is not so much the aeroplanes as the airports.

Yes, it's the walking that kills you. Back in the Dreamtime there was a real thrill in flying. One would actually walk out in the cold air across a windswept tarmac and climb up stairs into one's aeroplane. Now everyone knows there is something psychologically nasty about flying. There may be a fellow with a bomb inside waiting to take you off to Cuba. So they try to kid you you're not actually flying at all. They walk you about half a mile, laden with overnight bags, etc., to a holding room where you go out through a finger into the aeroplane.

This finger thing is carpeted wall-to-wall and air conditioned, so that actually entering the aeroplane is like going from one air-conditioned room into another. The theory is that from the time you leave your air-conditioned hotel in New York until the time you reach your air-conditioned hotel in London you need never go out into the crude, untreated air.

Even so, once you're in that aeroplane, the door still closes with the same terrible finality, like the door of a refrigerator with you inside.

This jumbo jet produced by Messrs. Boeing is the cause of all the trouble. Right now nearly every airport in the world is in a mess, a horror of cranes, concrete trucks and extensions.

Both Heathrow and J. F. Kennedy Airports are vast, divided into suburbs, and even veteran taxi-drivers get lost within their precincts. One hears terrible stories about lost luggage at these places but, oddly enough, in 20 scary airport situations, not once did I lose a bag.

Like Tullamarine, all international airports now have moving belts, rollers, carousels and such to project luggage at high speed. They're monsters. I'm convinced that they are all equipped with punch hammers and razor-sharp knives. In five weeks my brand-new case was cut up like coleslaw.

With the volume of traffic we are now far beyond the point of return. It is impossible to visit any major airport now without seeing at least three 747s on the ground. At Heathrow you can see six, the property of BOAC, idle, deserted, because the pilots are demanding too much money to fly them. And what is that costing BOAC? $50,000 a day.

But it is fascinating to watch them come in. They are not noisy, and their extraordinary size seems to kill all concept of speed. As they make their final approach they look exactly like a large ship coming in to berth. One feels they ought to have tugs up there to guide them in.

The Boeing people say that after a time you forget this impression of size. They just look like the size aeroplanes ought to be, and craft like 727s look like mini-aeroplanes. Perhaps that time will come. At one airport I saw a twin-engined Piper Aztec parked virtually underneath the wing of a 747. It looked such a dear little thing, as if it were nosing up for a nipple.

But if you think the 747 is large then it is worth a visit to the place where they're being made at Everett, 45 miles out of Seattle. Boeing have spent $700 million on this project alone and they have their own mighty airstrip. When I arrived there were 18 747s parked in two rows on the tarmac. Undoubtedly, it was the world's most imposing new-vehicle showroom. I was told: 'They're selling for $22 million each, but if you buy two I'm sure I could get the price down to $21 million.'

You get a little bored talking about the biggest and the heaviest, but the 747 factory, according to Boeing, is the biggest building in the world. It is 205 acres under one roof and you could fit two football grounds inside.

It gives an impact a little like the pyramids or Ayers Rock. It is a vast, white building with no windows. Inside there is only one floor and the ceiling is 115ft. up. But you can go up in a lift to an observation level and look at the astonishing sight of a production line of about 15 Boeings.

I found it all overpowering, the sort of feeling of awe one had after seeing Peter Sellers in 'How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Bomb.' The Boeing people, too, have yet to learn how to stop worrying.

They are extremely sensitive to all criticisms of the Boeing. At the moment they are doing well. Jumbos in service have carried their millionth passenger and they're operating at better than 90 percent capacity. The chief complaint at the moment is lack of toilets, only 13. Sometimes one has to travel 300 miles while waiting for an opportunity.

When I was at the factory there were 228 747s on order, and there were 200 on order. But what then? The Boeing 707, 727 and 737 production lines were almost done. They had closed out on the other Boeing project. There was still no final assent to the SST projection and, meanwhile, Boeing had laid off 21,000 staff since January alone, giving Seattle the highest unemployment rate in the country.

So what would happen if the Boeing 747 went into a decline? No doubt the Egyptians had the same problem laying off staff after they finished the Cheops pyramid.
