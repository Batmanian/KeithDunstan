Batman at Botany Bay Where It Looked as if Cook Discovered a Used-Car Yard

6 min read.

First published 09 May 1970

Keith Dunstan travels to Sydney for the Captain Cook bicentenary celebrations, finding Kurnell choked with cars and plastic pennants, a re-enactment hampered by failed electronics, and the Aboriginal community at La Perouse watching the festivities with a complicated grief.

I TELL YOU the truth, I was rather inadequately briefed on how Sydney was behaving in regard to Captain James Cook. In Melbourne we did a few nice things. We had a 'Son et Lumiere' performance outside Captain Cook's Cottage, which some say Captain Cook had little to do with, and Sir Henry Bolte changed the name of Cape Everard to Cape Hicks, which one Cook expert insists neither Captain Cook nor Lieutenant Hicks ever saw.

We struck a medallion or two and the Courage Brewery produced a Captain Cook Commemorative Ale, said to be a beefy ale reminiscent of the beer undoubtedly loved by Captain Cook. The idea, of course, is not to open it; keep it on your sideboard and write letters about it to the 'Sun-News Pictorial' in 2020.

As for our decorations, we swapped over our Moomba play balls and draped from our tram wires Cook medallions the size of plastic rubbish-tin lids. And that was about it. I expected Sydney to be the same. It had never been an emotional city. It had never developed a passion for its Waratah Festival like our Melburnian devotion for Moomba.

There was a fine tailwind from Melbourne and we made the 550-odd miles in about 50 minutes. The six miles from the airport to George Street took one hour ten minutes. This gave ample time for conversation with the taxi-driver.

'What d'you think of the decorations? Bloody waste-a money. They coulda given the $110,000 to the spastic children. See all these banners. We call 'em beach towels. Four of them were knocked off yesterday. And you want to get on to the dirty big picture of Cook on the Woolworth building opposite the Town Hall. Somebody has painted a moustache on him. Bloody marvellous effort. How he got up there to do it, God alone knows.'

Actually, I rather liked the beach towels — there was a homogeneity about them, all through the city, and they were much better than one lot of our Moomba decorations, which an architect described as urban dermatitis.

Upon arriving at the hotel one discovered that New South Wales received a public holiday for Her Majesty or for Cook. However, one noted that the patriotic exercises were to be patriotic Rugby League football matches and patriotic races out at Randwick. The race club, too, expressed its feeling by having a Sir Joseph Banks Handicap and a Captain Cook Trial Handicap. Why, very decently, they even remembered Banks' assistant with a Daniel Solander Handicap. Nor did the honours stop there; the Wentworth dogs had a Captain Cook Memorial Cup.

By this time I had learned my lesson. I set out for Kurnell at dawn. Once again I noted every available road, school, bridge, football ground, etc., was named after Captain Cook. But unquestionably the finest piece of inspiration was the garage at Sans Souci with the sign out 'Captain Cook Car Wash.'

The road into Kurnell was blocked for a solid six miles and almost every 100 yards there were large signs: PENALTY FOR DROPPING LITTER — $100. One wondered whether these were prettier than empty beer cans.

Kurnell turned out to be an attractive little area. Sort of an Ocean Grove or a Rosebud. The local citizens were angry because there had been no grant for decorations, so they had festooned the area with tens of thousands of plastic pennants. The roads from the area were one big jam, so with all the cars and pennants flying everywhere one could have been anywhere from Kmart to a used-car yard.

There were some nice touches. The organising committee had tried hard to make the place 1770. In one area they had put down instant lawn. On the point there was a caravan as a changing room for the actors. This they had disguised with rustic pickets all around. And a special prize should go to the ABC. They shrouded their TV cameras with banksia. Sir Joseph would have been delighted.

The re-enactment was good, done with no overstatement. Occasionally, as he put telescope to eye, Cook looked as if he were posing for a five-shilling stamp or negotiating dry-cleaning services aboard the Endeavour, for those uniforms were an absolute credit after 638 days at sea.

At the beginning of this third century the highlight of the afternoon was the total failure of electronics. The crowd derived great pleasure from watching the agony of the sound engineers trying to fix the phones. The technical men fixed them in time for Queen Elizabeth's speech. Here the royal lady with millions watching her on television missed a gorgeous opportunity — a reference to the frailty of modern devices, even a little blow on the microphone as Mr. Askin had done, would have been enormously popular.

The celebrations have been a great help to many people. Every mobile pie, hot dog, and doughnut stall was at Kurnell. You could buy a plastic bust of Cook for $2. Two gentlemen from Melbourne were there, Don Turner and Ralph Naylor. They said they had followed the entire tour. They had sold 85,000 20-cent flags by the mark by the end of the tour. This was up on 1963 and they reported that patriotism was at a high level. Why, even in Canberra they sold 5500 flags.

The La Perouse Aborigines were the first black people I knew very much about as a child. They used to come into Maroubra, where I lived, to the pictures on Saturday nights — the boys jumping on and off the footboards of the trams and racing across the street to the box-office. They always sat downstairs, whether from choice or compulsion we didn't know.

La Perouse hasn't changed much since then. It still looks rather like a stretch of the Cornwall coast gone wrong, with the perpetual cry of seagulls, the Paragon Cafe on the jetty with the big Shelley's sign painted on one wall and the old gabled house on the hill, which used to be a cable station. The houses of the Aboriginal settlement haven't improved. The best are pastel fibro, the worst are shanties patched with corrugated iron, and the families who live in them say they can't renovate them because only the Department of Public Works has the right to do that. And it doesn't.

Like the rest of Australia's Aborigines, the people who live at La Perouse don't think the Bicentenary has much significance for them. They are struggling to make themselves heard on civil rights issues and there is a growing group of young people and age groups. At a meeting the day before, the Aboriginal rights movement is struggling to be recognised. La Perouse has begun to look more aware of itself in the past three years, though verbal promises have since been made to improve the settlement but it only has meaning if it's on paper.

The celebrations have been a great help. But as we looked across the bay from Kurnell at the place where the settlement stands, the Aborigines at La Perouse were among the mourners.
