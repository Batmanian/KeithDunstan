Now Is the Time for All Good Men to Come to the Aid of Their Trousers

5 min read.

First published 13 Jun 1970

Melbourne's male-only pub culture crumbles as the new Eureka Stockade tavern throws open all its bars to women, tracing a long history from colonial mateship and female shortages through to the last bastions at Flemington's Members Stand and the Melbourne Club.

THE FASCINATING news is that a new Melbourne tavern has thrown open all its bars to women. Very little else remains. Perhaps the only things left exclusively male are the public lavatories, and I don't see how these can last. It is well known what happens when males loiter in there. The only answer is the unisex lavatory. Already we are getting unisex changing rooms at some unisex stores.

Actually, the loathing of the female has a very long history in Australia. Partly it was the carrying on of an old English tradition, where even after dinner the females were dismissed so that men could converse alone. But in Australia we developed the idea. In the days of early migration and the goldrush there was the great female shortage, and so was born the famous cult of mateship and a feeling that the deepest bonds of friendship were one male for another.

Women, after all, were a different species. It was almost impossible to talk to them and it was better if they were separated at schools from a very early age. Pure bliss was the all-male haven and even at parties it was better if the dinner were placed at a strategic point so that males could gather around it and the women elsewhere. Indeed, beer helped. It was always considered rather 'not done' for women to drink beer. So we created our all-male havens with great care. The most famous of them is still the Members Stand at Flemington. There is a line on the pavement beyond which women must not pass. It is like the famous wartime cry of the French — 'Ils ne passeront pas.' It is always something to show the tourists. Visiting feature writers are fascinated.

But the haven of havens is the Melburnian pub, the one place males can escape from their wives. Why, we even frowned on barmaids in our hotels. We didn't go as far as the South Australians, who had a complete ban on them until 1967. We tried to get rid of them by allowing only those who were registered prior to April 1, 1920, to work in bars, or the immediate family of the licensee. It was felt by the Licensing Court that the wife, daughter or sister of the owner wouldn't have too much of a corrupting influence.

Until modern times, our pubs had a magnificent monastic quality, so monastic they didn't even have female lavatories. In the Royal Commission on Employees of Shops of 1883 this was actually cited as an excuse for the abolition of barmaids. A girl of 20 had died of 'haemorrhage of the stomach' brought on by constipation and long working hours. For periods up to eight days 'she had been unable to move her bowels.' There was no lavatory for women in the hotel.

Of course, it was social ruin for a lady even to be seen entering a hotel. Slowly this stain disappeared and we acquired 'the ladies' lounge.' Here a lady, properly escorted, could be taken for a drink, which would be paid for at a higher price than in the public bar. Even as late as last year the unescorted lady was something to be feared. When granting a licence for a new St. Kilda cabaret, Judge O'Driscoll warned the nominee: 'The commission will not tolerate women attending without an escort.' He talked, too, about girls with 'very doubtful objects' in hotel lounges.

The situation broke down steadily so that a couple of ladies could go into the ladies' lounge for a couple of gins, but until now the public bar has always been exclusively monastic. We haven't banned the girls by law, publicans have just enforced a rule, with the result that it has been impossible for a female to buy a drink at public-bar prices.

Now the whole system is crumbling. The Hotel Australia used to be the male stronghold, and one of the bars even had a famous campy atmosphere. That's finished, gone — three of their bars are unisex. Matilda's, Melbourne's first tavern licence, is unisex, except for one bar which is for males only and called the Club Bar. It is decorated with stags' heads, leather and wood panelling — no doubt suitable surrounds for club stories.

The real breakthrough is at the just-opened Eureka Stockade, Melbourne's second tavern licence. Here all the bars are open to women, escorted or unescorted, and the girls can buy their beer at public-bar prices. The only exclusive male area is the lavatory, and I understand even this is rather doubtful.

So what's left? There are two Club Grills — one at the Southern Cross and one at the Oriental — which are the only men-only lunches. Then there are the clubs, but even these are now all open to women for dinner, and at the Naval and Military Clubs you can even take in girls for afternoon tea. Then, when the Melbourne Club was severely damaged by fire, the ladies' clubs, including the Alexandra, offered their facilities.

Gentlemen, it is practically all over. James Laver, the famous expert on dress, has written that brevity in dress is usually a sign of economic instability. He also says that when women adopt masculine clothing, society is in a revolutionary period. So she is doing it. She is wearing the trouser suit. She is going into our pubs. She is challenging us in every walk of life. Frankly, I don't think one can look to the future with equanimity.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Now Is the Time for All Good Men to Come to the Aid of Their Trousers, The Bulletin, 13 June 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
