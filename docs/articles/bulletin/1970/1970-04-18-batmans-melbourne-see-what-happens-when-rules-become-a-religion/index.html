See What Happens When Rules Become a Religion

5 min read.

First published 18 Apr 1970

The opening of the VFL football season reveals a new industrial mood among players, with strikes at Collingwood and Essendon over pay, prompting speculation about a Footballers' Association of Victoria aligned with the ACTU.

THE MELBURNIAN football season has opened with almost frightening menace. I think it has all been symbolised in a moving article written by Mr. Bill Barrot, centreman for Richmond. He said: 'I don't think footballers should be married while they are playing football. I regard both football and marriage as full-time jobs. That is why, for me, the two do not mix.' Mr. Barrot then went on to tell of the arduousness of his occupation, and summed it all up with: 'It does not leave much time to be a husband and father, or even a steady boyfriend.' Don't you feel this is terribly interesting? On the one hand priests are leaving the Catholic Church to marry. On the other the Victorian Football League is creating its own celibate priests of football.

I point this out as an example of the passion: on the Thursday before the opening day, the 'Sun News-Pictorial' brought out a 12-page football supplement, the 'Herald' a footy booklet and free club badges. Television and radio coverage has been stepped up immensely, so now there is a remorseless blanket of footy through the week. Thursday we get the teams, Friday we have the previews. Saturday there are the replays. Sunday there is the Association football. Monday we have the post-mortems. Tuesday there is the live cover of the tribunals, the hearings of misdemeanours at Harrison House. Unless someone does a knee at training, Wednesday is usually free. In Melbourne it is the football lover's Sabbath.

However, we are fearful. You may have noticed that we played a VFL match at the MCG on a Sunday, before the royal family. In the past we have allowed only Association football, Soccer and cricket on Sundays. The TV commentators said 80,000 were present and the London 'Telegraph' said it was a crowd of 100,000 'demented with excitement,' but actually only 38,000 were there, a poor crowd for such an occasion. Some say it was because of the TV, but your correspondent knows the answer. Our football is a deeply mystical, religious experience and we don't like the service being interrupted. That is what happened on Sunday. But with the royal blessing, the precedent has been established and Sunday football is sure to come again, which means more previews, descriptions and post-mortems, so that eventually we will lose our football Sabbath on Wednesdays.

In the light of all this dedication, it has been a shock to discover that players themselves are not so influenced by the religious aspect. They seem to play the game for money. First we had Barry Cable, who came over from Perth to play for North Melbourne, reportedly on a $10,000 tax-free contract for one season. Then awful things began to happen. Two players from Collingwood — as you know, the most religious, the closest order of all — refused to play unless they received substantial increases. They were Des Tuddenham, the captain, and Len Thompson, their champion ruckman. Representations, you will be interested to hear, were made by their lawyer. Well, eventually, a whole new award was negotiated with Collingwood, which meant that players, depending on their level of service, could receive more per match.

The fearful cancer spread. There was a footballers' strike out at Essendon. Five of their top players, including the captain Don McKenzie, refused to play in a match against South Adelaide at Essendon. The 'Herald' reported that 'Essendon officials were stunned.' The strike continued all the week and they were still on strike for the opening match of the season against Carlton. Then, after three days of haggling, the Essendon committee finally agreed to substantial increases for the players.

Now we have a fascinating prospect. The strike weapon has come to Victorian football with brilliant success. Strikes are likely to be called now in other teams. They could march down Bourke Street carrying placards, and perhaps even hoot Federal Ministers as if they were a bunch of farmers. However, their organisation so far has been poor. They have made some effective forays, true, but five players going out on their own at Essendon did not show sufficient display of unity. Obviously they need to form a union. Your correspondent suggests that this should be the Footballers' Association of Victoria. FAV would need to be registered with the Trades Hall Council and Arbitration Commission.

Apart from the crude desire for more money, FAV could fight for better conditions generally. FAV would insist that footballers be allowed to marry and have free association with the opposite sex, perhaps even two or three at once. FAV would fight for shorter hours — only one training session a week, no running on Sundays, none of these 15-mile marathons and certainly no belting up and down sandhills at Portsea in mid-December. FAV would battle for shorter matches, a cut-down in playing time from 100 to 90 minutes.

Membership of FAV would be compulsory. All footballers-writers, commentators, photographers and telecasters would have to join. So when FAV called a strike there would be a complete black-out in the industry. But we needn't all go out every time; we could call out key men, say, all the umpires, or all the ruckmen and ball followers. We could learn from the postmen with a work-to-regulation strike — players obeying all the rules on the football field, not an ugly blow struck, would make a historic match.

Or how about a go-slow strike. Mr. Hawke, president of the Australian Council of Trade Unions, is looking for ways of strengthening his organisation. If he took FAV to his bosom, he would strengthen the ACTU beyond measure. Once he had a national football strike at his command, I reckon he could crush this Government in 24 hours.
