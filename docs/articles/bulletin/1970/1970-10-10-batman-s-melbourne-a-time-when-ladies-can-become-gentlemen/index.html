A Time When Ladies Can Become Gentlemen

6 min read.

First published 10 Oct 1970

The 1970 VFL Grand Final, won by Carlton, is examined through the flood of newspaper supplements, the record-breaking crowd of 121,696, scalpers charging up to $60 a seat, and the curious instance of men removing their make-up to gain admission through the MCG's ladies' reserve.

ALL THREE newspapers, the 'Sun,' the 'Age,' and the 'Herald,' produced lift-out souvenir supplements on the Monday after the Grand Final. The 'Herald' supplement was eight broadsheet pages in full colour. On the cover there was the formal team shot of the premiers, Carlton, all the chaps sitting there triumphant, each with his arms folded. Then inside were the details of every match played by every team in 1970, plus the vital statistics of the Grand Final. These statistics are astonishing. For example, you can look up Sergio Silvagni and discover that during the match he had nine kicks, he took one mark, had five handballs, and six free kicks were awarded against him.

I have taken all my supplements and put them in my camphor chest to be kept for ever, along with my Melbourne Centenary special issue and the special issues for Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth and Prince Philip and Her Majesty the Queen Mother.

One newspaper on the Saturday published 46 columns of football information. There was a time when your correspondent was terrified that the game was on the wane, but not so. This year it attracted its biggest final series crowds ever. How good it has been. When Peter Bedford was declared the winner of the Brownlow Medal, one writer estimated that he received precisely three times as much newspaper space as another Melburnian, Sir Macfarlane Burnet, when he won the Nobel Prize in 1960.

Emotion, too, was up this year. Take these quotes from the aftermath. 'Sunday Observer': 'Collingwood captain ... left the table and strode to the privacy of the showers. He was close to tears. Coach Bobby Rose lifted his head from his knees and tried to smile.'

Lou Richards in the 'Sun': 'Oh, the agony of it all. The pain. The humiliation, the frustration, the sheer, ultimate, unforgettable horror. Will Collingwood ever beat the pies again? I doubt it.'

The 'Age': 'Sad and lonely, Bob Rose left the coaches' bench and wandered toward the centre of the MCG. How desperately he wanted to be alone. Over by the fence, the Carlton officials hugged each other like all football club officials do after their side has won a premiership. Rose did not look back toward the Carlton camp, he kept on going. I don't think he wanted anyone to see his tears. Des Teddenham, standing near the centre, was the first to put his arm around Rose.'

The 'Sun': 'They came off the big arena on Saturday into sickening silence. Only the same old faces were there to meet them; a few of the balloons and streamers already had been pulled down. Shattered hopes, tears and flashes of anger took the place of the shouts and cheers, smiles and handclaps. A couple of them kicked the wall in disgust, others banged the doors of their lockers and slumped down on their benches to cry inside. Coach Bob Rose was last into the rooms and looked ashen-faced and heartbroken.'

John Sorell in the 'Herald': 'I sat next to Bob Santamaria during one of the Carlton final games this year. He is a fanatical supporter, and frequently called on the heavens for help. He was successful ... I watched the replay eight times at the weekend. I hope it is on again tonight.'

The Grand Final is an occasion which moves Melbourne deeply. For example, there was a wedding on the Saturday afternoon at St. Andrew's, Brighton, a cruel time for a wedding. But wait, we are told that the bride ordered their car so that it would reach the hotel by 11 p.m., in time for one of the editions of the TV match replay.

There were 121,696 people present to see Carlton win. Scalpers pulled up by the police were charging from $20 to $60 for a seat, and standing room was bringing $10. And here is a very interesting example of our devotion. Melbourne Cricket Club members are given either one or two ladies' tickets according to their seniority. On two separate occasions an interesting ceremony was noted in the ladies' reserve. 'Ladies' entered, sat down, then proceeded to remove make-up to reveal themselves as gentlemen. The feeling is that Ian Johnson, the MCG secretary, will have to introduce a sort of panic games sex test for all lady members.

You may think that with all this football we become apolitical at this time of year. We do not. Sir Robert Menzies is number one ticketholder at Carlton. Gough Whitlam has associations with both Collingwood and Geelong. But what about North Melbourne? Now there is a delicate point. Is the number one member John Grey Gorton or Arthur Augustus Calwell? Believe me, there is much kudos in being number one member. On the Sunday night after the Grand Final this matter was settled on Channel HSV7. Mr. Calwell said that the North Melbourne coach, Keith McKenzie, who had served with Mr. Gorton in World War II, had presented the Prime Minister with a North Melbourne guernsey and said: 'This makes you number one ticketholder.' Ah, but Mr. Calwell, former club president, said he was the number one ticketholder. Finally, Mr. Calwell explained the beautiful compromise. Mr. Calwell remained the number one ticketholder and Mr. Gorton was number one ticketholder of the complimentaries.

One football lover who has been trying to explain it all is Professor Ian Turner at Monash University. He can give interesting sexual explanations. He sees sexual significance in the way the players burst through a club-coloured screen of paper as they run out on the ground.

Last week he was quoting a number of eminent sociologists and psychologists. He gave Dr. Gilbert Bell, who said in 1911 that ball players were responding to the very stimulus which maddened uncounted generations of our ancestors over ages of paleolithic savagery and tribal warfare. He quoted A. A. Bell, who wrote in 1929 that all men have an aggressive component in their psyche and this is part of the fight for survival. Sport is perhaps a necessary sublimation of combative instincts.

'People,' he said, 'are in an unsatisfactory state of their lives. By participation in a real, dramatic event they transcend their psychological inadequacies by identification with their team. This way they get a feeling of belonging. As something which they don't get in their own life. Melbourne is more inwardly turned culturally than Sydney. So we need this thing to get rid of our aggressions.'

Just one thing is worrying. How are we to let loose during the long, dull summer?

Source: Batman's Melbourne: A Time When Ladies Can Become Gentlemen, The Bulletin, 10 October 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
