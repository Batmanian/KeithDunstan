A Sacred Day in a City of Light

5 min read.

First published 14 Nov 1970

The 1970 Melbourne Cup, won by Baghdad Note, becomes the occasion for a survey of the day's religious significance in Australian life, the government's hasty legislation after Myer tried to open its stores on Show Day, the Flemington fashion parade in what looked like Baghdad-inspired costume, and the strange segregation of the MCG Members' Stand replicated at VRC.

SOMEHOW IT has always been looked upon as the most sacred day in the Australian calendar. What other event brings the entire nation to solemn homage for three minutes? Yet the 1970 Cup has been attacked on all sides by cultural heretics.

The Rev. Alan Walker of Sydney's Central Methodist Mission stated that the Cup was an example of Australia's 'crazy values.' And he added: 'We can see clearly that Australia needs a new set of values. Nowhere else in the world are jockeys more honoured than musicians and scientists — and where else is a public holiday gazetted for a horse race?'

And that's another thing: the attacks made on the first Tuesday in November as a holy day. In the Senate, the Government Leader, Sir Kenneth Anderson, stunned the nation with the announcement: 'I propose sitting again after lunch. I understand that certain honourable Senators will not be in the Chamber for a short period this afternoon, but I certainly shall be.' This was ignoring the sacred event. Some outrage came from the Chamber as his former colleague in the Menzies era, earnest Member Mr. Paul Hasluck, complained about the indignity.

Mr. Menzies, who always had a sense of the fitness of things, smartly quashed that idea. Proper ceremonies were always observed while he was Prime Minister.

But the sacred day was undermined from an even more dangerous quarter — Bourke Street. In November, 1969, the Victorian Government legislated to take from wages boards the right to declare public holidays, and in so doing it left many a legal hole unplugged.

The Myer Emporium was alert. Why, they could even open on Christmas Day now if they wished. So they opened all their stores on Show Day — Northland, Southland, Eastland, Cloud Cuckooland and all the rest. The Minister for Labor and Industry, Mr. Vance, was shocked: 'Never in our wildest dreams did we expect retail traders to open on accepted public holidays.'

But don't you see the real danger? After this, all the stores were preparing to open on Cup Day. The Victorian Parliament hasn't done much in 1970, but this time it moved with Baghdad speed. Sir Henry Bolte said this was a day of national significance and the Government brought down new legislation to fix up Rafferty's Rules. So on Cup Day the banks, the butchers, the grocers, the post offices and the State schools were all closed. Although if anyone tortured your cat you could still call on the RSPCA.

Even then, the 'Age' did not show proper reverence. It said: 'The "Age" is willing to enter into the spirit of the thing of giving holidays at odd times for unimportant reasons. To show our good faith we offer two anniversaries listed by the British Central Office of Information: March 1, the date in 1924 when public vehicles were allowed to be driven in Hyde Park for the first time in 288 years, and March 7, when, in 1855, the first aerial action was carried out by the British Balloon Corps as it fought at Suakim to relieve Gordon. With just a little ingenuity we will complete another half-dozen holidays and research the whole year will be ours for the taking (off).'

However, there were many who saw the fitness of things. P & O brought the Iberia to Melbourne just for the occasion. It berthed at seven o'clock on Cup morning then returned to Sydney at 11 o'clock that night. It was a great comfort for the 1200 passengers to know that even if all hopes were mangled at Flemington their transport home was assured.

Then, starting the week before Cup Day this year, one of Melbourne's catering firms provided breakfast from 5.30 a.m. at Flemington, serving such delicacies as eggs, hamburgers, doughnuts and hot coffee. You must appreciate that the devout like to pay a very early obeisance at the track, where they gain a mystic inspiration from the dawn gallops.

This year, too, the City Council decreed that Melbourne would become a city of light, with all buildings illuminated for the occasion. Other cities do this for royal tours and passing royalty, not for horses. Melbourne, however, takes a broader view.

Naturally, your correspondent went to the Fashions on the Field. Your correspondent's friend had a brilliant run by having her picture in most newspapers. She wore a black shiny pantsuit with cap — ideal for jungle fighting and clearly inspired by the boys in Vietnam.

Georgia Gold, too, made a brilliant comeback in the midi-look, and the general agreement of the fashions was pants first by the length of a straight, midis second by a tail and minis third. But one feels that perhaps the men had the edge this year. Mike Treloar was in a lace-up sort of pioneer outfit with his trousers tucked into knee boots. With this he wore a large floppy hat. One felt he was about to discover America. Mr. Brian Lidgerwood was in a midi-coat which looked as if it was decorated with broad arrows, convict style. This theme was carried through to his matching flared trousers. Lloyd Webb, of South Yarra, was in a dark suede maxi-coat, similar to the gear the Duke of Wellington wore at Waterloo. Then, to go with his coat, Mr. Webb had a Greta Garbo-style floppy hat. He said his outfit had been handmade in Sydney. He found it hard to get good clothes in Melbourne.

So Baghdad Note won the Cup and many of the fashions could well have come from Baghdad. Your correspondent found it interesting to look up into the Members' Stand of the VRC. This seems to be the last Maginot Line that females must not pass. So up in one stand you see males all on one side, females on the other, like a typical Australian party. Then down below on the lawn, there are females again. And as they all stand, wearing their brand-new trousers, one wonders when they will really revolt.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: A Sacred Day in a City of Light, The Bulletin, 14 November 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
