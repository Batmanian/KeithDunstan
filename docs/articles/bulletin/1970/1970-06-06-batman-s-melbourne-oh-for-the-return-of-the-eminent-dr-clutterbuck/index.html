Oh, for the Return of the Eminent Dr. Clutterbuck

5 min read.

First published 06 Jun 1970

The decision to demolish the Regent Theatre and replace it with a 53-storey hotel and office tower signals the end of the Paris End of Collins Street, as the accelerating destruction of the city's nineteenth-century buildings proceeds from the Coates ice-cube tray to the Oriental Hotel.

GOD KNOWS who the romantic was that first talked about the Paris End of Collins Street. But Roland Pullen, an Australian journalist now resident in Paris, did something slightly peculiar the last time he was here. He wrote a piece calling Collins Street the 21st Arrondissement. There is a difference of course. In Collins Street they allow nothing on the boulevard stronger than weak tea and they snap shut their striped umbrellas as soon as the sun goes down. In the Champs Elysees life doesn't even start until after 8 o'clock.

Yet some of the old buildings like number 9 Collins Street haven't lost their charm. Tom Roberts, Longstaff, McCubbin and Septimus Power all had their studios there. Doctors, too, have always given Collins Street great dignity. I like the story of one of the first, Dr. Clutterbuck. He had a place just near the Melbourne Club. One Sunday morning in 1842 he was airing his dressing-gown in front of the fire when the whole place went up, plus the house next door, plus the two-storey house he was due to occupy. He had a beaut cellar and, according to Garryowen, this was invaded by a horde of intoxicated ruffians, who carried away all the drinkables and distributed them about the town. Next day police discovered a cache of stolen liquor in one of the gullies which are now the Fitzroy Gardens.

Yet while trees are sacrosanct, buildings are not. The deterioration of Collins Street over the past five years has gathered pace. The rot started with the erection of the Coates Building, a dull ice-cube tray at number 18. It proceeded further with the destruction of the Occidental Hotel to make way for the Reserve Bank at the corner of Russell and Collins. Then came the greatest vandal: the building that now rises lofty and hideous on the very spot where Tom Roberts, Longstaff and McCubbin had their studios, balconies all down the front like drawers that someone has forgotten to shove in, and all unrelieved brick down either side.

Maybe we haven't that much history, but such as we do have seemed to cling to Collins Street. It gathered style and grace like an old English manor. We would get very upset if anyone harmed the trees. The torture of a tree in Collins Street was almost sufficient to bring down a government, and when PMG men dug holes, newspapers would send up photographers to make sure not a root was harmed.

Ah, but the destruction is just beginning. Next year we will see the wrecking of the Oriental Hotel. The south side, right down to the corner of Exhibition Street, will be laid waste to make way for some monster of an office or hotel. It is clear that the 21st Arrondissement already should be called the Wall Street End of Collins Street.

You will notice that I have led you rather gently to the disagreeable news. For the past five years the City Council has been slowly creating a civic square opposite the Town Hall at the corner of Swanston and Collins. This is a good piece of foresight, for these times we need a comfortable place for protests. Well, the City Council went further. It decided it wanted a grand building to overlook the square. It bought the Regent Theatre for the sole purpose of pulling it down. This caused a cry of pain throughout the community. The Theatre Organ Society sent a petition signed by 2400 people describing the tearing down of the present civic culture as the cult of soulless economy.

The Regent Theatre is Australia's biggest theatre. It has 3253 seats, a splendid stage, good changing rooms, marvellous machinery and an absolutely incomparable supply of Corinthian and Ionic columns, Etruscan friezes, cupids, topless girls playing lutes, and scalloped burgundy curtains.

The Melbourne City Council has decided firmly that it will go and it has accepted the tender of Star Holdings Ltd. for a $40-million hotel and office tower which will go on the site. It will be 53 storeys, 650ft. high and work will begin next year.

One of the first things we were told was that the Star Holdings' building would be Australia's tallest, 42ft. higher than the Australia Square tower in Sydney. This crude announcement was embarrassing to your correspondent who had always hoped that Melbourne would have the lowest and most charming buildings in Australia. The building will house 5000 people and have parking space for 1000 cars, and one can only hope that there will be some brilliantly skilful underground arrangement for feeding the cars out between 5 p.m. and 6 p.m. without causing dementia at Swanston and Collins Streets.

There is considerable disagreement among architects and town planners. Some have a sense of proportion. What was not needed was a very large commercial building. J. A. Headley, a lecturer in surveying at the Royal Melbourne Institute of Technology, said: 'I have grave misgivings about planning a large building alongside St. Paul's Cathedral and the Town Hall. My theoretical view is that we shouldn't encourage these sort of monstrous office complexes.'

On the other hand, Peter Stoughton, a town planner of note, was excited by the prospect. He saw the building as a vast focal point for Melbourne and he regretted it wasn't 800ft. high.

But your correspondent sees it as the ultimate end of the 21st Arrondissement and only wishes that the ghost of Dr. Clutterbuck would return to leave his dressing-gown in front of the fire once again.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Oh, for the Return of the Eminent Dr. Clutterbuck, The Bulletin, 6 June 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
