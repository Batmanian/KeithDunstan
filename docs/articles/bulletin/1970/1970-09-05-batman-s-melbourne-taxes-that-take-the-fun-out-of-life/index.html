Taxes That Take the Fun Out of Life

6 min read.

First published 05 Sep 1970

The federal budget's new wine tax prompts an elegy for the great Melbourne tradition of home bottling, with Dan Murphy lamenting the death blow to a joyous, laughing industry, while Keith Dunstan proposes compensating taxes on yoghurt, rosehips and Coca-Cola to make the wowsers pay their share.

TWO WEEKS have passed since Budget night, but only now is our full grief becoming manifest. Actually, the most moving words came from Dan Murphy. The headline in the 'Age' read 'DEATH BLOW FROM TREASURY.' Then he said: 'If you should see me in the street during the next few days with a black armband, please do not ask me why. I shall tell you now. It is there to represent the death of my hope for a vigorous wine industry. This was killed by a foul blow from a group of economists sitting in the Treasurer's office in Canberra.

'... The industry from now on becomes the dark, soulless, tight-lipped business that typifies spirit distilling. Instead of welcoming smiles and the cheerful clink of glasses, we shall be greeted by doors with double locks, iron grilles, concrete walls and the jangle of keys hanging from the belt of the Customs officer.'

Comments have come in from all over. The shock at Jimmy Watson's in Carlton could hardly be underestimated. This was the home of the great tradition. One gentleman of the exclusive Watson's 'House of Lords' said: 'For the first time ever the price of a bottle of red has gone over a dollar.'

Indeed, it was true. What with the tax, two South Australian reds were selling for $1.05. John Brown, at his Milawa vineyard, was also perturbed. 'It's chaos here,' he said. 'I have to take stock of everything — everything in the cellar is frozen down to the last bottle. You may remember I had a little private cellar under my office where I used to keep some old bottles for myself. Even that comes into it. Do you realise I now have to pay tax to drink my own wine, whereas a farmer up the road gets off tax free if his consumption could produce 400 gallons?'

All the professionals are bitter about the 400-gallon allowance.

Already one of the greatest sports of all in Victoria is producing wine for your own consumption. Our citizens, Italians mostly, buy grapes at the Victoria Market and, according to the figures on grapes sold, they produce a million gallons a year. The professionals in Victoria produce only about 3,500,000 gallons.

Jack Ludbrook of the Wine and Brandy Producers' Association in South Australia was impressed by the 400-gallon largesse. He said: 'Four hundred gallons is roughly eight gallons a week or a gallon a day, with, let's say, one left over for the dustman or the milkman. A gallon a day is six bottles or two flagons. So a man and his wife could have a bottle each for breakfast, dinner and tea or share one and give the other to the children at each meal.'

Mr. Bury has been sympathetic to the Italians, but obviously neither he nor anyone in the Treasury was ever a home bottler. Up to five years ago we lucky ones were in a lovely position. High-quality wine could be purchased for $1.20 a gallon or even less if you were prepared to buy by the hogshead.

Our Sir Henry was so in love with the Country Party and the primary producer that ten gallons could be shifted 150 miles by rail, and still can, for 60 or 70 cents. So it was possible to put a good bottle of wine on the dinner table for 26 cents. One looked upon beer as outrageously expensive by comparison.

So home bottling became a widespread occupation. Every university had its home-bottling groups, and when two or three people gathered together in Melbourne on a Sunday morning often they were not going to church but bottling wine. In the old days the only serious problem was finding enough empty bottles to cope with one's bottlings.

In the five years the good bulk red has almost disappeared, price per gallon has almost doubled and the tax on your ten-gallon barrel is $5. This is at least a 30 percent hike on the price of your bottled product. The ugly thought immediately comes to mind that now it will be cheaper to drink beer.

Half the fun before used to be the annual tour of the wineries. One would sit in a cool cellar with the winemaker, taste wine after wine through the afternoon and plan the home bottlings for the next 12 months. Dan Murphy reveals the picture: 'The laughing, joyous atmosphere of the wineries will be gone. Every glass, every drop of wine will be watched, wiped up and viewed with suspicious eyes,' he says. Yes, even if the winemaker gives you a bottle of wine, he will have to pay the tax on it first.

Now, what worries your correspondent is this. Why is it that fun is always taxed? If you go to the races or play a poker machine or buy a lottery ticket — you're taxed. If you smoke, drink gin, whisky, beer or wine — you're taxed like hell. It may be a comfort to know that, if you drink enough, smoke enough, gamble enough, then you are doing the decent patriotic thing on behalf of your country, building a beef road in North Queensland maybe, or supporting a boy in Vietnam.

But what about the wowsers? Why should they get off scot-free? Wouldn't you think they had a guilt complex because they were not building a beef road in North Queensland or supporting a boy in Vietnam? To relieve their undoubtedly stricken consciences, Mr. Bury should look carefully into new fields of taxation.

How about an eight-cent tax on tomato juice, grapefruit juice, and, particularly, yoghurt. Yoghurt deserves a ferocious tax. I would tax rosehips, bean curd, nut loaf, and I would hit chewing-gum at the rate of 25 cents a packet, plus sarsaparilla and Coca-Cola. Health-food shops would have to apply to the Licensing Court before they could get a licence and there would be a nice tax on their raw oatmeal, dried apricots and carrot juice.

As you can see I am still feeling my way and I would appreciate ideas from the non-drinkers, non-smokers and non-gamblers of the community. We all know they want to pay their fair share in building this great nation.
