Jazzing Up the Dear Old George

6 min read.

First published 10 Jan 1970

A report on the 24th Australian Jazz Convention, held over five days at the George Hotel in Ballarat, where 450 jazz lovers from across the continent took over the Victorian-era hotel for round-the-clock sessions, marches, and improvised mayhem.

IF YOU GO FOR a rich, eloquent scene, the old George is the best we can provide over Christmas and New Year. Donald Anderson, president of the 24th Australian Jazz Convention, said this year they decided they would get away from the big cities. They would take over a hotel somewhere, be all in together, more intimate. So they took over the George Hotel, Ballarat, and it wasn't all that intimate — jazz-lovers homed in from all over the continent, 450 registered.

But let me tell you about the scene at the George. From the outside it looks a dear, sweet old place. The look is pure Kenneth Jack and National Trust, the front is the most perfect example we have of triple-tier verandas with cast-iron balconies. Utterly gorgeous if you try not to see the Courage and Victoria Bitter signs.

Part of the dear old place dates from 1853. Go inside and the booking office is a splendid mahogany box, mirrors and all, like something out of an 1890 San Francisco gin club. The lofty ceiling has exquisite rosettes and the ventilators are ivy in delicate cast iron. The rest is pure Ballarat, circa 1965 — tubular-steel chairs, plastic flowers in the boxes, naked fluorescent tubes for lighting, and ads for Orlando Starwine on the walls.

The dining-room has been converted for the convention. The afternoon blow has just started. Bill Woodman, eyes closed, is hunched over the keys, head shaking side to side, left foot pounding, and the topless piano is rocking under the terrible action.

Jazz conventions are interesting; no fixed age or type. There are people lying on the floor, girls going around with jugs of beer. Some conventioneers look like elderly university professors, others like creatures rescued from the long-lost Leichhardt expedition. Some sit and booze with their backs to the music, others stand around the stage with a warm, religious look in their eyes, such as the young have when they look under the bonnet of a venerable car.

The scene doesn't just happen in the dining-room. Some get out for a blow on the balconies overlooking Lydiard Street. Another very relaxed blowing area is the vast old coach-house out the back. This is called Kelly's Stables and it is a sort of beautiful colony, in brick and vintage cast iron. Neville Stribling and Ade Monsborgh are performing with the Red Onions. Ade Monsborgh they call the father of Australian Jazz, and for the purists this is a big deal. Monsborgh is in a plain prison-grey shirt, his eyes, too, are closed, his face very red and he is making quite wonderful sounds on the tenor sax. Rowan Smith is on the piano. He has on a grey T-shirt, blue jeans and jacket, a panama hat on his head and a truly fantastic amount of hair growth. Give him a billy and he would be the perfect vintage sundowner.

What goes on round the clock? The vice-president, Allan Leake, explains the routine. The blowing sessions start at 2 p.m. and they keep going more or less until 2 a.m. Then there are private sessions upstairs in the rooms. In the mornings, if you can still surface, you come down and listen to the tape. The whole convention is taped, about 50 hours of it. This eventually goes into the convention archives, along with all the other convention records, to be stored at the Melbourne State Library. So you listen to the tape and if you like some excerpt you can order a copy for yourself.

The tape was worth hearing this morning, because George Tack, one of the Melbourne veterans, put on a turn last night. He played the tenor sax and the clarinet at the same time. This is George's thing. Actually he has an ambition to play three instruments at the one time, but he says even two are terribly hard on the chops.

I see the manager. He is Karim Jabbour, a Lebanese. The noise level is very high, the old George seems to be literally shaking. The convention is now five days out. How is he standing up to it? 'Wonderful,' he says. 'I love all of them. I have not had a single complaint.' However, Karim does not look overwell. He has the look of a man who has just spent 200 hours straight in the star chamber.

Karim was saying nothing, but the 450 have been nicely behaved. I hear they have turned on the fire hoses a few times and one drummer did a quite marvellous turn, schussing the stairs on his washboard. Regrettably, his precious musical instrument disintegrated just as he was making his last slides to ground level.

I look at the program. It's not easy to work out what's going on. The bands mix up so much, but the names are interesting. There's the Coney Island Jug and Washboard from Adelaide, the Egg, Blood, Gravy and Sauce from Canberra, the Mouldy Fig All Stars from Sydney, Mabel's Dream from Melbourne, the Vacant Lot, which has no fixed place of abode, and Clean Living Clive's Goodtime Palace Orchestra from Canberra.

The convention secretary, Ross Laird, claims this is the world's oldest jazz convention and as it goes it builds a tradition. Part of the tradition is the march. They had this on Saturday. It was a memorable blow. Maybe 200 marched, playing stuff like 'The Saints Come Marching In' and 'Lady Be Good.' They covered two miles around Ballarat and finished in the marvellous bandstand rotunda in Sturt Street. They marched past all the statues — Robbie Burns, King George V, Hebe, the South African Diggers and, of course, Queen Victoria. I understand the old lady almost dropped her orb and sceptre as they went through.

Ross Laird says most of the music played has been strictly trad. One or two groups are keen on the modern jazz. One hears that there is an amplified guitar and an amplified organ at the convention, but I haven't heard it. No doubt, like Rochester's mad wife, they are kept out of sight.

Ross Laird says that unless you are actually at a jazz convention it's hard to understand. 'Everything's there,' he said, 'a high level of music-making, a good deal of talking, beer-drinking, sex, a lot of fun-making and relaxing. Professional musicians go because they can play the music they like to play. Nobody is paid. It's all amateur. It's the time of the year when you take two weeks off, a week for the convention and a week to get over it.'
