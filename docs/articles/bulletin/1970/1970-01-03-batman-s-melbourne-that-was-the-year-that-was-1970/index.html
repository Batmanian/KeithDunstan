That Was the Year That Was 1970

6 min read.

First published 03 Jan 1970

A satirical prediction column for Melbourne in 1970, forecasting the Tullamarine Airport opening, Sir Henry Bolte's election strategy, a disappointing VFL season, the "Hair" and "Oh! Calcutta!" controversies, the mining boom, the fashion industry's maxi-skirt battle, and the general human comedy of city life.

YOU HAVE READ a great number of brilliant surveys on the sixties, but a pox on that. Your correspondent would like to tell you what will happen in Melbourne in 1970. Dear reader, it is all too obvious.

The major event for the early 1970s will be the opening of Tullamarine Airport. Because of its size, position and ability to operate at night, quickly it will become known as a superior national airport. The major influx of tourists and B.O.A.C. jumbos will come to Melbourne. Several Sydney luxury hotels will go to financial ruin and relations between the two cities, already good, will deteriorate further.

I won't say there will be actual incidents with shots fired between rival police patrol-cars on the border, but it is not impossible. The crisis eventually will be eased by a summit meeting between Sir Henry Bolte and Mr. Askin. It will take place in the dining-room of the Grand Hotel, halfway between Albury and Wodonga.

In 1971, Victoria will, at first, be embarrassed by the arrival of 3000 tourists daily aboard Boeing 747s. However, we will quickly acquaint them with the beauty and perfection of the Melburnian Sunday, of the quiet charm of Captain Cook's cottage, of the lure of hovercraft rides across the water by St. Kilda beach and the devilish fun of Luna Park.

On May 30, 1970, there will be a State election. In early May, magically, the SEC workers will get a very satisfactory wage increase plus four weeks' annual leave, parents of all schools will be offered $100 a year per child and suddenly Sir Henry will announce that the entire Little Desert will be turned into the finest national park this country has ever seen. He will get back by a very narrow majority.

In April there will be the blooming of another football season. Once again it will be a lovely time of year full of heady optimism, the newspapers full of tall recruits who have been discovered in the country and can kick a football 200 yards with either foot and show devastating power in practice games. When play actually starts none of them will make the final training list. In April all 12 captains will announce that what with brilliant recruits, new scientific training methods, and utter dedication, this will be their year to take the flag. In September, 11 captains will explain that their failure was due only to the viciousness of the League tribunal system and an incredible run of injuries.

In June, Harry Miller will put on "Hair" at the Princess Theatre. It will last two nights and Harry will be asked to leave the State. David Mellwraith will put on "Oh! Calcutta!" at the Lido. It will be the ingenious Victorian version done entirely in body stockings with dirty words deleted. The vice squad will take it off after a week. Triumphant after the elections, Sir Henry and Sir Arthur Rylah will make a needed attempt to protect us all against the horrid dangers of the permissive society. As an example of their decent intent they will cover up the large naked breasts of those Victorian queens on the roof of the Legislative Council Chamber. They have distracted MLCs for far too long.

Just before the Budget session, Major-General Risson will describe the superiority of the tram over all other forms of transport and the Railways commissioners will boost our pride with the news that the Victorian passenger transport system is totally unmatched in other countries, and we won't feel nearly so bad about a loss on the road.

It will be a clean year when we won't buy any new roads, when we won't start on a railway subway system, when we will talk magnificently about decentralisation and the quality of our plans will be the admiration of all.

While wringing our hands about decentralisation and traffic congestion, we will complete another half-dozen 30-storey office towers in the heart of the city, creating several million square feet of office space we didn't need anyway. Meanwhile, land values in Collins, Bourke, Swanston and Elizabeth Streets will continue to rise, another 12 pubs will discover they can't compete against insurance companies and will be forced to close. The city will cover about 11 miles between "wells" and ponder the thought that what the Rechabites always wanted has been done without their help, by insurance houses.

What else? In 1970 you will spend 25 days, 4 hours and 22 minutes looking for parking places and you will get three parking tickets. When you want to make a telephone call you won't have five cents in your pocket and when you look up the telephone book the requisite page will have been ripped out.

Come the first Tuesday in November and in the office sweep you will get a horse you didn't know existed until that morning. A New Zealand horse, apparently backed by everybody else except you, will finally win at 35 to 1.

In November the English touring cricket side will arrive. There will be a marvellous cocktail reception at the Melbourne Cricket Club, where the rival captains will toast each other and pledge that this will be the brightest season ever with dazzling high-speed scoring. Then they will proceed to play six Tests and complete only one of them.

In January, February, March and April it will be the turn of the fashion industry and particularly the Wool Board will announce that the mini-skirt is not being worn anywhere else in the world, that it is decadent and that the maxi is in. Our lovely Melburnian girls will continue to wear it precisely as before.

Jay Thomson will bring out three more thought-provoking London bedroom farces. Moomba will be accused of lacking in culture and will attract another crowd of 750,000, and in July there will be another riot outside the Melbourne Cricket Ground over football tickets.

Just when you're having fun at noon, just as the children have been allowed to stay up until 9 p.m. to watch TV 280 times and you will say "In my day . . ." 491 times.

That's the worst of it: I can't stand it.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: That Was the Year That Was 1970, The Bulletin, 3 January 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
