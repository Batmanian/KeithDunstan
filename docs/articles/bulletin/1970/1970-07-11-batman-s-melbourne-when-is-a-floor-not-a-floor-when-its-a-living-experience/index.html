When Is a Floor Not a Floor? When It's a Living Experience

5 min read.

First published 11 Jul 1970

Two new exhibitions at Melbourne galleries, an undulating timber floor built from Whelan the Wrecker salvage at Gallery A in South Yarra and a Philip Morris air-themed show at the National Gallery, test Keith Dunstan's willingness to take conceptual art seriously.

FRANKLY, I am a little disturbed. Once upon a time there was a beautiful sense of repose about art. One remembers the awe-inspiring dignity of the old Stawell Gallery at the National Gallery. One walked very quietly across the parquet floor, sat upon one of the very elegant settees to admire from a distance of 15 paces a Canaletto, a Tiepolo or a Constable. It was impossible to conceive of anything untoward ever taking place there. One's sensibilities were never disturbed unless, perhaps, by an overvoluptuous Botticelli.

Friends, art is no longer the same. For one thing, a 27-year-old artist, Guy Stuart, has built out at Gallery A, South Yarra, what has been described as 'A Continuous Wooden Floor.' We were told that Mr. Stuart would build his floor for several weeks and then, when it was complete, he would tear it down again. This would be living sculpture. Not only would people be able to look at this floor, they would be able to feel it, experience it, walk on it and get the excitement of it. Mr. Stuart said: 'Normally when you see a floor you don't pay any attention to it. I want this floor to force its attention on you.'

When he completed his work of art your correspondent hurried out to South Yarra for the excitement. There was a sign on the door: PLEASE ENTER CAREFULLY — AT YOUR OWN RISK.

It was extremely difficult to go farther. Mr. Stuart's floor occupied the entire gallery. It was rather like the old mountainous floor at the Giggle Palace in Luna Park, except that it was perhaps more like a mad roof in a mansion occupied by one of the nastier cannibalistic witches in Grimms' Fairy Tales. It was made from second-hand timber won from Whelan the Wrecker and it had corrugations or wooden waves 8ft. high. Mrs. Cherelle Hutchinson, of Gallery A, was there; so, too, was the artist. Obviously they were going to be very upset if one didn't walk on their floor.

'Lots of people have tried it, all ages,' said Mrs. Hutchinson. 'One mad young thing danced all over it at the weekend. Then we had a lovely man who really enjoyed it yesterday. He was at least 60.'

Oh, yes, Mr. Stuart was absolutely right. His floor wasn't at all like the ordinary, dull, unforcing-attention-upon-you flat floor, which offered no challenge. This one was utterly daunting.

The giggle palace waves were set at angles of about 45 degrees, the timber pieces overlapping like weatherboard in reverse. Going up the side of the wave wasn't so bad, but going down the other side was unnerving. Here one could get distressingly intimate with the work of art. Then, having reached the bottom of the gulf, one had to tackle the next wave, and so on.

When one left, Mr. Stuart was contemplating what he would do next. Perhaps he would cut sections so that gallery visitors could contemplate the artistry of the framework underneath. Or, if this was a floor, perhaps he would now build the walls. It sounded like an awe-inspiring encore.

But that wasn't all. As mentioned earlier, art isn't restful any more. Earlier this year we had a visit by Mr. Christo. In New South Wales he wrapped up a stretch of beachfront. At our National Gallery he wrapped up a heap of wool bales, which, one would have thought, were fairly efficiently wrapped in the first place.

Well, this time Professor James Harithas, of Hunter College School of Visual Arts, New York, brought us the Philip Morris exhibition on 'Air.' The exhibition, we were told, was the complex reaction of 11 artists to the motion of pure air. The magnificent catalogue was full of profundities from the professor. It begins: 'Air is above all content. Its origin, extension, volume and its meaning as experience is barely conscious. Air is a mysterious legacy, sometimes called the breath of God, and the essential condition for life's being. For the Quiche Maya the air is like a god. In metaphysics and mythology, air is the instrument of the gods, etc.'

So as one enters the Exhibition Hall at the gallery, the sight is almost as surprising as at Gallery A. One is surrounded by a vast whirring, panting and hissing. On one side there is a snake-like plastic thing, like so many lounge-room pouffes stuck together, going right up to the ceiling. On another side there is a sculpture like a parachute, and just as large. It is held up by the puffing of an electric fan, which turns itself off every 14 minutes. So in a quite obscene fashion it falls and has to be reinflated.

There is a room with another room inside it. Fans push air around an enclosed corridor and the excitement here is that you hear the rushing of air. There is a gadget which reacts to your breathing and pumps up red balloons. There is a closed room with a ceiling cooled to below freezing. The heat of people who enter the room and remain for a time raises the temperature to the point of condensation, provoking rainfall.

I have a nostalgic memory of sessions at the old gallery when this novel idea could be simulated by a leak in the roof.

Alan McCulloch of the 'Herald' found the exhibition 'wholly fascinating' and Ann Galbally of the 'Age' thought it lived up to its advance publicity, 'a lot of hot air.'

But your correspondent can hardly wait to see what will happen next. Why not try an exhibition which has just been a hit in Los Angeles? The Swiss artist Dieter Rot put on an exhibit of 37 cases of rotten cheese. Swelling limburger, cheddar and camembert cheeses were packed into boxes, which burst, creating beautiful art forms. So here was a show that was not only spectacular on the eye but on the nose as well.
