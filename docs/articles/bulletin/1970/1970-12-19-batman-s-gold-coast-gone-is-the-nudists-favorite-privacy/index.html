Gone Is the Nudists' Favourite Privacy

5 min read.

First published 19 Dec 1970

A Christmas visit to Surfers Paradise reveals the Broadbeach International hotel remodelled in Spanish and Mexican theme bars, a revolving giant beer mug on the main strip, and Marineland's new killer whale shipped from Seattle at great expense, while the nudists' favourite dunes have been turned into a tourist aquatic park.

FRANKLY, I never know whether to love or loathe Surfers Paradise. In Brisbane, among the nice people, it is done to be a loather and to take your holidays on the northern beaches, at Bribie, Mooloolaba or some such. I did this one year and it was lovely for the first week. Then the tomb-like nature of the place began to take a grip. The most thrilling entertainment to be had was euchre in the shire hall on Wednesday night.

But Surfers, we found, was always so vulgar, so indescribably horrid, that there was always something to look at. It is good for one's soul to be shocked to the eyeballs every quarter of an hour, to have something to tut-tut over.

So this year I sat at a table at the Beachcomber taking in the sights — ladies from Sydney's Vaucluse with bottle-yellow hair, stained flesh bulging over their bikinis, brilliantly successful stockbrokers from Queen Street, Melbourne, with their young friends, and young men from South Yarra in their purple pants, so eloquently covered in buttons, and purple shirts held in place with leather harness sufficient for a Clydesdale.

On the way down from Brisbane to Surfers Paradise I spotted a new place a-building called the Tramburger Diner. As a Melburnian it was a scene to bring tears to the eyes. A clearing has been carved out of the scrub, and there, in a huddle, a dozen or so old Brisbane Silver Bullet trams in mint condition. Let us hope you can sit on the original seats, ring the tram bell for service and that the waiters will be dressed in correct dingy Brisbane tram conductors' uniforms and those lovable Foreign Legion caps.

In much-abused places like Honolulu there are ruthless laws on the size of signs that may be erected, and perhaps they have a law also on the Gold Coast — no signs under 12 feet high. On the way into Southport there is an extraordinary collection of billboards which conveniently saves one from having to look at the scenery.

So you move through looking at such contributions to culture as the Mota Vue Theatre, O'Nite and Holidays, the Trailotel and Caravanserai. Right in the centre of Surfers there is now a gigantic beer mug with foam on top, which gently revolves; a piece of pop culture which no doubt will be for ever remembered like the old Camel smoking sign in Times Square, New York.

Years ago I was present at the opening night of Lennons' Broadbeach Hotel, so for old times' sake I paid a call. It has changed, oh, how it has changed. It is now the Broadbeach International, and it has the Spanish look with Spanish entrance. It has the Matadore Cocktail Bar, the Spanish Restaurant, the Calypso West Indies Bar, the King's Road London Bar. A sign says the fourth floor is Mediterranean, the third floor Oriental, the second floor South Seas and the first Mexican.

By the swimming-pool there used to be an exquisite seal sculpture by Len and Kath Shillam. It is still there but it has been painted black to get rid of that horrid look of natural stone.

Constantly one gets the suggestion: 'I suppose you noticed tremendous changes.' Well, it has changed and it hasn't changed. Last year, there was much talk of a $4 million hotel-shopping development right in the centre of Surfers with a ten-storey tower like a mini Australia Square. There was no evidence of that, but the same beautiful optimism prevails.

On the very strip where three years ago a cyclone tore away the sand and swimming-pools, and gardens and beach-houses dissolved into the surf while all night teams of workers put up sandbags to save apartment towers, more are being built.

The beach, particularly in the early mornings, where one can walk almost alone on the white sand, is beautiful. But then some of the same old charming things are still done. They still pipe music on to the beach, even with the warmth of commercials. At Burleigh Heads you can even get a complete show with pop bands on a stage, hypnotist and all.

But you are waiting to hear what is new. Keith Williams, the water-ski promoter, has created a new tourist attraction for Christmas, called Ski Land. He has spent $250,000 in creating a lake among the dunes and staging a continuous aquatic show. It used to be a favourite spot for the Paradise nudists — they gained privacy among the cave-like shapes of the dunes. Not any more.

Then two months ago Marineland acquired a genuine killer whale. It is now 12 feet long and weighs 1500 pounds. It came out first class by Qantas, lying on a stretcher while water was constantly reticulated over it. The economics are interesting: cost Seattle to San Francisco, $1300; cost San Francisco to Brisbane, $6000; purchase price, $5000 down and $5000 on a safe alive arrival.

At first the whale refused to eat local fish, so they had to import herring from his own waters. This cost $3.50 a pound air mail and the whale ate 50 pounds a day. But it is a gorgeous, happy little creature and David Brown of Marineland seems able to teach it anything. In January, when the whale is completely trained, a young lady — their own whale jockey — will ride it around the pool on its back. Eventually the whale will grow to 25 feet long. Until then it is still taking riders, and making spectacular 12-foot leaps out of the water.

There's the same old fuss between council and businessmen over parking meters. Sexy metermaids are still one of the more delightful sights, and for Christmas there's a new idea — Father Christmas on the steps of the post office. He will accept your parking ticket and make sure matters go no further. But he will do it only once for each owner. After all, Father Christmas comes but once for everyone.
