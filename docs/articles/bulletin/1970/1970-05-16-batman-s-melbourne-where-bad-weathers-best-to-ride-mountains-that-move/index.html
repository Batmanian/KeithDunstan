Where Bad Weather's Best to Ride Mountains That Move

6 min read.

First published 16 May 1970

The World Surfing Championships at Bell's Beach near Geelong draw 80 competitors from 18 countries, with Midget Farrelly the finest artist on the waves, and the event saved from cancellation only by Sir Henry Bolte backing it with $22,000 weeks before the state election.

ACTUALLY, Melbourne doesn't get much publicity as a surfing town. Admittedly, St. Kilda in one of its brochures suggests that it is a lovely place for surf, but even Prince Charles didn't take his board there.

Ah, but if you head out 18 miles past Geelong toward the Great Ocean Road there's a place called Bell's Beach. It is not a beach for the kiddies. Until a few weeks ago there was no respectable road in and you were hard put to find even a sign. And it was a beach without a trace of civilisation — not a house, not a pub, no mini golf, not even a chiko-roll stand.

The beach is less than a quarter of a mile long and ends in a bluff with a terrible rocky outcrop. There you can guarantee you'll smash a board a season. But Bell's Beach has the perfect geometric shape, and even when beaches two miles away have waves as flat as Albert Park Lake, superbly shaped waves are rolling smoothly at Bell's. Surfies claim it as the surfie's Wimbledon — there's nothing as good in the country.

You may well ask why the World Surfing Championships should be held in Victoria when freezing weather almost can be guaranteed. Well, there were four reasons: Captain Cook, Bell's Beach, the appropriately hideous weather that comes up Bass Strait in May promising great waves, and Sir Henry Bolte.

The championships were beset with crises. The Australian Surfriders' Association needed nigh on $100,000 to back the titles. They thought everything was fine until a soft drink company which they hoped would provide $20,000 decided to back out. They appealed to John Gorton and received a straight 'No.' Then, with only 24 hours before they had to decide to cancel, they appealed to Sir Henry Bolte. Sir Henry agreed to back the titles for up to $22,000. Tony Olsson, president of the association, said he was 'overwhelmed' at the offer. Some coldly cynical people said it was actually Sir Henry who had been overwhelmed because there was a State election on May 30.

So 80 big-name surfers from 18 countries came to town. Some of them were beautiful, the Hawaiian girls with hair 36 inches long. Some of the American boys were pretty good too, with hair almost equally as long. Handsome curves tend to disappear under wetsuits, so with long hair always flying in the spray the identification of the sexes was a problem at Bell's.

Yes, there were plenty of big names. We were honoured, too, with the presence of James Arness, Marshal Matt Dillon of 'Gunsmoke,' no less. His son Rolf is in the American team.

The first three days the surf was wretchedly flat, but on the fourth day Bell's lived up to its reputation, with six-, eight-, ten-foot waves. The surfers were pulled out of bed at 5.30 with the object of working as long as there was daylight. The heats ran for 30 minutes with judging on each surfer's three best waves. At the end of the heat the judges sounded a vintage car horn and they had to come in.

As for the spectators, they sat, thousands of them, on the rocky bluff, and one could look straight down, almost as close as at the centre court at Wimbledon.

One wasn't quite prepared for the beauty of the spectacle. For example, there was Ben Aipa, of Hawaii, a huge man, all of 16 or 17 stone, who, when lying down, covered his whole board. Yet he could handle this thing as the most delicate of instruments. Midget Farrelly, of Australia, was the greatest artist. As he did his side slips and climbs across the front of the wave, undulating knees and back, here was all the grace of a Nureyev. Or Wayne Lynch, the local boy doing a re-entry — this was something to see. He would wait for the precise moment when the wave was about to break, turn around, head straight up the face and then soar off the top in a great arc.

The crowd was interesting. There was the hippie set in goodly numbers. The ideal hippie uniform right now is bare feet, ragged jeans, T-shirt and a blanket poncho. Rich hippies have glorious embroidered ponchos or ponchos in mohair. But there is also much prestige to be had by wearing an army jacket with a hole in the middle.

On the other hand, there were surflovers who had flown in from all over Australia, many of them not young. There was Pop Bendle, 65, from Queensland, with his wife, both still keen surfers. There was Dr. Robert Spence and Ron Ware, of Sydney, both 45, and still competitive board-surfers.

Ron Ware, whose son Stuart is in the Australian team, believes there is nothing on earth to compare with the excitement and exhilaration of being on a wave. When one suggested snow-skiing he said this was a very poor second, 'because here one rode a mountain that moved.'

It was then that Dr. Spence provided the line of the day: 'I'm saving up snow-skiing for when I'm an old man.'

There were movie and still cameras with long lenses all over the bluff. One particularly long lens was being operated by Thor Svenson, executive director of the Windansea Surf Club, San Diego. Svenson, with very bleached hair, looked an aging surfie. He said he followed the sun, Hawaii, Fiji, Tahiti, Queensland, Peru, writing for a surf magazine. Bell's Beach he thought was wonderful, almost beyond belief. Where else could you find a beach like this with such surf, untouched, uncommercialised, unpolluted? He thought the world's best surfing was at Hawaii, but Bell's was second.

While working his camera through shot after shot he was interesting on the philosophy of the surfer. 'They're individuals. Now most of the Californian boys believe there should be no competitions. Do you know why they surf? To get away from the dreadful rules and restrictions of life, to be free out there on the water. What happens in competition? They get caught up in rules again — exactly what they're trying to avoid.'
