Ho, Yo-Yo and a Bottle of Coke

5 min read.

First published 20 Jun 1970

A Coca-Cola sponsored yo-yo promotion at the Southern Cross Hotel gives Keith Dunstan occasion to mourn the passing of spontaneous childhood hobbies, from hula hoops and go-carts to silkworms and cigarette cards.

FRANKLY, there isn't the spontaneous passion in kids' hobbies any more. Unless they get the big promotion, inspired directly from Chicago and New York, they are nothing.

As I walked up to the Southern Cross Hotel for the Coca-Cola promotion, I thought back to the biggest craze I could remember. This was serious. I had the misery of coming from a non-cigarette-smoking family and at the age of eight every penny I could earn, borrow or steal went on cigarette cards, or those marvellous silk-finish cards of butterflies.

Hoop bowling was also pretty big. The ideal hoop was a T-model Ford tyre. It was high and thin and it would go like the devil.

The other day I found an old T-model tyre in a paddock and took this precious thing home in triumph. I showed it to my son, but he looked at it only in contempt. It wasn't a tubeless, it wasn't a radial; it didn't even have the blessing of Coca-Cola. I left it for the rubbish man.

Go-carts, too, were tremendous. We used to hang round the garages to get those ball-bearing castings, and when used as wheels they made a splendid, satisfying screech on the bitumen. But nobody makes go-carts any more. You buy go-cart kits at Myer's or McEwen's and the wheels are refined rubber-tyred affairs, the same as those used on motor-mowers.

And silkworms were a very serious matter. We had them by the thousand in shoe boxes and we used to wind off the silk from the cocoons on to empty wine bottles. One of life's little tragedies — it was like producing wheat — was that we could never find the slightest use for our annual silk production. There are not many silkworms about these times. They have, in fact, become a gourmet's delight for canapés. You can buy them at Myer's either fried or chocolate-coated, individually wrapped in silver paper. I am sorry that I keep mentioning the Myer store, but they do have a lot to answer for.

Now for a hobby to soar it must be scientifically promoted. The idea is given a test run in a small area in, say, California. If it makes it in San Diego it goes to a larger town, say Dallas, and if it makes it there it gets the multi-million-dollar promotion and, finally, it is exported to Australia.

This happened with the revival of the hula hoop, which curiously enough we first invented. It happened with the bounce ball, and it happened with frisbee, a plastic dish which would glide through the air.

And so I entered the Southern Cross to witness the revival of the yo-yo, now promoted worldwide with the blessing of Coca-Cola. The Coca-Cola people have the dream that if they have a yo-yo in every child's hand he will have a bottle of Coke in the other.

The public-relations firm had declared this as Yo-Yo Week. It was also Library Week, but Yo-Yo Week was getting rather more publicity. In the Delta Room, Coca-Cola had the world's international yo-yo team, including stars from Japan, Colombia, Argentina, Mexico, Spain and Australia.

Oh, they do these things very thoroughly. The Australian champion, Mr. David Jones, wearing a red blazer with Coca-Cola on the left breast, told us that the yo-yo began in the Philippines. It was a massive thing, a foot across with knife edges, and the natives used it for hunting. They would sit in trees waiting for their prey, using the yo-yo to wrap around the legs of the animal. Your correspondent, as he reached for a chicken sandwich, wondered what shot they used, 'Over the Falls' or 'Round the World.'

Mr. Jones said further that the skill of the yo-yo player could be compared with that of the billiards player and he saw the day when it would become an international sport. Your correspondent wondered whether Mr. Jones would win our first Olympic Games yo-yo gold medal.

Each of the national representatives came forward in turn and did unbelievable yo-yo things you never saw before. Then they all stood in line and did a yo-yo ballet in time to the old Bob Sanders theme tune, 'The Spanish Flea.' Next we had a daredevil act. Mr. Jones balanced a 20-cent piece on the back of his hand, kept two yo-yos going simultaneously from either hand, danced them closer and closer to Mr. Jones' ears until one yo-yo delicately flipped away the 20-cent piece.

The representatives from Japan were two exquisite ladies, Miss Atsuko Terashima and Miss Noriko Aoyama, both in formal kimonos without Coca-Cola on the left breast. They did such manoeuvres as the 'Boomerang,' the 'Japanese Elevator,' the 'Eiffel Tower' and the 'Tokyo Tower.' Subtly, yo-yo style, they conveyed a sense of national pride. Then came the most deeply moving part of their act: they sang the Coca-Cola commercial in Japanese.

On the way out we all received our yo-yos. We were told that there would be competitions in Melbourne on behalf of charity. One team of women led by Lady Bolte would compete against a team led by Mrs. Andrew Peacock — unquestionably one of the greatest events of Yo-Yo Week.

Being careful not to be seen, I snuck off to the lavatory at the Southern Cross. I looped the string over my finger and let the yo-yo drop. Superbly it snapped back into my hand again. I tried an 'Over the Falls,' the yo-yo going perilously close to the washroom mirror. Back it came again. The old skill was still there. Pure bliss.
