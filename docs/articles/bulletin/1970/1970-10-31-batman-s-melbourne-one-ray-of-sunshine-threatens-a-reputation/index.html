One Ray of Sunshine Threatens a Reputation

5 min read.

First published 31 Oct 1970

A week of unusually withering comment about Melbourne, from a visiting Clement Freud who called it an ideal city for escaping Dartmoor, to Robin Boyd lamenting the loss of creative spirit in its architecture, and Barry Humphries delivering a fond but cutting ode to its musical-comedy qualities, prompts Keith Dunstan to wonder whether becoming internationally understood will cost Melbourne its carefully nourished reputation.

THIS HAS BEEN a wounding week for our pride in Melbourne. After the collapse of the West Gate Bridge the 'Sunday Observer' brought out the theory that sharp fluctuations in temperature, wind strength and sunshine could have been a factor in the disaster. In the 36 hours before the accident, the temperature fluctuated between 43 degrees at midnight and 62 degrees at midday on two successive days. The wind fluctuated from 3 to 30 knots. As yet nobody has challenged this theory. We take it for granted that Melbourne weather could do terrible things.

Then a gentleman named Clement Freud came to town. He said he was a grandson of Sigmund Freud. He was here for a curious phenomenon called a Buttercups Bake-Off. He was writing articles for the 'Financial Times' in London and he was here for this curious phenomenon. He hated Melbourne, he liked Sydney, he loved Adelaide. He put out the theory that Melbourne was still in the Biggles era and said: 'Verily I say unto you, this is an ideal city for an escaping prisoner of war. There is a general air of incompetence, the kind of cheerful inefficiency which pervades everything that is done, and above all the pervading fulfilment of suffering from delusions of grandeur.'

Could Biggs be driving a taxi? No Melbourne taxi driver would punch a passenger in the face. And if Biggs is here it is nice to think of Ronald Biggs. And yet, one wonders whether the feller mightn't have had a better time in Dartmoor.

Then, in this same week, as the new president of the Victorian chapter of the Royal Australian Institute of Architects, Robin Boyd said the initiative, the explorative spirit, had gone from Melbourne architecture. 'We Melburnians will have to let Sydney become the hub of creative urbanity, youth and progressive building. We are only representative builders in the world's big-city league. Melbourne will have to settle into a quiet middle-age. But do we look it? Can anyone claim that the standards of average building design and techniques have matured in proportion to our population?'

Mr. Boyd mentioned the fury in Melbourne over the proposed 53-storey skyscraper by the city square. After this, word seeped back from London that the skyscraper would be reduced in height. 'How correctly the developers judged Melbourne,' said Boyd. 'Any developer sniffing the air of Melbourne would be likely to come up with a similar soothing promise. To make a controversial building more acceptable here one cuts it down to size ... Melbourne size. An astute developer in Sydney, wanting to make a controversial proposal more acceptable, would promise to add ten storeys to it.'

Barry Humphries, too, in an 'Age' article, told of his devoted marriage to Melbourne. 'It's not so much a spot as a musical comedy, really, written by a New Zealand hosiery salesman in the sixties. It cleverly combines astrology with the Korean War, so there's something in it for everyone. After the show there's ballroom dancing on the stage if you don't feel like cappuccino and toasted cheese with chutney sandwiches.

'Seriously, friends, Melbourne is unique from the spiritual point of view. It's the only place on earth where the visitor from abroad can close his eyes and wonder if there really is life after death.'

That was just one week of comment. Why is it that Melbourne so brilliantly attracts comment far more than any other Australian city? Over the years I have collected a little scrapbook of treasured gems.

Robert Morley: 'I don't care for Melbourne people because they are spineless. They are all yogis. They get used to their spiked beds and finally begin to worship them. I found Melbourne a boring town, an unpainted town, a town with bad roads. I got tired of the methylated spirit drinkers lying round the stage door. Why on earth don't they paint the Town Hall?'

Terence Stamp: 'Melbourne — it's a giggle. Melbourne is such a conventional place I'm surprised you didn't ask the Beatles to shave off their hair.'

Max Bygraves: 'I've always wanted to see a ghost town. You couldn't even get a parachute to open here after ten p.m.'

Peter Black in the 'Daily Mail': 'Melbourne is a city full of Victorian cant. You would hardly believe the difficulties I had in getting a drink.'

Oh, I have lots, lots more. But wait, a young English blueblood, Lord Zouche, has come to town. He has taken on public relations for Sunshine. Now Sunshine is an industrial place, quite horrid, flat, uninteresting and nobody in their right mind would go there to sunbathe. He is quoted as saying: 'Sunshine will be like selling Australia. Terribly easy if you have the right contacts. I have driven through Sunshine three times. I have met some of the councillors and some of the people. They're a fine lot.'

One can see Lord Zouche creating a splendid new image for Sunshine, and then it will be just a simple step to take over PR for all Melbourne.

Then, dammit, we'll lose our carefully nourished reputation. We'll become as common as other places.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: One Ray of Sunshine Threatens a Reputation, The Bulletin, 31 October 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
