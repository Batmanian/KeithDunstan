You Get One-Third Less at Tullamarine

5 min read.

First published 04 Jul 1970

A comparison of the newly opened Tullamarine Airport with Sydney's Kingsford Smith finds Melbourne's terminal consistently one-third smaller in every measure, from baggage carousels and immigration desks to souvenir koalas and VIP lounges.

AS YOU KNOW, this page has always shown great sadness at the little things that have come between the beautiful relationship that normally exists between Melbourne and Sydney.

One of the little things that has come between us has been the airport at Tullamarine. It was in 1967 that our relations sunk to their stage of least beauty. Sydney politicians and leader writers were describing Tullamarine as a nasty plot designed to destroy Sydney.

Oh, it was all too obvious. Look at the Prime Minister, Mr. Holt, a Victorian. Look at the Deputy Prime Minister, Mr. McEwen, a Victorian. Look at the Leader of the Opposition, Mr. Calwell, a Victorian, the Minister for National Development, Mr. Fairbairn, a Victorian. And how about the Minister for Civil Aviation, Senator Henry — a Tasmanian, but clearly allied to Victoria.

Well, all that was quietly smoothed over. Sydney was allowed to build its bigger and grander international airport, but, sadly, one has to report that the quality of our friendship once again has become strained.

The theory behind building a $50-million airport way out at Tullamarine was that it could operate around the clock. Ah, but just a fortnight before it was due to open, the Minister for Civil Aviation, Senator Cotton, announced there would be a night curfew between 11 p.m. and 6 a.m.

Sir Henry Bolte announced he was furious. 'One of the most shocking decisions I have ever heard,' said he. And he made it clear it was all a Sydney plot. After all, here in Melbourne we had hoped to help Sydney out. There would be big jets, unable to get into Sydney because of the night-flying ban. So we could offer them the convenience of carrying on to Melbourne. And some of us did a little arithmetic. The average American tourist spends $600 in Australia. Say a jumbo jet brings in 350 passengers, then that is $210,000 a time.

But wait, before anyone gets too upset over this, your correspondent has now inspected both international terminals. They are almost identical. At both you arrive on the high level and leave at the low level. They both have the same, hideous blush-pink reflecting windows. Actually, I find them unnerving. As you arrive at the airport they look as if they are reflecting the flames of some awful disaster.

The big entrance halls, too, are almost the same, with a great stretch of unrelieved terrazzo going off for ever into the distance. Indeed, the airports are so similar one could forgive a traveller who had just flown from Sydney to Melbourne via Tullamarine for not knowing where he was, and after leaving Tullamarine asking a taxi-driver to drive him to Bondi.

Yet, after all the agony, it is important to point out, in the interests of our beautiful relationship, that almost everywhere Sydney has been given the edge. Sydney opened on May 2 with an official launching by Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth. Tullamarine had to wait until July 1 and we only rated the Prime Minister.

And while the design is the same, everything at the Sydney airport is bigger by one-third and one marvels how they have done this with almost mathematical precision. The Sydney terminal is 700,000 square feet, Melbourne's is 500,000. Sydney has parking space for 1,600 cars, Melbourne fits in 1,360. Whereas Sydney has six baggage carousels, we have four; 36 baggage inspection counters, we have 24; 20 immigration desks, we have 18. And whereas Sydney has three special VIP rooms, we have two. I didn't get around to counting the number of toilets, but one can safely presume that here, too, Melbourne is down by one-third.

Sydney has a complex of bars and reception-rooms worth $2 million on their own. Melbourne is considerably less grand. Out there on Botany Bay the reception-rooms all have attractive non-Botany Bay names, such as Coogee, Bondi, Manly, Narrabeen and the Waratah Room. The best of them is the Macquarie Bar, done with the real roaring 'nineties atmosphere — red plush seats, heavily embossed crimson wallpaper, more panelling than the House of Lords, and gas-style lamps.

Tullamarine, on the other hand, has no olde worlde bar. The catering area has the mundane title Top Air. Up one end there is a Top Air Lounge. This is all in gold with a wall-to-wall gold carpet and an illuminated, tiled, padded bar. Next door there is the Top Air Cocktail Bar, with a theme of deep red. Behind the bar there is an illuminated painting of what look like red clouds against a red sunset — the better to match the alarm you are already experiencing from the red windows. Then, next to that again, is the Top Air Restaurant, with seating for 350, dance floor and all.

However, one does feel that Melbourne matches Sydney with the souvenir shops. In Sydney you can buy opals, perfume, binoculars, chunks of mulga with barometers in the centre, bottle-openers made out of kangaroo paws, and genuine Digger hats. Melbourne, on the other hand, has memo books decorated with pictures of the Shrine and Flinders Street railway station, glasses decorated with Foster's labels, mulga-wood salt-and-pepper shakers carved to the shape of the map of Australia, and stuffed kangaroos and koalas in serried thousands. You can buy a little koala for $1.65, or a whopping, fat koala, 3ft. high, for $33.22.

But, no doubt, the best buy of all is the koala perfumed with the scent of Australian wildflowers, which comes in a can. On the can is a picture of a koala with the message: 'Canned toy cuddly koala. I bring you the fragrance of the wildest flowers in Australia.' Open the can and one receives a beautiful whiff, hard to describe — perhaps a cross between Woolworths scent and lavatory lollies.

But, all in all, there is little between the two airports. To preserve the beautiful relationship between the two cities, I think the Department of Civil Aviation should continue its one-third policy right through — allow Sydney one-third more night-flying time and the sale of one-third more scented, cuddly koalas.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: You Get One-Third Less at Tullamarine, The Bulletin, 4 July 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
