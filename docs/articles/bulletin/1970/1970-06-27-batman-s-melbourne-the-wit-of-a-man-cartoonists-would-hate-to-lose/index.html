The Wit of a Man Cartoonists Would Hate to Lose

6 min read.

First published 27 Jun 1970

A pocket compendium of Sir Henry Bolte's most quotable remarks, assembled after his crushing 1970 election victory, argues that his deadpan bluntness deserves its own volume alongside Churchill and Menzies.

WHEN ALL ELSE is worried, insecure, unstable and crumbling in Australia, there is the immense security here in Victoria of having Sir Henry. He is our rock. He has been here now as Premier for 15 years. On election night he was all powerful. He told every TV and radio commentator that they did not know what they were talking about. He also intimated to the computer that it was terribly ungifted. There were suggestions that the Liberal Party would be struggling to hold power in Victoria. Sir Henry didn't have the slightest doubts. The Liberals would be as strong as ever.

That weekend 'Four Corners' did a piece on him. John Pinkney, the 'Age' TV critic, wrote: 'He remains (for the cameras, anyway) a friendly, unspoiled son of the soil, who, on his property, wears a tatty jumper with a panache few premiers could match. It was telly verite with a vengeance. My favourite moment was the country-pub sequence in which Sir Henry, sipping a drink, turned to the camera and assured the director: "I come here every week. I'm not staging this for your jolly show." '

See what I mean? He is gloriously mellowed. He is what we would hate to lose. We have become accustomed to his face, and, good grief, he now actually looks like a genius.

He was born in Ballarat on May 20, 1908, which just puts him under Taurus, the sign of the bull. The other day his stars advised: 'Your approach to basic tasks should be laced with enthusiasm. You gain respect by showing initiative for your own ultimate gain. Remember, many look to you as example.'

And what was he doing this day? He was having a go at the Senate: 'The Senate and its committees are the greatest farce ever pulled. All Senate committees do is poke their nose into business they know nothing about.'

Senator Mulvihill (Labor, N.S.W.) was offended by this and replied: 'Sir Henry is a hillbilly politician with a core mind and Adolf Hitler ... there is a distinct similarity between Sir Henry and Adolf Hitler. Hitler would attack somebody or something to appeal to the basic desires of the people.'

And was our rock crushed by this? No, it was splendid. Sir Henry continued to approach his basic task laced with enthusiasm. He replied: 'Who is this bloke Mulvihill? I've been attacked by bigger men than him. That's really amusing. If the Senator had my record we'd listen to him. But it's just a lot of hot air.'

See what I mean? There have been volumes on 'The Wit of Sir Winston Churchill,' 'The Wit of Sir Robert Menzies' — isn't it time that we had one on 'The Wit of Sir Henry Bolte'?

'Newsday' has tried, the 'Sun News-Pictorial' has tried, but now it is up to The Bulletin to give you a pocket compendium of Sir Henry Bolte quotes:

On censorship: 'The State Government will do its utmost to save Victorians from filth. I want to make it perfectly clear that my Government and my party stand for decency.' (November 27, 1969.)

On striking: 'The unions can strike from now until doomsday. The position will not alter.' (November 27, 1969.) 'The SEC strikers are a bunch of brigands.' (November 18, 1969.) 'They bloody well foot-sore; it's nothing to do with ...'

Gems from his London tour of 1969: 'I deny that we are wowsers. We've got the best liquor laws in Australia.'

On 'Hair' and 'The Boys in the Band': 'I wouldn't cross the road to see them.'

Did he believe fruit machines had broken down the moral fibre of the people of N.S.W.? 'I believe so. Home ownership is greater in Victoria than in New South Wales. Our savings are $100 a person higher and, this being so, where is all this being dissipated in New South Wales?'

Again on poker machines: 'I'll have them here over my dead body.'

On the Country Party: 'I've kept them in a corner for the past 15 years. I sometimes think they ought to become part of the Labor Party.'

On the Riverina: 'We will certainly welcome them if they want to join us.' (August 22, 1969.)

On hearing that Mick Jagger was to play Ned Kelly: 'Who is he, anyway?' Answer: He leads a group called the Rolling Stones. Sir Henry: 'Let them roll somewhere else.' (July 1, 1968.)

On the Rose-Rudkin fight: 'It would be a sporting tragedy if the fight were televised interstate and not in Victoria.' (February 26, 1969.)

On the pure beauty of Victoria: 'Victoria is the gem in the Federal Government's crown.' (October, 1969.) Also, on return from trips, we have had: 'Victoria is the greatest little State in Australia' and 'An afternoon run in the Dandenongs will do me any time.'

On entering Parliament: 'I thought it was a joke when I was invited to stand for Parliament in 1945.' (November, 1963.)

On Jack Galbally's fourteenth annual attempt to abolish hanging in Victoria: 'I have no intention of amending the Crimes Act. It's just another stunt.' (November, 1969.)

He has received his honorary doctorate from Monash, but there are some worthy possibilities there for our book.

On Monash University application for more land: 'Who do they think they are? Farmers?'

On the low pass rate: 'I'd lock the bludgers up in a room until they pass.'

On quality of life: 'The electors have made Victoria safe for some time from Socialism.' (December 12, 1955.)

Again on musicians, this time the visiting rockers the Small Faces and the Who: 'Then we have the spectacle of these crummy hooligans misbehaving themselves.'

On seeing the Prime Minister: 'If Senator Gorton wants to see me he knows where to find me.' (February 1, 1970.)

And on his 62nd birthday: 'I've never felt fitter. What does age matter provided you feel all right. I've done a lot of quail shooting this year. I go home at the weekend. I get the dog and we walk miles.'

There's no nonsense of rumours of retirement with our Sir Henry. He's improving daily like an old bottle of Mount Pleasant.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The Wit of a Man Cartoonists Would Hate to Lose, The Bulletin, 27 June 1970. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
