If You've Got Wine, Who Needs Women and Song?

6 min read.

First published 21 Mar 1970

A report on the Rutherglen Wine Festival, which drew 20,000 visitors to a town of 1600, featuring free wine at the wineries, a street procession with a working still on the back of a truck, a barrel-rolling contest, hay-bale singing under the stars, and the election of a Vintage Queen from candidates including Miss Riesling, Miss Port and Miss Claret.

RUTHERGLEN is a dozing country town — population 1600. It has all its veranda posts and the awful Coles-Woolworths-Colonel Sanders civilisation has passed it by. Normally, dogs sleep in the main street and it would be a perfect set for 'Bellbird' or, even better, 'Blue Hills.'

Other towns, almost with tears in their eyes, plead for public attention. They turn on their Buttercup, Begonia, Rose Festivals, their Hay Frolics, their Apple Jamborees, never make a line in the metropolitan dailies and hardly rate a visitor beyond grandmother calling to see her young.

But put the simple word 'wine' in front of your festival and you get an extraordinarily warm-hearted response. I understand that back in the last century, during the local goldrush, citizens moved to Rutherglen with an astonishing vigour. But never was it anything like this. During the 1968 festival the city fathers went pale when there was an invasion of nigh on 8000. This time 20,000 came to Rutherglen.

The score for the weekend was 106 arrests, three killed and six injured on the roads within five miles of Rutherglen, plus a breaking and entering of a hotel. The president of the festival, George Sutherland Smith, jun., said: 'You can't promote a festival like this without someone doing the wrong thing. If you work it out, the wrongdoers amounted to .05 percent of the people who came.'

Your correspondent is not in a position to comment on the arrests. Most of them were on the Sunday night, when the great wine flow of more than 2500 gallons had ceased, but here is what I saw through my unstained blue eyes.

Rutherglen, for a start, put a great steel framework on its water tower so that it looked like a gigantic wine glass. Shop after shop put out displays of local wines. The hairdresser had bottles of wine among the curling pins and wiglets. The baker had special tawny port with the lamingtons and buns. Commercial Insurance Co. Ltd. had Campbell's aged muscat on display — and on receiving one's festival tickets at the office, one also bought gear for the weekend. This included a Festival Foreign Legion Cap.

Then there was the Festival Bib. This was a brilliant orange affair with a pocket in the front which took the Festival Wineglass. So, like a Western cowboy, one always had the essential equipment at hand. Should a bottle be thrust out, then the glass was at the ready. In the three days there was no opportunity to wash it and a noble red crust developed on the glass.

On Friday there was a formal ball. On Saturday morning there was the wine auction, pleasantly staged in the sun by the palm trees outside Seppelts. The wines sold for an average of close to $4 a bottle.

At 12.30 p.m. there was the smorgasbord lunch in the Memorial Hall. On one wall there was a frightful picture of the war memorial picture Midnight at Menin Gate. Then in between they had posters of our local wines. On the stage there was an all-female band, Alma Quon and Her Joy Belles. As we ate our turkey and salad, Alma played 'Lil' Ole Wine Drinkin' Me.'

By 3 p.m. things were moving. The corners of Main and High Streets were a solid mass of people, mostly 24 and under. The bikies had taken over one corner and were sitting in the gutter in front of the Star Hotel. If one wasn't in a leather jacket, the ideal gear was T-shirt, jeans and bare feet. Over the road the Victoria Hotel had a sign out recommending a visit to the Rutherglen Wine Cellar. Down there they were selling bottled wine and festival T-shirts, with a boozy cherub Bacchus on the chest.

Outside, one young gentleman was to be seen bugling down a half-gallon flagon of muscat. Some were sound asleep on the pavement. The crowds went out to the wineries by the thousand. At Seppelts they were serving glasses of sherry and some characters took their glasses outside and filled up bottles. Some were sitting under the barrels singing.

Maybe you have been to All Saints' winery, a lovely old place, battlements and all. There were cars banked solid all up the Avenue of Elms and out along the Wahgunyah Road. George Campbell had a staff of 35 serving wine, plus a young lady at a microphone. She had a guitar and soothingly she droned 'Kisses Sweeter Than Wine' — a very bold claim around Rutherglen.

At 4 p.m. there was a street procession, which included a truck with a still on the back. The suggestion was that this could be the ideal method of solving the wheat glut. There was a grape-picking contest, a barrel-rolling contest and, in the evening, an outdoor barbecue on the sports oval. Just possibly, this was the best event of them all. There's a perfection and brilliance about the nights in north-eastern Victoria. The wine was 10 cents a glass. The claret was served in jugs, and the white was straight out of the barrel by Pluto tap.

One sat on hay bales, ate steak and chicken, listened to pop groups on the stage. At 10.30 there was a fuss when, according to the law, the wine went off. Someone set alight one of the hay bales. A quick-thinking festival man smartly put out the blaze with wine. This infuriated some wine-lovers. They thought it miserable that a fire could get a drink after 10.30 and they could not.

On Sunday there was another open-air night affair with the roasting of an ox on the spit. This followed a morning symposium, with a formal reading of papers, while out at the racecourse there was a rodeo, wood-chopping, parachute jumping and the judging of the Vintage Queen. Among the candidates there were Miss Riesling, Miss Port and Miss Claret. Very interesting titles: one would have expected Miss Riesling to be clean and fresh, Miss Port to be bold and tawny, and Miss Claret, as a north-eastern girl, to have plenty of body. As for Miss Vintage, she should have been well matured in the wood.
