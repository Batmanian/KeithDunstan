Revolution upstages the border war

4 min read.

First published 29 Mar 1983

The Victorian football season opens with a state-of-war atmosphere between Victoria and South Australia over player transfers, while upheaval at Collingwood — a new president from South Yarra, a new captain recruited from South Australia, and a spectacular Guernsey Night — overshadows the interstate dispute.

THE VICTORIAN football season is starting off in a beautiful, classical manner. It is clear that a state of war almost exists between Victoria and South Australia.

Malcolm Blight, superstar, has returned to his home town of Adelaide to play for Woodville. North Melbourne is refusing to let him go.

The National Football League has come down on the side of Victoria. South Australia is threatened with disaffiliation from the NFL. It looks as if the rival States will refuse to play each other come next May and the South Australians have imposed a minimum $100,000 transfer fee on any player going east across the border.

But that is a minor affair. Football in Victoria really is an in-growing toenail. Nothing else exists. Matches against other States are tiresome affairs which interfere with the local round of club matches.

More interesting to us are the cataclysmic events at Collingwood.

Collingwood — according to Collingwood — is the greatest, largest, sporting club in the country. It is indisputably working-class, an area of high-density housing, a nest for laborers and boot manufacturers in particular.

By tradition, Collingwood players have come from Collingwood. They learned to play the game by kicking home-made footballs, bundles of newspapers tied with string.

Tradition? The Royal Horse Artillery didn't have a tradition as great as that of Collingwood. To play for the Mighty Magpies was the ultimate ambition. A Magpie had guts, fearlessness and never gave up.

The Collingwood clubrooms boast so many pictures of great players of the past that you feel you have to cross yourself wherever you walk.

The club has won 13 premierships and been runner-up more than 20 times. But things are grave. You can't recruit there any more. Collingwood is a suburb full of New Australians, people who don't speak English. Indeed, a Magpie born in Collingwood is now a rarity.

Their two premierships in the 1950s are their latest and in 1982 — oh, horror! — they finished 10th.

A new term came into use for the troubles at Collingwood — "the Colliwobbles."

Season's end brought ructions, a spill and a landslide on the committee which even now make the end of Malcolm Fraser seem a minor matter.

The members elected as their new president Ranald Macdonald, managing director of David Syme & Co, MA Cambridge, M.Sc Columbia, USA, but, worst of all, utterly non-Collingwood. He came from South Yarra.

Events have been moving speedily ever since. The football club has had a recruiting campaign which must run to more than $1 million and last week brought the famous "Guernsey Night" at the social club.

This time, incredibly, women were allowed into the club; wives and sweethearts and mothers sat down to dinner in a gathering of more than 1000 people — an occasion so vast they had to run it on three floors with gigantic TV screens so that all could participate.

They had called in Mike Brady, the man who wrote Up There Cazaly, the man who wrote the song for the latest campaign of the Liberals. He had rephrased the Collingwood song, timed it a little slower, and he put it over with a depth of emotion which made all feel that they were on the eve of an even greater battle than Agincourt. The mighty Brady voice, backed by several thousand watts of power, intoned:

Well, we all know that this team's a legend It's the biggest and the best — that's understood So we give our support to the Kings of the sport So go out and win, Collingwood.

Before The Conferring of the Guernseys, Macdonald announced the captain and vice-captain for 1983. This had been a preciously guarded secret.

The new captain was Mark Williams who came to Collingwood from South Australia in 1981 and the new vice-captain David Cloke, 28, the former Richmond skipper who had joined Collingwood at a transfer cost of $130,000.

Then we had The Conferring of the Guernseys. There were 53 and this is the greatest honour in Magpieland. It is like the conferring of degrees at Melbourne University.

Tony Charlton called out the names one by one, listed each candidate's brilliant past and physical skills, and Macdonald handed over each guernsey to general applause.

Lou Richards commented: "You didn't get a new guernsey every year in my day; you'd have to wear the same one for at least six years.

"It'd shrink so much, you'd have to cut it under the armpits to get the darned thing on."
