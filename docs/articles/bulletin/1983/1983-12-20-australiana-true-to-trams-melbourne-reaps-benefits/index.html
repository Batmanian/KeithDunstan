True to trams: Melbourne reaps benefits

5 min read.

First published 20 Dec 1983

Melbourne's loyalty to its tram network is paying off as the city exports vintage W-class trams to Seattle and San Francisco, Elton John buys one for his garden, and a new book celebrates Australia's tramway heritage.

TRAM LOVE, I understand, can be cured. Turn your car into Toorak Road from Park Street about 5 pm and get behind a tram. You will sit there, fuming, stop after stop after stop, all the way to Glenferrie Road.

Or, even better, get into Sydney Road, Brunswick. They travel in bunches of four out there. You will be half way to Sydney before you pass the darned things.

You must appreciate, however, that I was born in Melbourne and my devotion to trams runs deep. We breed tram nuts who are worse than train nuts or even vintage motorcycle nuts. They buy recordings of lovable tram noises and play them to each other the way normal people play Beethoven sonatas.

Make one error in a newspaper article about trams, just get your mark number wrong or commit some trifling mistake regarding the electrification or braking systems and you will get more letters than if you made a ghastly howler in the lineage of Phar Lap.

Melbourne is one of the few places on Earth which is still expanding its tramway system and we are spending so much money on trams right now we have obsolete models to spare. We are a superb antique shop for old trams.

Dudley Snell, the Melbourne Tramway chief, says there are only two places in the world where you can buy antique trams: Melbourne and Lisbon. What's more, even Lisbon seems to have cut out. Almost certainly, Melbourne has the market on its own.

So we are selling trams to other cities. You know the elegant old green and gold jobs, the W class which date back to the 1920s? We sold three of those to Seattle. They wanted a tourist line along their waterfront.

Go to Seattle, rub your eyes; the trams look so familiar you would swear you were in St Kilda Road. Those trams have been such a success, Seattle is expanding the line. They want another three. The original price was $6000 each plus about $21,000 for transportation. It's going up now. The going price for an all-complete W is $10,000.

San Francisco is in trouble over its cable trams. The line will be out of action for more than a year. The city bought one of our W trams to run in Market Street. They loved it; a wonderful tram, according to the experts there. Now they are buying another 15. It's so comforting that we are helping them to become a tourist attraction.

Elton John, would you believe, announced that he wanted one of our trams as a summer house to put in his garden in Berkshire. A publicity stunt, I thought at the time. But, no, I understand that his cheque for $10,000 has arrived.

Too cheap, of course. The trams are available now only because the city is switching over to the fancy Z-class variety. But the old Ws are becoming rare. They should at least fetch the price of an old Renoir.

Now tram buffs should be apprised of the news that a great book has just come on the market: The Tramways of Australia, by Samuel Brimson (Dreamweaver Books, $24.95). Not only is it written with a very sensible prejudice in favour of trams but the research is deep and the photographs are glorious. You can relive old Baldwin steam motors trundling along Elizabeth Street, Sydney, charming horse-drawn double deckers in Adelaide and, good grief, a little Birney in Bendigo.

The Birney cars came out from the US during the Depression of the 1930s. As the US scrapped all its tram-car systems, Bendigo became the world's last repository of Birneys. Honestly, I know of special American tours to Bendigo just to see those trams.

Brimson lists Brisbane, Sydney, Newcastle, Hobart, Launceston, Geelong, Bendigo, Ballarat, Adelaide, Perth, Fremantle and Kalgoorlie as the tram cities. Adelaide had the finest horse-drawn trams and Sydney was best for steam. Melbourne boasted nearly the finest cable network on Earth.

The trams were triumphant until the end of World War II. Brimson writes with bitterness: "The trams were a product of the 19th century and in the brave new consumer-based society spawned in the 1950s anything pre-war was considered undesirable and ripe for extinction. During those years and the 1960s Australia went on an obscene orgy of destruction, tearing its cities apart and sacrificing public amenity on the altar of motorism, in a mad quest for so-called modernity. During the 1950s the motoring lobby had the loudest voice in Australian cities and numerous governments capitulated to its verbal assaults."

Destruction of the immense Sydney system began in 1956 and during the 50s the executioners also went through Newcastle, Kalgoorlie, Fremantle, Brisbane and Launceston. Geelong ended in 1956. Hobart succumbed in 1970. Ballarat and Bendigo went in 1972 and only Melbourne, "the shining beacon," remained.

Brimson rhapsodises about Melbourne. He says: "To those people throughout the world who claim to truly love trams the City of Melbourne is a jewel . . . the most complete tramway system in the English-speaking world."

Yet, he says, the Melbourne Tramway authorities had to carry on in the face of thunderous opposition from newspaper editorials. They thought the trams laughable and trumpeted progress every time a city scrapped its trams. The motor car was God.

But the trams survived and the Tramway Board was proved impeccably right when it called them the best of all crowd-movers. Says Brimson: "There are many visitors for whom a trip to Melbourne is not complete without a ride on a tram; and the sight of a continuous line of green-and-cream or orange tramcars in the main streets of Melbourne during the morning or afternoon peak hour is a sight to bring joy to the heart of any tram lover."
