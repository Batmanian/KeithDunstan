On Holey Ground

5 min read.

First published 02 Dec 1972

A tribute to Melbourne's hole-watching tradition, tracing the city's rivalry with Sydney's famous Chevron 'Golden Hole', celebrating the new 60-foot Mainline Corporation dig at the Collins Street end, and noting that Whelan the Wrecker's demolition of a whole block has finally given Melbourne a hole worth admiring.

FOR the dedicated enthusiast hole watching has always been a most satisfying pastime and for years now Sydney's holes have always been superior to those of Melbourne. One thinks of the famous Chevron hole at Kings Cross, known as the 'Golden Hole.' It is 66 feet deep and covers two acres. Back in the dreamy optimistic days of Chevron the management planned to build a magnificent new wing, a wing which has never taken to the air, so to speak.

Back in 1962 it cost $420,000 to dig that hole. The cost of digging it today would be nigh on two million. So you see the hole is constantly going up in value. They could well take note of that famous song 'Digging Holes' by Flanagan and Allen. If they cut up their big hole into little holes and sold them off to other people who needed holes, then they would make a huge profit.

Qantas down near Circular Quay had a marvellous hole, 100 feet deep, in solid sandstone. And then there was the NSW Institute of Technology hole on Broadway. I have watched that hole for many years and a fine hole it has been, 532 feet by 328 and 42 feet deep. Over 340,000 tons came out of it. Usually it has been full of water and some hole lovers have gone boating in it. Alas, it is ruined now with the beginnings of a building.

But the big news is that Melbourne has caught up and we have holes even superior to Sydney holes. There is the hole at Jolimont where the workmen have the open cut for the underground railroad. Here you can get a very worthwhile matinee display with an all-star range of activity. At the bottom of the hole there is concreting, bulldozer work, crane lifting and even railway line re-laying.

However we have something much better than that. The area at the top end of Collins Street once was absurdly called the 'Paris end'; now it would be better known as the holey end. This year Whelan the Wrecker destroyed the block bounded by Collins and Exhibition Streets and Flinders Lane, leaving an extraordinary open space, a gap akin to the Red Square in Moscow.

In this gap we will get $100 million worth of towers — the ANZ tower, 45 storeys, and the Collins Place Tower, 52 storeys. Actually by the careful arrangement of floors the two towers will end up the same height.

For the hole watcher all this is splendid. Right now Mainline Corporation is digging the biggest city hole in the country and already it is bigger than the Chevron hole.

Now they are down to 45 feet and they will go to an average depth of 60 feet with some areas down to 80 feet. But for hole watchers the show is the best in town. They have 35 ten-yard trucks, and a 25-ton bulldozer which feeds a monster of a five-yard front-end loader.

The front-end loader takes two scoops to feed a truck, but the driver of the loader is a great artist. Such economy of movement — his machine never makes an unnecessary move and he never misses, not even by a crumb. He drops his 10 yards with exquisite accuracy into the back of the truck and each time he uses the enormous scoop to pat the load down, as if he were putting the finishing touch to a sandcastle. Lovely.

The hoardings are interesting. Once upon a time you would put up a wretched paling fence. Now the competition is immense. As yet Melbourne hasn't equalled the spectacular non-figurative artwork that was used on the hoarding for the Kingsgate Hotel in Kings Cross, but the Mainline job here is nice. The hoarding consists of individual panels, all different colours. The architect wanted to continue the boutique feeling of Collins Street.

Site offices also have come on. In the old days they used to be lean-to sheds of galvanised iron. Mainline is building its own. You would think it is putting up flats for the Housing Commission. They are 35 feet high, and most are air-conditioned.

They go down the entire Exhibition Street flank. On the other side there was the problem of the famous Collins Street plane trees. The project director, Bruce Connor, said: 'We had to draw up plans taking in every leaf.'

In Melbourne you can pollute the sea, burn down a few schools, even break a couple of bridges and the city takes it calmly, but harm a Collins Street tree and the outraged public is ready to march. So they have built an intricate scaffolding, designed to accommodate every twig. The pedestrian walk is reminiscent of the new competitive era. It has a charming arched church roof.

As for the hole watchers, there are already windows at various heights for viewers, but the plan is to put in two proper viewing platforms, one in Collins Street and the other in Exhibition. This is good to see. In recent years there has been a sad decline in facilities for hole watchers.

The effect of holes is interesting. Bruce Connor says that before they started they had to make a minute survey of all the buildings for blocks around. They didn't want clever lawyers blaming them for every tiny crack and lean. They have instruments attached to all the surrounding buildings designed to detect all movements and shudders. They are particularly anxious that none of their neighbours slide into the hole.

Holewatchers are watching with keen interest. Nothing will be above ground until about May or June next year. And we have been promised a continuity of holes. Any time now, just up the way in Exhibition Street we will get the hole for the 45-storey Nauru building. That promises to be a beautiful hole.
