Random Notes of a Citizen in Gloom

5 min read.

First published 19 Feb 1972

Melbourne's SEC power strike of February 1972, observed from the premiere of a new Australian play in darkness to Sir Henry Bolte backing winners at the Pakenham Races while the city's air conditioners and swimming pools sat idle.

COULD future historians write the random notes of a citizen of Melbourne living in the Bolte dynasty early in February, 1972? To begin with there was the recent premiere of the Australian play, 'Talk to the Moon,' by Pete Kenna, held in the distinguished presence of the Governor of Victoria, Sir Rohan Delacombe.

The theatre operated on emergency power and one had to be led to one's seat by an usherette with a torch. Time and again the suffering audience was plunged into total darkness. The story was of Jack Heath and his merciless nagging wife Florrie. They had a sort of occasion. They invented new lines for the play. Out of the darkness we heard: 'Dunno what's wrong with you, Jack. I think the candles is the ideal.' (Cheers from the audience.) 'Think so, Florrie, do you?' (Applause.) Yet most shows seemed to carry on regardless.

Ido was still offering 'Wine, dine and dance until 3 a.m., lunchtime strip shows for worried businessmen.' Historians note that in crisis week they had 'Direct from Jamaica Miss Montiego, the world's biggest bosom. Miss 52in. (four inches bigger than Alexandra the Great) plus five of the best nude girls, plus two show bands from Tokyo and the Philippines presenting the best of Jesus Christ Superstar.'

In crisis week we had the visit of the President of Indonesia, General Suharto. It gave great joy to every cartoonist. He went to a reception at Government House where the power was provided by emergency equipment brought in by the Public Works Department. The 'Australian' reported that someone let loose three stink bombs in the midst of the reception. 'Visitors, diplomats and politicians held their heads high in the air and ignored the odor.' The CIB was called in the next day and the smell actually was not caused by stink bombs. It was the plastic covering which burned off the overheating party bulbs.

But obviously the strike was upsetting the 'Australian' also, for it printed the same 'For those who trust the stars' columns on the Tuesday and the Wednesday. This must have caused some wonder to Bob Hawke (Sagittarius) for on the Wednesday he received an anonymous phone call threatening his death. His stars advised him: 'Improve appearance. Brighten surroundings. More persons now become aware of you.'

Sir Henry Bolte (Taurus the bull) had: 'Emotions tend to dominate. Express yourself, but don't play games.' But what was he doing on Wednesday? Getting death threats? He was at the Pakenham Races. He backed Fantasiane in the third race and earned 15-to-one. Then he presented the Pakenham Cup to Charles Gawith, owner of the triumphant Special Boy. There were a few hoo-hahs from the crowd. Someone remarked: 'When Sir Henry's about there's no trouble of any kind whatsoever.'

The power restrictions were quite interesting. Air conditioning units and swimming pool filtration systems were banned. The temperatures for most of that week hovered in the nineties. The new flats in Toorak had the most superbly architected death traps, provided with windows of tinted glass. Like the windows in the old Spirit of Progress they don't open. So during crisis week some of the nicest people blossomed in an atmosphere suited for orchids. Then they went home to their swimming pools which day by day became increasingly murky, ideal perhaps for growing lilies.

Stores like Myers, McEwans and such continued to operate their escalators, but only the up ones. This meant that you could be transported up with comfort but to get out you had to use your legs. Psychologically very crafty.

The latest thing now, as a sort of survival kit, is to put in your own home generator. You can get a nice little Japanese portable unit for as little as $595. Hawker Siddeley recommended a more powerful 10 kilowatt unit for $1320. Weight 200lb., 20 gallon fuel tank, runs for 10 years without problems. But its real advantage was this: an automatic cut-in device. As soon as your SEC power falls, within seconds your emergency unit has turned itself on and your refrigerator, swimming pool and so on are safe.

There have been other agonies. The coin-operated laundrettes have been out of action. The dry cleaners haven't been re-working. The shoe repairer down the road has had idle machines. The city has had a lovely dull glow, most of the electric advertising signs, and those electric jump clocks which somehow seem to announce the inexorable approach of doom, all dead.

It was a curious strike. The unions announced very early that all the plugs would come out, there would be no power blackout. Yet from the beginning the community showed a remarkable unflappability. Never have I seen restrictions so frequently ignored. Street lights burned all night. Shops continued to floodlight their windows, trams kept running. One suspects that the automation the union fears so much is already here. The truth is Sir Henry can now run the whole damn lot from the Pakenham Races.

Source: out and about with Batman: Random Notes of a Citizen in Gloom, The Bulletin, 19 February 1972. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
