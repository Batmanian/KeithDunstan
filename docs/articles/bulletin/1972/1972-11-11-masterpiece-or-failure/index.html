Masterpiece or Failure

5 min read.

First published 11 Nov 1972

A report on the National Gallery's purchase of Jean-Robert Ipousteguy's 'La Mort du Pere' for $60,000, in which Patrick McCaughey called it 'bloody silly', Eric Westbrook called it 'absolutely stunning', Fred Williams said it was 'all a bit bloody queer', and an anonymous Melbourne Revolutionary Surrealist Society donated a rival sculpture on a bullock dray.

MELBOURNE is a city which normally only gets disturbed over deeply important matters — the quality of edible shark, the price of meat pies and the sentences handed out to footballers by the tribunal on Tuesday nights. So it is awfully good to see the citizens in a state of conflicting fury over a piece of modern sculpture.

Right now the sculpture is in a large darkened room at the north end of the Arts Centre. A spotlight shines down on a large, glistening figure of a naked man poised over a tomb and the death head of a pope. Scattered around are the skulls of generations of previous popes.

It is called 'La mort du Pere' by the French artist Jean-Robert Ipoustegui. The hand-out distributed by the National Gallery calls it 'the most important piece of contemporary art yet purchased in Australia.' It is also the most expensive; but we are told it was a bargain: $60,000 reduced from $85,000.

The caustic young critic of the Age, Patrick McCaughey, described the purchase as 'bloody silly.' He said: 'The sculpture itself, in gleaming white marble on aluminium canisters, is just a stagy spectacle. The central sprawling nude figure gazes with suitable puzzlement into the face of the Godfather. Around them are a chorus of the gnomes of Zurich, in mitres with decaying faces. What can one say about such vulgarity? The slickness is astonishing as the marble drips and shines like plastic. But the slickness is nothing to the corniness.'

In a rising tide of eloquence he compared it to the footy Bronlow medal presentation, to the Moomba parade, to the figures by Madame Tussaud. 'How could the gallery get suckered into this one?' said he.

One correspondent to the Age wrote that Michelangelo was very lucky he didn't have McCaughey about at the time when he finished his David. Mr N. R. Seddon, president of the gallery trustees, wrote that one could have predicted McCaughey's reaction with considerable accuracy. 'Does he really have to be so discourteous in his comments,' said Seddon. 'To write of the gallery as being "bloody silly" is gratuitously offensive; to refer to "extravagant and ignorant squandering" is equally offensive and suggests a claim to omniscience which is totally unwarranted.'

Alan McCulloch in the Herald wasn't too sure whether it was a meaningful masterpiece or a magnificent failure. Jeffrey Makin in the Sun thought it a 'great work,' 'a breathtaking experience,' 'a work that exists beside those of Rodin, Moore, Rembrandt, Carracci and Bonnard.'

Fred Williams was quoted as saying: 'It's just doubling up on what we already have. It's all a bit bloody queer.' As for the director of the gallery, Eric Westbrook, he said: 'It is an absolute stunning, marvellous work.' And he added: 'I appreciate its realism is a bit of a shock to the Angel Sculpture School, but you've got to have both.'

The cost of the sculpture represents 300,000 20-cent entry fees, but the controversy has been splendid for business. When your correspondent was there the crowds were practically queueing up to get in. One young lady said: 'I like it. It reminds me of that big marble job in Rome. You know the one I mean, the big thing with the fig leaf.'

The sculpture was bought on the recommendation of Miss Annette Dixom, the gallery's curator of European and American art after 1800. She has had a number of telephone calls, some from people with Irish accents. One woman said: 'It's scandalous that it portrays Archbishop Mannix.' (Mannix himself might have been flattered by this sudden posthumous elevation to pope.)

McCaughey returned to the battle. He quoted figures on how little the Melbourne National Gallery had spent on Australian works over the past seven years. He quoted Daniel Thomas of the Art Gallery of New South Wales with the line that of all the state galleries save Tasmania, Victoria had spent the least on the works of living Australian artists in the past 12 months. The figure was $4000. Even the Newcastle city art gallery had doubled that amount.

Mr Seddon's reply to this was: 'Whether Mr McCaughey likes it or not, the indiscriminate acquisition of works of art simply because they are Australian is not and never has been the gallery's primary objective. The fact remains that we have by far the most significant collection of world art in Australia.'

Then in the midst of all this the Herald received an anonymous telephone call. A man said: 'I am from the Melbourne Revolutionary Surrealist Society and we have presented the gallery with a sculpture.'

The sculpture was nine feet high and was parked on a four-wheeled bullock dray outside the Arts Centre. It depicted a monstrous Dobellian figure lying on its back, limbs in distorted positions. Or was it really a huge version of Drysdale's 'Woman in a Landscape,' knees up high and wide apart, about to give birth?

Actually it was called 'Death of Capitalism.' It was made from papier mache and painted in smears of red, green and black. Also on the dray was a brick, petrol can and a tuft of grass. There were also gaping wounds in the side depicting the flow of blood.

The police were a little confused by the whole thing. These times you have to be terribly careful as to what might be a work of art. Finally they decided it was an obscene object and had it carted away.

Eric Westbrook was thrilled. This was just the extra boost he needed. 'La mort du Pere' is now a bigger drawcard than Bazza McKenzie.

Source: out and about with Batman: Masterpiece or failure, The Bulletin, 11 November 1972. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
