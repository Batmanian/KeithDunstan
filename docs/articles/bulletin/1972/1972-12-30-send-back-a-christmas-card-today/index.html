Send Back a Christmas Card Today

5 min read.

First published 30 Dec 1972

A ruthless knocking attack on the commercial Christmas machine, tracing the year's selling seasons from January sales through Mother's Day to Cup Week, announcing the formation of the Scrooge Society of Australia to abolish Christmas altogether, and rehabilitating Ebenezer Scrooge as a visionary done in by bad public relations.

This is a ruthless, knocking attack on Christmas. If your stomach is too full of office-party goodwill to take it, then you had better move elsewhere.

I've had it. Actually, the year is very craftily divided commercially into five sections. On the first shopping day after Christmas you get the January sales. While you are actually eating your Christmas dinner there are poor suffering display men working in the department stores, tearing down Father Christmases and reindeer and getting ready for the year's biggest sale. It is then that they sell all the things you were conned into buying as Christmas presents, one third cheaper.

After the January sales we get the Easter selling. Myers has always felt that God and the Church designed the year pretty badly. What they need is a late Easter, say 20 April, with plenty of rain and snow around 13 April, forcing the citizens to buy new early-winter wardrobes. Sunshine right up to Easter is great economic cruelty.

From then on the city looks forward to Mother's Day, the first Sunday of May. Mother's Day constantly builds, working up very nicely to a mini Christmas. Father's Day, the first Sunday in September is better than it used to be, but needs much promotion. The phallic bottles of after-shave and deodorant have been a help this year, but fathers never pick up as much sympathy as mothers.

Then in Melbourne we get Cup Week. A very good department store time and the stores have a firm non-aggression pact: you don't go over the top with Christmas until after the Cup, on the first Tuesday in November. This year the Cup was as late as it could be, the first Tuesday was the seventh. So even before we had decently shed our money on the Melbourne Cup, the Christmas season was on.

So there in Bourke St in the beginning of November, we had holly, Christmas lights, a mechanical Santa, reindeer, artificial snow and all. The official city decorations came a little later. Robin Boyd, in a revolt against what we do to cities with festive tinsel, bells, Santas and such, once called them urban dandruff, a good name.

There has been a small revolt in our council over the money spent on decorations, so this Christmas we have been blessed with exactly the same decorations as last Christmas. They went up in the hard shopping commercial core — Flinders, Collins, Bourke, Swanston and Elizabeth Streets. They have an orange theme, large oranges pinned together side by side and great fructive displays, oranges on wires all leaping out of a central bucket. It is all being construed as a tribute to Mildura and the citrus industry, but seeing they have had it for the second year running, the Victorian Egg Board and the Apple and Pear Board are likely to make claims for a hand in the Christmas decorations next year.

But Christmas gets tougher, more agonising, more full of strain. You notice it in the tortured faces of mothers dragging their children through Myers, Buckleys and Georges, buying the phallic bottles of after-shave that they can't afford. And it is very interesting, all the Christmas parties are back this Christmas, stronger, more aggressive than ever. Last year many of the companies sent pious notes — they would not hold a party, the money they normally spent would go to the starving. And it was interesting what they spent on their Christmas fun and games — some of them went to $1500.

They are all back this time. I actually have 30 invitations on my desk right now. One, an advertising agency, reports that the party goes from sundown until dawn and it includes a numbered card 'For your lucky present under the tree.'

It's all too much. How can the system stand it? We have really only just finished the 1971 Christmas and now we have to start the 1972. I have a theory that the year is being ruined by inflation, that no longer are we getting the true value out of our year. If you look at it carefully the year is being devalued by a possible 4.2 per cent every year. I am not very good at mathematics, but clearly the years we are getting now are only a third of the value of the years we used to get in the nineteen forties. That is, you need three 1970 years to be as good as a 1940 year.

Do you see what I am getting at? It would be sensible to run Christmas only every third year, and it would be like old times. We would be stronger, better equipped to cope with it.

Actually we might even go further than that. Here in Melbourne we have launched an organisation called the Scrooge Society of Australia. Its object is to abolish Christmas altogether. Scrooge has been chosen as patron because he was a very hardly-done-by figure.

It is well known that Lucretia Borgia was not as bad as she was painted. Richard III merely suffered from bad public relations and it is the same with Ebenezer Scrooge. He was the first to realise the terrible suffering that Christmas caused in the community and until Dickens forced some strange spiritualist tricks on him, he had some sound ideas.

The society already is gathering a remarkable and huge following and by 1973 is expected to go international with offices in 5th Avenue, Regent St, and with paid advertisements in the Neiman Marcus Christmas catalogue. Our slogans are 'Relieve Santa,' 'Send Back a Christmas Card Today.'

We would give all the department stores four weeks off, starting on 15 December. They might even be relieved.

And all the best to you and yours.
