The Playing Fields of Oakleigh Motel

5 min read.

First published 01 Jul 1972

A visit to the Victorian War Games Association's re-enactment of the Battle of Waterloo, staged with thousands of model soldiers at the Oakleigh Motel, where Napoleon was played by the restaurant manager and Wellington by an excitable computer programmer.

I HAVE always had an admiration, even a love for people who devote an enormous portion of their waking hours to doing things which are of no practical purpose whatsoever. Take, for example, the gentlemen who have written careful and beautifully detailed theses on the life of Sherlock Holmes. Or those who have produced books on Greyfriars School, its history, character studies on everyone there from the headmaster, Mr Quelch, down to Billy Bunter. I have in front of me the height, ages, and weights of all the students in the lower fourth and even the study lay-out plus occupants at Greyfriars. What a gloriously unnecessary piece of trivia!

The gentlemen of the Victorian War Games Association are of a similar bent. They re-fight the great battles of history using — ah — I was about to say toy soldiers, but that wouldn't do at all — model soldiers is the term. The soldiers are 20 millimetres, less than an inch high. A true war games enthusiast insists on decorating his soldiers in precisely correct colours, uniform, etc., right down to individual regiments. It can take up to five hours a soldier to do this.

So when a man's private army consists of several thousand troops you can appreciate the enormity of the unnecessary task.

Two months of intense preparation took place for the Battle of Waterloo. This is almost twice as much time as Napoleon had in 1815. The scene for the 1972 battle was the Burgundy Room at the Oakleigh Motel. In the centre of the room was an immense table turned into an historic re-creation of the old battlefield, complete with houses, towns, correct terrain and the regiments of Napoleon and Wellington arrayed for battle.

They had a public address system which early on played awfully hearty military marches, but as the battle got under way this switched to a sound track of cannon fire, blood-stirring bugle calls and even heroic soldier-like shouts.

Normally at conventions and such it is done to have a label on your chest which says 'Hello, I'm Winthrop Jones.' Here all the members were labelled with their job in the battle. We had Napoleon, the Duke of Wellington, the French chief of staff, Lieutenant General D'Erlon, the Prussian commander in chief, General Bulow and so on.

Napoleon — Max Hatrick, the manager of the restaurant — had a private table at the back of the room. In 1815 he was to the rear of the battle, so it was appropriate that he should be at the rear here also.

He commanded the Marshals of the French Army through runners and he studied troop movements through field glasses.

At first I was a little timid about seeking an interview with Napoleon. After all, the Battle of Waterloo was under way and he was frightfully involved with tactics, troop movements, issuing orders and such. But the magic words The Bulletin were sufficient to pass me through his aides.

He was a dark man of strong features, not unlike the famous pictures. He said: 'You must understand anything can happen today. We do everything correct historically, the same terrain, the same troop line up, but from then on we use our own tactics. We'll win today. Napoleon nearly always does. I used to take part in war games in England and Napoleon invariably won the Battle of Waterloo. I mean, he should have in 1815. Everything was going for him. Wellington just had an immense amount of luck.'

Max Hatrick went on to say that he had given his marshals orders last week, but security wasn't good. So he had given them all sealed orders just before the start of the battle at 10 am, changing the tactics entirely.

'I must destroy the British army quickly,' he said. 'Much depends on how quickly the Prussians arrive. They are now 20 miles off and if I can reduce Wellington's army by 50 per cent before they get here victory is mine.'

Your correspondent was in a privileged position. Immediately after talking to Napoleon he was taken to meet the Duke of Wellington. The Duke was Paul Staff, a computer programmer and one thought just a little gay and excitable for the role. In moments of triumph he tended to stand on a chair and cheer.

His Grace kindly explained how it all worked. The battle was like chess, except that there were over 3000 figures on the board and all could be moved at once. There was also an element of luck; at each move there was a throw of dice. For example, the time of arrival of the Prussians would be governed by the dice.

The firing of cannon and musket was immensely complicated, worked out through a combination of equipment used by different regiments on the day, terrain, angle of fire, plus chance through the throw of dice. 'We even take into account the bounce of cannon balls. Oh yes, they did bounce, very high in the air.'

Oh, the battle went on and on, right through the day. Bonaparte was depending heavily on his crack light infantry troops making a brilliant break-through on the left, but the attack was never pushed hard enough and it failed. His cavalry on the right fought well but just as they charged Wellington anticipated his move and moved forward strong regiments of foot. Then at 3 pm the Prussians arrived. Bonaparte switched his cavalry in a suicide charge to delay Bulow for just a few hours. Meanwhile he launched the rest of his cavalry in a death-or-glory charge straight down the centre. Wellington at that very moment did precisely the same thing. By 5 pm there was barely a mounted horseman left alive. Terrible, it was.

So failing all along the line with Bulow now ready to attack, Napoleon withdrew his troops from the field and Wellington, surprisingly, had won the Battle of Waterloo.

All sorts of possibilities come up now. I would like to put money on Agincourt, Hastings, Bannockburn, the Boyne, Crecy, but even more exciting would be to upgrade things a little.

How about planning a war game between the Commonwealth police and draft resisters?
