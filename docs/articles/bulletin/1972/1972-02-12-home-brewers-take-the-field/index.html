Home Brewers Take the Field

5 min read.

First published 12 Feb 1972

The home brewing boom that swept Melbourne following Carlton brewery's public objections to amateur beer-making in 1971, with fermenters filling refrigerators and the Olinda kit-maker reporting growth of several hundred percent.

THE humiliating sight in our street at the moment is to see some of our most respectable citizens on the prowl stealing empty beer bottles from rubbish tins. They do it soon after dawn when only house-breakers, joggers and milkmen are at large. It is hard on our garbage man because he uses his profits from his dead marine sideline to make his regular Pacific cruises aboard the Canberra.

Since the Carlton brewery people raised the fuss about home brewers back in May 1971 the homemakers have gone berserk. I don't think anyone realises just how much is produced. All my friends are doing it. They trot it along on Saturdays at tennis parties. Some of it is beautiful beer — you wouldn't fault it. But some tastes like sour alcoholic porridge and some seems to have a large section of the sludge of the Yarra in the bottom of the bottle. Some of it has a magnificent head but some is as still as the waters of Lake Como.

There is a special IN thing about it, like the formidable people out at Eltham who dress in kaftans, sing madrigals, pot and make their own bread. They have their own specially printed labels with very arch stuff in antique lettering: 'Thompson's Pale Ale.'

A lady down the road complained to me just yesterday that her husband had taken everything out of her refrigerator, the vegetables, the weekend roast, the lot. He had to fit in his five gallon plastic beer fermenter. With the current heatwave he couldn't get his brew temperature down to 75 degrees to start his yeaster working. 'The rest of the family could starve just as long as his beer was all right,' said she with superb acidity.

Well, I've just been taking note. A year ago it was no trouble buying home brew stuff, but you had to know the outlets. But now it is extraordinary — all the health food stores have it. You can buy it at the local hardware shop, at the delicatessen and any department store. Coles, McEwans and Myers all have big home brew departments.

The officer in command of home brew at Myers, a position not without importance, told me that there were now at least 10 firms which were producing home brew kits and the sales were remarkable. On his counter he had all the latest gear, which has become very refined. You could buy devices such as hydrometers for precisely measuring alcohol content, beautiful lever-action crown sealers, and plug-in electric devices for giving exact controlled temperature during fermentation.

Of course it is against the law in Australia to make an unlicensed beer of more than two percent proof spirit. Penalty, $200. All the kits point out this fact, but with beautiful subtlety every recipe explains between the lines how to make a beer of normal strength.

Several weeks back McEwans had a home brew week. They had the place done out to look like a cellar. They had home fermenters all over the place. They had personal appearances by Keith Linden who wrote 'Brewing Beer at Home.' His book has gone through four quick editions. Then they had a representative of one home brew firm giving talks and demonstrations. He had a beautiful line. He said: 'Ladies and Gentlemen, to conform with the Customs and Excise Act your home brew must be less than two percent proof spirit. The only way you can legally make home brew the same strength as you buy it at the hotel is to jump on an aeroplane and fly to New Zealand. There if you used one of our kits and added only half the quantity of water and double the sugar you would get, to brewery strength beer.'

And looking at the crowd assembled in the store he added: 'But none of us here would want to break the law by doing that in Melbourne . . . would we?' We looked at each other. Indeed we would not.

According to one survey, and that was nearly a year ago, 2,000,000 gallons of home brew is being made yearly in Victoria alone. I spoke to John Klose, managing director of Olinda, one of the largest of the home brew firms. He said he did not know how much was being sold in Australia: 'But I can tell you,' he said, 'we have expanded many hundred percent since this time last year. We have outlets in department stores all over Australia. The only people who don't sell our stuff here are Woolworths, but they do in Queensland. We are expanding so rapidly that we are even appointing a full time research scientist to work in his own laboratory.'

Your correspondent asked Mr Klose if any of his home brewers had suffered unnerving experiences: 'I'll never forget one pensioner,' he said. 'He liked getting his beer for about 7c a bottle. It was winter, it was cold and he couldn't get his beer to start fermenting. He'd call up every day. Still wouldn't work. Finally he was triumphant. "I've solved it," he said. "I've got the fermenter under the bed with the electric blanket." I laughed and laughed. He replied: "It's all very well for you to laugh young man, but at 73 even the sound of beer fermenting under the bed is a joy."'

'Then,' continued Mr Klose, 'we had the man in Perth who decided that he would make his own draught beer. So he fitted his fermenter with sparklets. He wasn't even in his own house when he did this and he had the fermenter in the living room. The fermenters are made out of plastic and they are not exactly strong. The gas pressure built up and the fermenter exploded, covering the living room chairs, the carpet, everything.

'We had a report too from New Guinea. A character couldn't wait to bottle his beer. As it finished fermenting after five or six days, he drank it all straight out of the fermenter. Heaven knows what it tasted like.'
