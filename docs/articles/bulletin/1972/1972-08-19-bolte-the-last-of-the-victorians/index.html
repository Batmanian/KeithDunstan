Bolte — The last of the Victorians

14 min read.

First published 19 Aug 1972

A profile of Henry Bolte on his retirement after 17 years as Victorian premier, arguing through assessments from Clyde Holding and Jim McDonald that Bolte's rough, anti-intellectual self-certainty was both his greatest political skill and a perfect mirror for the city he ruled.

MELBOURNE daily newspapers these past few weeks have clutched Sir Henry Bolte to their warm, very ample bosoms and literally smothered him with love. Even the Age, which has conducted a Bolte feud for years, said: 'Looking back, it is perhaps the greatest compliment we can pay him to say that we were from time to time both supporter and opponent and that in either role we admired him.'

The truth is that Victoria adored him. We don't always rise to great heights around here, there can be a porridge-like mediocrity about Melbourne, and over the years we have shown a remarkable capacity for creating and building larger than life characters. Dame Zara Holt was one, Percy Cerutty another, Arthur Calwell we gave an almost Dickensian quality, Ron Barassi too, and we loved the cheekiness of Lou Richards. Bob Hawke also is achieving more than hero status.

To understand this you need to understand Melbourne. Australians in theory are supposed to be rugged, sun-tanned, hard-swearing individualists. Actually we are far from rugged, far from individual and we are easily pushed around. That goes for Victorians in particular.

How else can you explain that for 80 years Melbourne was a sort of organised Sunday school camp, made to endure the most repressive, soul-destroying legislation? Our state politics for so many decades operated on such a pedestrian level that out of our meekness we react with a 'Thank-you, Sir' when at last someone comes who sounds like a leader.

Sir Henry was a leader, there's not the slightest doubt about that. In his 17 years as party leader he blossomed like a good cabernet wine. He became more and more of a character, Mr Melbourne, day by day. He became hugely amusing, and always quotable. What's more he knew this and his press conferences were always entertaining. Instant Talk Back with Sir Henry — ask him anything and you would get an answer.

Back in June 1962 The Times of London was so impressed with Henry Bolte it dug up that famous line from Anthony Trollope when he visited us in 1871: 'They blow a good deal in Queensland — a good deal in South Australia. They blow even in poor Tasmania, and very loudly in New Zealand. But the blast of the trumpet as heard in Victoria is louder than all the blasts — and the Melbourne blast beats all the other blowing of that proud colony.'

Mr Heffron, premier of New South Wales, agreed that Henry Bolte's blowing was in world class, on a par with that of 'Satchmo' Louis Armstrong.

Satchmo Bolte had a perfect sense of timing, and he always knew how to make a headline. One of his rare political errors was the hotel-motel 10 per cent bed tax. It was unworkable unless all other states joined and besides, 10 per cent was far too high. But just look at the way he dropped it. At 11.40am he left the party room and announced to reporters that the bed tax was scrapped. He was the premier in his noble role, the man big enough to admit he had made a mistake. Then at 12.45 Labor and Industry Minister Rafferty left the party room — in good time to make the Herald home edition — with the news that from now there would be an open go on night shopping, shops open every night of the week.

It was a beautiful counter punch. Number one story was totally smothered by number two.

Yes, he was a good lip man. I can't remember an occasion in 17 years when he was ever beaten by a strike, and Melburnians even looked forward to his comment, like the time he told strikers that they could march up and down until doomsday for all he cared; or the time he told the teachers they were louts and larrikins: 'Go back to school — you're not teachers, you're a rabble.'

Then there was the time during the electricity strike. Sir Henry told the strikers with great bluntness it had nothing to do with him. An enterprising firm brought out a candle in a perfect Bolte likeness. So one could light one's dim kitchen with the Bolte candle and watch the flame burn down into his skull. Yet even as this happened one still had to admire the Bolte toughness.

Yes, the character grew and grew to giant proportions and he actually liked the daily cartoons by Les Tanner and Jeff Hook. Tanner tended to make him a toothless nonagenarian koala bear. Hook had him more as a pickled walnut.

His greatest achievement unquestionably was his political skill in giving Victoria continuity of single party government for 17 years — no coalitions, no going on bended knee to independents — pure Bolte power.

To really understand the true mystery of Victorian politics it may be necessary to give you a short history lesson. In the old days the cartoonists had a standard cartoon. It depicted the Liberal, Labor and Country parties buzzing around in a revolving door. Between 1921 and 1955 Victoria had 21 governments with an average life of 18 months. If you took out the 10 years in the late thirties and early forties when Sir Albert Dunstan, with brilliant conniving, managed to stay in office, the average life expectancy was only a year. Maybe the most interesting battles took place between the end of 1947 and the mid-fifties. The era began when Tom Hollway, the Liberal leader, and J. G. B. McDonald, the Country Party leader, parted company. Just to quote you some of the friendly CP-to-Liberal phrases on that day.

Mr McDonald: 'This contemptible ratbag . . . the prince of appeasers and king of disruptionists . . . the most irresponsible premier ever to have strutted across the political arena.'

From Dunstan: 'This worm-like premier with swollen head and shrunken back. This pampered playboy craven cur looking in the mirror in the morning and thinking himself savior of this country.'

From Hollway: 'In the words of my leader Sir Stanley Argyle the rogue is at his old tricks again. Everything he touches politically he attempts to destroy.'

The problem was not so much to run Victoria but to stay in office. There were Liberals and breakaway Liberals, the Country Party and the breakaway Country Party. It was in 1949 that we gave birth to the Liberal and Country Party, a creation of Liberal and CP members. In 1952 we had four governments, one of which lasted only two days. In 1953 Liberal leader Trevor Oldham died in an air crash, and Bolte, then 45, took over the leadership of the Liberal and Country Party. (They don't need that 'Country' bit any more.) Then came the Labor split and on the comfortable inner spring suspension of the DLP, Henry Bolte rode to victory in 1955. A very rough-hewn country boy he seemed, and everyone called him the stop-gap premier. Even the pictures of him at this time seem to depict a non-lasting innocence.

How did he perform the miracle? It was interesting to consult two men who knew him well, but knew him for very different reasons — Jim McDonald MLA, his close friend and racing partner, and Clyde Holding, the leader of the opposition. They both agreed toughness was part of the story.

Jim McDonald: 'He has a very earthy touch. He understands people, knows how to yarn to backbenchers or the fellows in the bar in his electorate. And he has an almost uncanny political understanding of what he can get away with. He's rough, he's rude, he's ruthless and he's decisive. If you come up with something, he might say "That's good" or "That's bullshit." No messing around. In politics the leader must be a strong man. You don't get ministers doing a Wentworth in Victoria.

'Sometimes if there has been disagreement in the party room it has been a case of "take it or leave it", but the people concerned have had to remember that resignation like death is permanent. I've heard people say "He's a rude bastard, but by golly he's good." I think he is one of the most unusual men in the world. He had no proper training but he understood one thing — there can only be one boss. That was Menzies' success, but this bloke is better.'

Clyde Holding: 'He reflected all the prejudices of the average Australian and oddly enough that was his strength. Australians saw themselves in Bolte. He was absolute boss, and I have a theory he learned how to put iron in his soul by being in opposition. He had John Cain as premier to cope with, a very fine debater. Then he had the DLP on one side and Tom Hollway sniping at him on the other. Oh yes, he was the outstanding member of his party, a good debater, rude and rough. I always noticed that when the going was tough, he was the one who instinctively went for the bucket. I was brought up in Richmond, I understand these things too. The only thing to do was to be equally rough in return. He understood you then and respected you for it. Any sign of weakness and he would walk right over you.'

Yes, stability is what he provided. As with Menzies, it is not easy to draw up a chart and say precisely what the Sir Henry Bolte era did for Victoria.

He said in his summing up that Victoria was a better place to live in than when he took office, that nowadays an employee and an employer could drive the same car to the same picnic spot, open up a picnic basket and the employee's would be just as good as the employer's. Yet as Douglas Wilkie pointed out in the Sun News Pictorial, you could say the same thing of almost every place in the Western world. You could say the same thing of the two new universities. Would anyone seriously suggest we wouldn't have had these if Sir Henry were not here? Of course, he was magnificent as Victoria's number one PR man. One remembers with pride several of his quotes. 'Victoria is the gem in the federal government's crown,' and 'Victoria is the greatest little state in Australia. An afternoon run in the Dandenongs will do me.'

He started the system of business trips by the premier with a team of stars, and almost every second year they would tour the world. His practical knowledge of world economics always startled those he met. On his last trip he received a standing ovation from a group of New York financiers.

Yet some people were confused. Often they did not know whether he was premier of Victoria or prime minister of Australia and his reputation is such that now he is better known than any Australian prime minister.

His greatest achievement in cultural life unquestionably was the building of the Melbourne Arts Centre. This was an interesting example of Bolte in action. He didn't interfere, not from the time of the first presentation of plans to the finished article. He merely gave orders that the government would keep signing cheques, as long as Roy Grounds and his team kept to the original quotes. There was much satisfaction to be had in the news that Melbourne did not have to stoop to the shame of an Arts Centre Lottery.

He also took care to make sure that government money was shovelled into country areas. Under the Bolte reign we won six first-class provincial art galleries, some of them better than the Queensland National Gallery. Libraries? Now that was a different story. Books were never spectacular vote-getters. The Victorian State Library became decrepit and dangerously short of funds. Provincial libraries followed in its wake.

Yet while showing little sensitivity for the arts in general, he showed no interest in music, theatre, plays, books or ballet. Indeed he was suspicious of books and his chief secretary, Sir Arthur Rylah, said that he wouldn't like his teenage daughter to be seen reading 'The Group'. What's more Sir Arthur's granddaughter, if he has one, even now couldn't be seen reading The Little Red School Book because Sir Henry's government considers it too dangerous. His comment on 'Hair' and 'The Boys in the Band' was: 'I wouldn't even cross the road to see them.' In April 1970 he told some women members of the Liberal Party that pollution of minds was a more important problem than air or water pollution. 'You can trust a Liberal government to uphold the morals of the community,' he said.

Of course, it is unfair to put the blame on Bolte for many of things. Could anyone else have done better or even as well? The sound heard during much of his 17 years was that of the Bolte head being bashed against the immovable federal treasury walls. Some of his bashes were spectacular, like his attempts to get back taxation rights and his introduction of payroll tax. But nothing worked. He could never muster trade union unity with the other premiers, so he had to run a pauper government in the midst of Australia's most affluent community.

It was Bolte determination also that won us the Tullamarine airport. He had one of his vintage quotes on this subject: 'Tullamarine's advantage is that you will be able to fly direct from New York to Melbourne without having to waste time calling at Sydney.' And as the press reported at the time, he added with a smile: 'However Sydney will always be important.' Sydney's reaction was immediate. Mr A. Bridges MLA, chairman of the Kingsford Smith airport committee, said: 'Tullamarine will become Australia's number one international airport over our dead bodies.' This gave us another Bolte vintage quote: 'They're just like a lot of school children who have lost their all day suckers.'

He was premier during two major engineering disasters, the King Street Bridge and the West Gate Bridge. He rode easily through the worst moral scandal Australia has known, which resulted in the abortion inquiry and the jailing of top policemen. As a result of this he did little in the way of abortion reform.

The strangest episode in his career was the hanging of Ronald Joseph Ryan in January 1967. There had not been a hanging in Victoria for 16 years, but Bolte was cold-bloodedly determined to go ahead with this one. He declined to receive a combined deputation on Ryan's behalf and he even made the comment: 'I notice the people who are protesting, in the main, are the same people who campaigned on Vietnam.' His detractors have a theory that this was a time when he had to prove something to himself. Not only did the ALP oppose the hanging, the DLP opposed it and so did some powerful members within Sir Henry's own party. He had to force the toughest discipline to keep them in line. The press also unanimously opposed it, and the Flinders Street newspapers campaigned in a way they have never campaigned before or since. So the theory is that all this made Bolte more and more determined. He had to prove that he was the boss, that he was the leader, and once he had done this thing, he would never have to do it again.

Whether that was true or not, his political judgment was sound. The general mass of Victorians weren't unhappy to see Ryan hanged. They proved that at the electorate. Bolte later in one of his reminiscing interviews said: 'I would take a lot of convincing that there is not occasionally one man who should be hanged. That is my own view. And as things are, capital punishment is party policy.'

There have been some ugly murders since, but no hangings and nor are there likely to be. John Galbally MLC has tried again and again with private members' bills to have hanging abolished without success. He has tried also to have flogging removed from the statute books and he has had no success there either. There is not even a slight possibility of a flogging taking place next week or even next year, but the Bolte government would not move. Sir Henry liked to have these things around.

He is a man of simple tastes. The government once bought him a Rolls-Royce, but he didn't want it. He gave it to the governor. He personally owns only one car, a utility truck. He is not interested in gourmet foods, he is fond of lamb and boiled mutton. Nor does he go for fancy wines. His favorite drink is Scotch and water, no ice. His main recreations are fishing, shooting and racing. He likes to have a punt and there have been occasions when he has put $1000 on a horse.

I'm told that he is an extremely good farmer. His property Bamganie is a sound, economic operation with no frills, and he really does drink with the boys down at the local pub. I treasure one memory, the day in 1970 when Sir Henry appeared at the pub with the boys on 'Four Corners.' There he was in a tatty old jumper, every inch the local farmer. He turned to the camera and said: 'I come here every week. I'm not staging this for your jolly show.'

Ah yes, he is vintage stuff. A complete original as the Age said in its farewell editorial. Actually one of the finest compliments came from Clyde Holding. He said: 'Being a leader of the Opposition is a curious job. You work very close to a premier but at the same time it is your job to destroy him daily, a strange love-hate relationship. I'll say this for him. He was a good party man, for his party that is, and I admired the way he finished. It would have been so easy for him to announce several weeks ahead that he would retire on such and such a date. Then parliament would have laid on a great stream of eulogistic speeches from both sides of the house. It wasn't his style. He made his announcement on the day. It was a very sad moment really. I was sitting on the other side of the table. I saw what it meant to him. Yes, 17 years of his life had been spent there. You don't give up that easily. But he just stood up and walked out. It was all over. He went out like a man.'
