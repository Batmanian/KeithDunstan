Nureyev Observed

6 min read.

First published 16 Dec 1972

A behind-the-scenes visit to the filming of Nureyev's 'Don Quixote' at Essendon airport hangars, where Sir Robert Helpmann lay in a vast four-poster bed, Ray Powell endured 12 hours sewn into a Sancho Panza costume, and Nureyev himself, in purple polo-neck, woollen trousers and a balaclava in 90-degree heat, never once stopped moving.

HOW INTERESTING to sit back and quietly observe Rudolph Nureyev. The temperature is over 90 and when the lights are on it is nigh unbearable. Nureyev, director of the film ballet Don Quixote, is wearing a vast purple polo-necked sweater, woollen trousers and an absurd woollen balaclava which covers all but his pixie-like features. And over his sweater he has a shortish cotton dressing gown of a garment which gives him a cossack look. Beside him is his manager, confidant, friend, go-everywhere, Wallace Potts. He too defies the heat, because Nureyev has a nervous fear of ever catching cold.

Potts is wearing exquisitely cut leather trousers. He has long brown hair, he's American and he has the same delicacy of features that Nureyev has. The third member of their party, Claire De Georges is at the motel. She is the not-young French cook. 'A lovely motherly lady,' the publicity lady whispers, 'really mothers them, but speaks hardly any English.'

The filming has only two days to go. It ends on the Thursday and on the Sunday Nureyev performs in a Stravinsky memorial program in Paris. He dances in Petrouchka and Firebird. Sir Robert Helpmann is even more rushed, he has a rehearsal for Alice on the Saturday, in London. 'Oh Christ why do I do it?' he says.

Nureyev is a hot electric wire of a man, never still. He is never actually motionless. He is directing, looking down on the lens of a camera, checking costumes, discussing lighting, calling out 'Barreee, Barreee that cloak must look darker.' Oh, he did sit down once, for five minutes only and this was fascinating. He had a cassette recorder, playing Petrouchka, and quietly, just with himself, while still sitting he went through foot and hand movements. His own little rehearsal for Sunday. He arrives on the set at 10am, films and works with only breaks for meals until midnight then looks at rushes of the previous day's filming until 2am.

Twenty six days of filming it has taken and Melbourne hasn't seen him. Melbourne for Nureyev hasn't really existed. The locale is a series of hangars, now unused at the old Essendon airport. Melburnians have come up with a hundred thoughts on what they could do with Essendon, now perhaps they have the answer. Practically no place on earth could be better for the manufacture of films.

John Hargreaves (pronounced Hargrieves) the producer is there, and he complains of the awful cost of making a film in Melbourne, $650,000. So why does he come here? 'Because the Australian Ballet is so good,' he says. 'Young, attractive and completely professional. Do you think Nureyev would come out here and dance with them if they were a lot of codswallop?' It seems to answer the question.

It is the third last day and they are filming the opening of the movie. That seems to be the way you make films, you begin at the end. Barry Kay's set is marvellous, 20 foot high stone Pentridge walls. These walls are made of the new miracle of our time, polystyrene; to get that crumbling, ancient stone look you just run over it with a blow torch and then spray on acrylic paint. The property men are busy hurrying round with cans of spray on cobwebs and spray on dust. Occasionally for a really beaut cobweb they run a piece of string from point A to point B, then spray. The plastic cobweb is better than the real thing.

The centre piece is Don Quixote's bed. One wonders if a larger bed has ever been made anywhere. It is a four-poster with the posts actually the thickness and height of telegraph poles, but each post is beautifully carved. Barry Kay says it was inspired by a Dore painting. They made it out there at Essendon in the workshop and it cost $2000. 'Bloody expensive, but worth every penny,' said John Hargreaves. 'It will be the very first thing you see when the film opens up, tremendous.' Lying on this bed is Don Quixote himself, Sir Robert Helpmann. What dignity, what elegant style the man has. He has a white pointed beard, and those wonderful TV antenna eyebrows.

After three hours of fiddling with lighting, costumes etc they are ready to roll. The sound equipment floods the set with the huge hi-fi noise of Don Quixote already recorded with the Elizabethan Trust Orchestra. Sancho Panza bursts into the bedroom with a live chook under his arm, frenetically pursued by three peasant girls. He hurls the chook into a corner. They repeat this bit for what seems 30 times. I am terribly impressed with the uncomplaining patience of the chook. It has been very well cast.

Of course, so has Sancho Panza. He is Ray Powell, a thin good-looking man. For this part he is being slowly tortured to death. He has immense Billy Bunter padding all over him, and he can't get out of it for 12 hours, because he is sewn into his costume. The part requires him to be bald. One catches him in a spare perspiring moment. 'I could have worn a bald headpiece but you know what that means, three hours in the make up chair to get it right. I didn't think it was worth it. So I get my head shaved every morning then usually I have to get it shaved again in the evening. Honestly, you get five o'clock head shadow.

'It's funny though. You go to scratch your head and suddenly you find you've got no hair.'

Late in the evening there is another scene to be filmed, a vision Don Quixote has by the windmills. They filmed the tilting at the windmills the previous Friday, and all concerned were very happy with the success of that. The windmills are superbly ghostlike in the antiquitous polystyrene tradition, and they have vast tattered orange sails. Sir Robert had to do a daring stunt act. When assaulting his windmill he had to ride one of the sails from the three o'clock round to the nine o'clock position. And as he did this lightning flashed on and off, thunder rolled, and a battery of wind machines produced an awe-inspiring gale. So there he was swooping round 15 feet above the ground. 'What would have happened if they had dropped you?' one asked.

'They all would have needed new jobs,' said Don Quixote imperiously.

By the time you read this, the company will have broken up, Nureyev will be in Paris, Helpmann in London and the cutting men will be preparing the film for release, maybe next July. And here in Melbourne for 18 December we will be preparing an auction of all the effects. I tell you, for some time 1690 vintage swords, saddles, armour, beds, candelabra, etc are going to be a drug on the market.

Source: out and about with Batman: Nureyev observed, The Bulletin, 16 December 1972. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
