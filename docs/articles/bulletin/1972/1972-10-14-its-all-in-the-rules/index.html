It's All in the Rules

6 min read.

First published 14 Oct 1972

A Grand Final week report covering Cyril Pearl's symbolic football burning outside the MCG, the Anti-Football League selling 60,000 non-seats, and the Ron Barassi Sixth Memorial Lecture at Monash University, in which the football-as-sex-act thesis was proved to thunderous applause.

STRANGE and awesome things take place in Melbourne during Grand Final week. There is an eerie atmosphere like the witches scene from Macbeth. For example, the Richmond town hall was decorated with all sorts of horrid manifestations of Tiger superiority including a dummy model dressed as a footballer. This was hoisted high up by the antique clock tower. The dummy incensed some citizen to such a degree that he shot it with a rifle and destroyed its jaw.

Then there was the Cyril Pearl incident. Mr Pearl, Melbourne-born and a very distinguished author, is now resident in Sydney. He wrote letters to newspapers all over the country complaining of the insidious spread of Australian Rules football. He ranked it among the foremost plagues of Australian history, putting it on a level with phylloxera, the vine louse and the prickly pear.

He finished his letter with the words: 'At an appropriate time and place I propose to burn an oval football (22¼" by 29½") as a fiery and symbolic gesture.'

Some people were very shocked. Mr Don Whitington, Mosman, Sydney, another distinguished author, wrote: 'Burning a football may sound an amusing way to register a protest, but there are people in the community who may take Mr Pearl seriously, and consider he is setting them an example of commendable behaviour.

'How big a step is it from burning a football to burning a book, as Hitler did, burning human beings, as napalm has in Vietnam, and as Hitler did also?

'... To dispel any doubts, I should admit I shall be at the Grand Final in Melbourne. I haven't missed for 20 years.'

Now there is a curious organisation in Melbourne called the Anti-Football League. It raised money for charity in 1972 by actually selling seats in the Melbourne Cricket Ground and inviting people to stay away from the place. The AFL has as its motto 'Make love — not football' and it sold over 60,000 non-seats for its non-match.

On 28 September the AFL had a little ceremony and Mr Pearl did indeed burn a football and he burned it right in the goal mouth at the Melbourne Cricket Ground. He had been anxious to burn a football that had been kicked by Cazaly so that he could compound the sacrilege. Regrettably it was impossible to locate one of these, but a football was found that had been kicked by Royce Hart, the present captain of Richmond.

Your correspondent was present at the burning ceremony. One must admit to feeling a little uncomfortable — the same feeling you might get joining in a black mass before the altar of St Paul's.

Members of the navy band were there practising for the big match on the Saturday. They were clearly shocked. When asked to play a tune for the occasion they refused.

The football was placed on a four gallon drum and doused in kerosine. Mr Pearl made a few suitable sacrilegious chants such as 'down there Cazaly' and struck a match. At first it refused to burn. Ian Johnson, secretary of the Melbourne Cricket Club said: 'I told you. There is something special about the atmosphere and feeling of this place. It will never burn.'

One did feel that suddenly there might be a terrible clap of thunder or that Cyril would be turned into a pillar of salt. Yet finally the ball did begin to burn and it burned splendidly with a terrible smell almost like a human sacrifice.

As the flames curled over the leather a member of the navy band played 'The Last Post.'

The ceremony brought mixed reactions. Some put Pearl on a level of combustible sainthood along with St Joan. Some said it was a commo-bastard sort of thing to do and some, oddly, questioned his manhood. Mr Pearl lists his recreations in Who's Who — 'exploring, social myths, discovering Australian wines, omphaloskepsis.'

Omphaloskepsis, of course, is contemplating one's navel with a mirror. One letter arrived: 'Contemplates his bloody navel with a mirror, does he? Typical. Why isn't he a HE-MAN like Ron Barassi or Des Tuddenham?'

The charred cadaver of the football has since been offered to a Melbourne charity auction and undoubtedly will fetch an enormous price.

Another event in this strange week was the Ron Barassi Sixth Memorial Lecture given at Monash University. This is a learned address, always with the same theme, very nearly the same words, that has become almost like a mass given every Grand Final week. Normally it takes place in one of the larger lecture theatres. The professor divides his lecture into four quarters, keeps a tube of Fosters on the lectern and one drinks a tube and eats a pie at half time.

This year the congregation was so large the lecture had to be moved to the Sir Robert Blackwood Hall, a far more dignified scene, and there was an enforcer on the door to make sure there was no food or drink. The professor arrived wearing a Richmond beanie and he gave the quotation from the Richmond Koran by that learned authority, Mr Jack Dyer: 'If you live in Richmond you do two things — you vote Labor and you barrack for the Tigers.'

His lecture began to the accompaniment of the Four'n'Twenty Pie commercial played over the loudspeakers, and as he spoke he held a bottle of tomato sauce in the air.

At half time he expressed regret about the absence of beer. He said: 'Also I had hoped to get the RAAF band but it had a prior engagement at the McMahon christening.'

But as he said this a student nicked forward and placed a can on his lectern. Also it was the signal for a mass can-popping right throughout the Robert Blackwood Hall. Obviously it had been one of the most skilful smuggling operations since the days of Edward III.

The professor traced the history of football until it developed into the recurring malaria disease that it is today. In the words of George Johnston: 'Melbourne has no summer, only a hibernation between football seasons.'

Taking himself as an average citizen, he spent eight hours a week on football, therefore Melbourne used up between one and five million hours a week on the game. His actual figure was 3.2 million, but he allowed two million either side for possible error.

The mania was relatively classless. A New Zealander friend once told him Melbourne was the only city where you would get the same conversation in the university staff club pissoir as in the lavatory of the local pub.

So the lecture went its ritual course. It was proved that footballers didn't enjoy the game while they were playing, nor did spectators go there for fun. 'The game is not yet compulsory in Melbourne,' he said. They went to the match to ease their paleolithic violent urges. Finally, using Freud with great skill, he proved that the game throughout was a symbol of the sex act... the ball was the shape of a womb, it was kicked between the big sticks. The footballers burst through a membrane of streamers when they ran on to the ground... even the terms used were very sexual.

All this was received with thunderous applause from the congregation.
