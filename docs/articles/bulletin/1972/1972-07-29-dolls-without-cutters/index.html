Dolls Without Cutters

4 min read.

First published 29 Jul 1972

A visit to Cairns during the cane-burning season finds the cane-cutters replaced by machines, a city whose temperatures are close to perfection despite wet-season toads and burning-cane ash in swimming pools, and novelist Xavier Herbert working in a bush house on a 500,000-word novel he describes as his reason for being on earth.

NOBODY is quite so offensive as the journalist who moves off on a junket to some exotic area and then proceeds to gloat. Stand by, your correspondent is about to be outstandingly offensive.

The temperature here in Cairns is 78 degrees. I wouldn't say it was absolute perfection. There was a time about midday when I would have wished it two or three degrees warmer. And take last Tuesday; there was a little, scattered cumulus and at least eight to ten minutes of the day was lost to sunbathing. To be honest, when I drop out, this will be the place — and the dropping out might come very soon.

The area is a little untidy during the wet season; the great toads gather around the street lights looking as if they're holding little stop work meetings, and follow you, kerplop, kerplop. The cane on either side of the Cook Highway is as high as a giraffe's eye and the feathery bit on top that carries the seed is a delicate pink when seen against the light.

Driving through at night is an awesome experience; they are burning the cane in field after field. Sheets of red flame seethe up into the very black night and the whole operation seems to take only a few minutes: a wild crackle from one end of the field to the other and suddenly it all goes out and the air is filled with a sickly, sweet, burnt toast smell. All this is terribly hard on Cairns swimming pool owners. The pools draw to their bosoms the falling black ash and when the cane fields are burning you have to vacuum your pool at least twice a day.

But there has been one interesting social change. Remember the cane-cutters, the brawny characters in their blue singlets, the heroic figures who would hack their way 1500 miles of infinitely detailed sugar coast, wielding their cane knives? They don't exist any more. The job is done by machines. It makes one think of Ray Lawler and The Summer of the Seventeenth Doll. Oh, the dolls are still there; the Cairns show is on this week and they have a full range of pretty dolls on sticks but no cane-cutters to take down south for the seventeenth summer or whatever.

You see all types up here: from hippies to the American after the black marlin.

The hippies have settled mostly near Kuranda, the nearest town to Cairns on the tableland, and here the climate comes closest to perfection. I am also told it has the finest conditions for growing quality grass. You also get hippies on the beaches all the way up to Port Douglas. The beaches there are lovely.

At the other end of the scale there are the rich marlin fishermen. The season gets under way in another month to reach a crescendo about October. I like the story of the American millionaire, Warren Kendell, who last October caught a marlin which weighed 1072 pounds. After weighing in, someone asked him how much he thought that fish had cost. Well, he worked out his fares, how much it had cost him to fly out his personal skipper and mate, boat charges at $200 a day, etc. 'Oh, I guess if I packed up and went home tomorrow that fish would cost me $30,000.' And what happened to his $30,000 fish? It's no good for eating in the usual style. It was filled with rocks and dumped at sea.

Author Xavier Herbert lives about six miles out of Cairns in a little wooden house. Once upon a time he said he preferred the beach with three indoor dunnies but he infinitely prefers Cairns — and the little house that has only one outdoor dunny.

For the past seven years he has been working on a novel. He has only two chapters to go and it will reach nigh on 500,000 words. Of the book he says: 'It is my reason for being on earth. It says all I want to say.' When you remark that surely he did this in Capricornia he replies: 'I want to say it again. It had to be said again.'

Each morning he starts his exercises, a series of physical jerks that would do credit to any Army PT instructor, and then sits down to write. He has an almost superhuman dedication.
