The City That Lost Its Taste

4 min read.

First published 15 Jul 1972

A visit to Brisbane's rapidly modernising cityscape prompts regret for lost cast-iron pubs, demolished heritage buildings and the cancelled potential of an Australian New Orleans, while still finding warmth in the sunshine and the avocadoes.

SOME TIME back in the early 1950s the city fathers should have put Brisbane in the hands of a smart public relations firm. The gentleman in charge of the account immediately would have made these recommendations:

  1. Buy up huge quantities of cast-iron lacework. (They do some very nice durable stuff these days in plastic.) Order that all hotels and public buildings have it fitted at once, particularly those dear old pubs with big verandas.

  2. Any hotels covered with lavatory tiles must have them stripped off. Those without saloon swing-doors must have them fitted.

  3. Present all houses on stilts, particularly those weatherboard places with wooden latticework on the veranda and pimply stained glass windows, with a grading from the National Trust. Indeed, insist a couple more thousand be built.

  4. Stop the defoliation policy and plant two million tropical trees so that the city can become a shady paradise.

Had this plan been in operation Brisbane now would be known as the Australian New Orleans with a huge tourist trade and all the big hotels packed with guests.

And at these pubs I would have a fat lady in slippers who would go around the bedrooms at 7am knocking on each door and shouting 'black or white.' This wouldn't be a racist gesture but a re-establishment of that unique old Australian hotel custom of virtually insisting that everyone have a cup of tea. With each cup would be a thin slice of white bread and butter.

Of course, it is too late to do this now. Brisbane has the same dream that we all have. The city must be a shiny replica of Dallas, Cleveland, Kansas City or Atlanta, so Americans coming here will get the feeling they never left home.

Oh, Brisbane still has its shimmering acres of galvanised iron, its square miles of weatherboard with peeling paint like a vast attack of civic dermatitis.

Ah, but the place has changed. Six years ago practically the only tower on the skyline was the campanile of the Brisbane city hall. Now that campanile is reduced to nothing by an entire mini Manhattan of towers. Most of the old pubs with swing-doors have gone. The lovely old supreme court building in George Street has gone. Remember that little Anglican pro-cathedral in George Street? In the old days it was occupied by the Brisbane CIB, and there was a standard gag line: 'It hasn't really changed from its church days. They still use it to take confessions.' Gone. The Bellevue Hotel, a most beautiful example of a cast-iron pub, used to be a place of great distinction, filled with good furniture and a view across to Parliament House and the park. It was closed as a pub, turned into rooms for MPs and now it is being described as a fire trap and certain for destruction.

I did read that they might preserve some of the railing and put up a nice plaque to say the Bellevue Hotel was here. How can Brisbane allow these things to happen? The citizens should lie down in front of the bulldozers.

Now the city is alive with stories that the National Bank at the corner of Queen and Creek Streets is to come down and be replaced by a 22 storey building. The old bank was completed in 1885 and built at a time when banks were supposed to fill one with a total sense of awe, an utter respect for their strength and solidity. It has a grading from the National Trust and is an irreplaceable gem. It must not be allowed to go.

King George Square is now surrounded by towering buildings, plus two churches, the Albert Street Methodist church and the Ann Street Presbyterian church. You have your choice of a number of faiths: T and G Insurance, State Government Insurance or traditional faiths. Frankly, the new square is a disappointment. The layout is appallingly severe. There are stone rectangles of grass, looking more like the city crematorium than the city square.

There is a fountain, imported from the US. It adjusts itself according to wind strengths, changes colour and all that sort of thing. A local man's idea for a sculpture was rejected. However a majestic statue of King George in his full regal gear, mounted on a horse, presides over all. He looks very oddly dressed for the climate. He would have been more comfortable in shorts and an open-necked shirt.

Does all this sound critical? Actually I love the place. Right now the sun is shining warmly out of a clear blue sky, the poinsettias are in bloom, and the avocadoes are the most heavenly fruit ever created for mankind. The new Victoria Bridge is lovely, an exquisitely sculptured thing. If only the same taste were shown in buildings everywhere.

Source: out and about WITH BATMAN The city that lost its taste, The Bulletin, 15 July 1972. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
