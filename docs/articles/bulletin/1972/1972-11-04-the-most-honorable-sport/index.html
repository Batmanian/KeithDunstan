The Most Honorable Sport

5 min read.

First published 04 Nov 1972

A review of R. S. Whitington's illustrated history of Australian cricket, tracing the game's Golden Age from the 1890s to 1912 through Trumper, Macartney and Ranjitsinhji, and noting the book's peculiar distinction of two prestigious forewords and no index.

I HAVE always felt that the non-watching of cricket is one of the world's most rewarding pastimes. Although there have been vile attempts to speed it up with one-day matches for grand occasions the ritual has been maintained. Now it is the one prolonged completely ulcer-soothing, five-day folk dance left to us in a polluted world of tension and mayhem.

The happiest days I can remember have been spent asleep or quietly dozing at the Melbourne Cricket Ground. For this reason I have missed many of the greatest events. Indeed have you noticed how often it is that you sit for three hours watching a Test with vast concentration, waiting for something to happen — then when finally it does, you miss it. You have suddenly dipped down into your lunch, you have turned to wipe a fly off your nose and Cowdrey, most controversially, has been caught at slip, an event worth 750,000 cabled words to London and you have missed it. So you sit back and wait another three hours for something to happen.

For this reason I am a romantic about cricket, and the memories of cricket long ago become beautiful as the years go by. For this reason I also like cricket books. The most prolific producer in this country is R. S. (Dick) Whitington. He has produced 17 of them and the latest An Illustrated History of Australian Cricket has just arrived in the mail.

Unquestionably it is the best thing he has done — a large and beautiful production. Dick Whitington is also a romantic and he writes as if he actually adores the game, a rare thing these times. He even gives pride of place on the title page to that quotation by Lord Harris: 'You do well to love it, for it is more free of anything sordid, anything dishonourable, than any game in the world. To play it keenly, honourably, generously, self-sacrificingly, is a moral lesson in itself, and the classroom is God's air and sunshine.'

Yes, one can think of a few cricket writers who could write that one out a hundred times, as they poise over their machines ready to send another cable around the world.

On the cover of the book there is that famous picture of Victor Trumper. It is simply the greatest cricket picture in the history of the world. It shows Trumper half way down the pitch. His outstretched front foot is six inches off the ground, his back swing is such that the bat is etched in the sky higher than his head, and the whole vision is of awe-inspiring devastation and aggression.

He reports that even today only three batsmen, Bradman, Trumper and Macartney have started from scratch and scored centuries before lunch in Anglo-Australian Test matches. Charles Macartney performed so brilliantly one newspaper editor reported that 'he mocked the summer lightning.'

Whitington puts forward the view that Australia has always had its greatest victories when its batsmen have mixed devil-may-care with determination, when they have behaved like adventurers. He shows less affection for the tortuous, slow-moving sixties when our cricket was in the hands of Simpson and Lawry.

The Golden Age, he said, was from the mid-1890s to 1912 in the days of Trumper, Ranjitsinhji, Joe Darling, Clem Hill, Macartney, Stanley Jackson, Jessop, Fry and Hobbs. Fry wrote of Ranji: 'He moved as if he had no bones; one would not be surprised to see brown curves burning in the grass where one of his cuts had travelled or blue flames shimmering round his hat as he made one of his strokes.'

Of all the batsmen Trumper gets the warmest terms. 'Put Vic on a pedestal of his own then start talking about the rest,' said Vernon Ransford. One of his most extraordinary efforts was a club game in Paddington when he made 266 in 165 minutes, including 22 fives and 39 fours. Those fives would have been sixes today. One broke a window on the second floor of a shoe factory 150 yards from the batting crease. The window was retained in this broken condition for 60 years as a memorial to this mighty stroke.

This could explain much about Sydney's broken windows. They could all be memorials to Victor Trumper. When he died, aged 37 of Bright's disease, 20,000 people lined the streets of Sydney for the funeral.

Oh, there were so many great names during that period including that mighty South Australian fast bowler, Ernest Jones. When asked at Buckingham Palace whether he attended St Peter's College, Adelaide, he replied: 'Yes, I drive the dirt car there every Monday.'

What was the greatest Australian Test side of all time? Was it Darling's XI in 1902, was it Warwick Armstrong's of 1920-21, or Bradman's of 1948? Many an old hand still clings to the belief that the Armstrong team with stars like Herbert Collins, Kelleway, Jack Gregory, Oldfield, Pellew, Taylor, Macartney and Arthur Mailey was the finest.

Australia's superiority was so great that during the final Test at the oval Armstrong borrowed a newspaper from a spectator and read it as he leant against the boundary fence while play was in progress. Whitington records that when asked about this he replied: 'I just wanted to make sure who we were playing.' It wasn't a remark that contributed to international understanding.

The photographs, generally, are excellent. There is that picture of Keith Miller, leaning slightly forward, in a GT super-power drive to the boundary. It could well be the second best cricket picture in the history of the world. Then this is the only book I have come across with two prestigious forewords, one by Sir Robert Menzies and the other by Lindsay Hassett.

But nowhere is there a source list showing where any of the photographs first appeared. Nor is there any kind of detailed index.

Any historian who fails to do this deserves the sentence of having his membership tickets revoked or 10 days' hard labour.

Source: out and about with Batman: The most honorable sport, The Bulletin, 4 November 1972. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
