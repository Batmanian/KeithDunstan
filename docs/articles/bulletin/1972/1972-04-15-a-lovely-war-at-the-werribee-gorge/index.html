A Lovely War at the Werribee Gorge

4 min read.

First published 15 Apr 1972

Australian units of American Civil War re-enactors camp for four days at the Werribee Gorge near Bacchus Marsh, with muzzle loaders, Confederate uniforms, a Union loyalist doomed to die at every outing, and evidence of Foster's Lager burned black in the campfire.

IF YOU drive out four miles from Bacchus Marsh in Victoria you come to the Werribee Gorge. There are willows all along the river, lofty cliffs, large boulders and crevasses — lots of splendid spots for ambushes and for holding out in immensely brave last stands. You couldn't mistake it for Virginia but it does smack a trifle of spots in Georgia or even Arizona. Last week the Werribee Gorge looked like a major Civil War battle site. I even thought that I might get an interview with old Rhett Butler himself.

The site was occupied by Australian units of the Confederate Army for four days. The company included the 1st Tennessee Infantry Regiment from Victoria, the 66th Georgia Infantry, the 9th Georgia Artillery, the 13th Georgia Cavalry and the 16th Alabama Infantry from Western Australia and the 30th Alabama Infantry Regiment from South Australia.

We had a little trouble finding the camp. The camp was manned entirely by men in Confederate uniform. They were all armed with utterly authentic muzzle loader rifles, sabres and .44 calibre pistols. Tents and gear were all correct 1864 vintage. The only jarring note was evidence of Foster's Lager beer cans. But you will be pleased to hear they had been burnt black in the camp fire and the real men had been drinking rum out of Confederate stone jars.

I was immediately paraded before the leader, Colonel John K. Couzens. I must say he looked very fine indeed with his mutton chop whiskers, gold buttons, leather knee-boots and cutaway jacket. The Colonel admitted that when off-duty he ran the 'King of Clubs' night club in Perth. His chef, two of his barmen and two chaps on the door were all in his Confederate troop from Western Australia.

'I have been accused of trying to raise my own private army. But that's not true,' he said. 'We are merely a group of people who are fascinated by a certain period of history.'

'The Mayor of Fremantle, Sir Frederick Sampson, is our patron and honorary colonel. We also have two regiments of ladies. They too wear uniform. Actually one of our ladies is the Brigade sabre champion. She beat all comers, male and female.'

I mentioned to the Colonel that the whole thing seemed to lack a certain balance. Were they not all Confederates? Who did they fight against? What about Diggers and Germans? They could dress up in World War I uniforms and have mock trenches. First Sergeant Peter Smith, CO of the First Tennessee Infantry Regiment, was a little hurt by these questions.

He said they were devoted to the Civil War because it was the last romantic war in history, the last which was fought hand to hand. And he liked the Confederate Army because of a natural sympathy for the underdog.

Even so the whole thing seemed to lack balance. They needed someone to fight against. 'But we do,' said Sergeant Smith. He indicated several members of the party who were in Union uniform. They had Dennis Middlemiss of Blackburn, Melbourne, who stood there defiantly in his dark uniform with the red stripe down the side of his pants. Why was he the bounder among this crowd? Because he was a devoted student of the life of General Custer and therefore it was appropriate that he should remain a Yankee.

General Custer, of course, was the man who died a most spectacular and violent death, and Dennis Middlemiss also has to die at every outing. While I was there they had a 'skirmish.' The Yankees were given 20 minutes start then the Confeds went out to get them.

The muzzle loaders are .58 calibre and the members buy reproductions for $120 each. The Colonel said they put in 60 grains of black powder and fired only blanks. He personally was armed with a .44 calibre Remington revolver, imported from the US.

'There are 25,000 of us around the world,' said the Colonel. 'In England there are about 600. They go round Britain giving demonstration battles. Say you are running a fair, you can get a half hour Civil War battle for 100 pounds, or if you want something really spectacular you can get a six hour battle for 500 pounds.'

The muzzle loaders were blasting terrible gun shots. A muzzle loader makes a very satisfying display of smoke and flame. Fellows were screaming and rolling over dead. Prisoners were taken. Oh, they all had a lovely day and at night they came back to sing Civil War songs round the camp fire.

Source: out and about with Batman: A Lovely War at the Werribee Gorge, The Bulletin, 15 April 1972. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
