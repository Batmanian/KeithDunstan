Manning the Ramparts

5 min read.

First published 18 Nov 1972

A defence of the Windsor Hotel against the threat of redevelopment, tracing its history from the 1886 temperance grand opening, when James Munro burned the liquor licence, through 1920 reinstatement, to its status as Melbourne's last great hotel with atmosphere.

THE NATIONAL TRUST has just announced a 'B' classification for the Windsor Hotel in Melbourne. 'B' is about as high as the trust will go for a commercial enterprise. They will give an 'A' to something like St Patrick's Cathedral which Whelan the Wrecker is unlikely to get anyway. 'A' in real terms means that they will have to pay to save it. 'B' means they won't have to pay, but they are prepared to do almost anything should it come to the crunch.

Last week's 'B' grading was handed out with a very real air of menace. We all feel that we have been called to man the ramparts. The Victorian chairman of the trust, Mr Rodney Davidson said very firmly: 'The trust is aware that recently there has been heavy trading in the shares of the Windsor Hotel. Large parcels of shares have changed hands and the emphasis of ownership is changing.'

You see the Windsor is on a block that runs from Little Collins Street to Bourke Street, opposite Parliament House, a chunk worth $5 million or more. What a choice site for another shiny, artificial, granite insurance palace. The sentimentalists could be looked after. Somewhere deep in its bowels it could contain a tavern. This would be called the Windsor Tavern. It would have a chandelier from the old Windsor and in the dimly lit bar with its windows of tinted glass, its wall-to-wall carpets and its padded leather seats there would be a painting of the old Windsor spotlit. The idea is not far-fetched. On 13 October we had the opening of the Menzies Tavern in the insurance palace, 500 Collins Street. The idea was to woo back the old Menzies' bar trade. They celebrated the opening with bagpipes, haggis and all, but it was as much like the old Menzies as a piece of down-town Chicago.

However, we have been assured by the management that the Windsor is not in real danger, the parcels of shares referred to by Mr Davidson have been doing the rounds for some time, and that the hotel is under no real threat for at least five years. Yet even that period is frightening enough. Just the idea of the Windsor being destroyed is unthinkable. It is in beautiful condition and the last of the great hotels. Where else can you find a dining room with nice balustrading and lovely stained-glass cupola skylights, like so many bosoms? Where else can you find corridors so wide that there is room for sofas and armchairs for a little rest on your way to your room? The lifts too are a work of art, lovely iron grille affairs, and as for the grand staircase, it is so magnificent every time you descend from the first to the ground floor you feel like the archduke of Prussia.

My favourite story concerning the Windsor was the day it became a temperance hotel under the name of the Grand Coffee Palace, back in 1886. The grand re-opening was one of those never-to-be-forgotten moments in the history of temperance. There was a banquet for distinguished citizens and shareholders. The Hon James Munro was in the chair. He wore a frock-coat and in his buttonhole was a slip of blue ribbon indicating that he was a member of the Total Abstainers' Blue Ribbon League. At the climax of his speech he paused, then with great solemnity he drew from his top pocket a long piece of paper. 'Well, gentlemen,' said he, 'this is what we think of the liquor licence.' Thereupon he set a match to it. And up with the flames went the fortunes of the Grand, from which the hotel did not recover until 1920 when it regained a liquor licence under the name of the Windsor.

Now the Windsor is the last hotel with real atmosphere left. It had a million extensions added as recently as 1966 and if you can't be talked into buying shares in the place at least enjoy the privilege of staying there when you come to Melbourne.

There was a time in the sixties when there was a real danger that all hotel bars would be priced out of business; now they are returning in large numbers. In the old days, of course, bars contained no seats, there was lino on the floor and the walls were adorned with the purest of white lavatory tiles.

All that has changed now. Your bar must look either like the boardroom of BHP or Elvis Presley's boudoir. The Hotel Melbourne has a beautiful mixture of both. The Golden Nugget Bar is gloomy with thick carpet, see-through swivel seats and golden nugget paintings. The Federation Bar has a leather-padded bar, thick purple carpet and purple tops to the seats. On the walls is a charming collection of framed Invitations to the Federation celebrations at the Exhibition Building in 1901.

The old, homely, dart-throwing, drop-your-cigarette-on-the-floor atmosphere has disappeared. Indeed these times it is difficult to get plans for a bar passed which do not provide for wall-to-wall carpet.

It could all make a very interesting study for sociologists. They could take the case of the office clerk who works on the 23rd floor of an air-conditioned, wall-to-wall carpeted office with a view over the bay. He has his counter-lunch and drinks in a tinted-glass, air-conditioned bar. He uses a lavatory which has gold taps and gorgeous Regency wallpaper.

Then he goes home in an ancient train to a wooden house with an outside dunny. A penny for his schizophrenic thoughts?
