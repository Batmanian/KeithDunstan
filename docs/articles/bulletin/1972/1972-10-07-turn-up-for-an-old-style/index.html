Turn-Up for an Old Style

5 min read.

First published 07 Oct 1972

A column on the return of trouser turn-ups, enormous lapels and high heels to men's fashion, prompted by the visit of London designer Hardy Amies, with statistics showing Australian men spend only $32 a year on clothing.

ACTUALLY I was always very fond of cuffs on my trousers. At cocktail parties they were ideal for the disposing of tooth picks, olive pips, even cigarette ash. They could be rewarding, too; quite frequently they would pick up the falling threepence.

Also, as the trousers began to wear the cuffs could be turned. With careful use one could make a suit last for 15, even 20 years. Indeed, with me, cuffs have never gone out. Imagine my pleasure, my satisfaction to note that cuffs are back in again, absolutely the height of fashion.

You've got to have trouser cuffs. One has survived a complete fashion cycle.

All this came to my notice several weeks ago. I was reading one of these very 'with it' men's wear magazines. There was a full-page advertisement for 'Oxford bags.' It announced that Oxford bags, 'the fashion fad of generations ago,' had returned. It showed the dear old things, the voluminous bagginess around the calves, the big cuff turn-ups.

Admittedly they weren't quite right: I don't think the young Edward, Prince of Wales, or Lytton Strachey, or all that lot, would have worn them so sexually tight about the crotch. But the idea was there.

I kept turning the pages and, by golly, all the trousers had cuffs. The suits too had the enormous lapels, they had the stripes, they had the perfection of the old demobilisation suit as given to us — was it by Mr Dedman? — on behalf of His Majesty in 1945-46.

After looking at all this it was very interesting to note the arrival of Mr Hardy Amies, the distinguished fashion designer from London. Ah yes, he had his cuffs and his big lapels. But it is sad-making how these devils get you in the end. Fashion does move in a cycle, but they never repeat a cycle exactly and they always mix it a trifle in the process. I had my cuffs and Mr Amies had his cuffs. But his cuffs were much wider, more than two inches, and his trousers were more broadly flared. I had my broad lapels, but mine were so obviously vintage. The Amies lapels started at his navel and spread like sails to his shoulders.

I mentioned my enthusiasm for cuffs, how marvellous they were for olive pips and tooth picks. He was quite cold about that. 'That's not the reason for them at all,' he said. 'Trousers are now being flared to 24 inch bottoms. Therefore you need weight to give them substance, to make them stand out.

'And if you have large flares on the bottoms of your trousers then you must balance the whole look. You must have broad lapels.'

And the shirt to match this look? Mr Amies demonstrated his own shirt. The colour must be strong; you must have long points on the collar and the collar must sit up high under the neck. Mr Amies' shirt was pink. And, it was true, that way you could see its full glory above the suit collar.

'Ties,' he said, 'also match this look and they must be high, wide and handsome. They must have also a large chunk knot.'

It was so. Mr Amies' tie, although not distastefully bright, was wide enough to be a yacht spinnaker. The one piece of good news was that he had no jewellery, no tie tack, no cuff links, just one button. 'Cuff links are out' he said. He also chastised the ridiculous style of having two buttons on the cuff. 'One was enough,' he said.

One approached the matter of high heels with timidity. Surely the male wouldn't swing to this nitwit fashion? But according to all reports they have been selling like crazy at Myers, great chunky shoes that look like ski boots, with platform soles and three inch heels. They are also selling male knee-high suede boots with three inch heels.

'Yes, you must have high heels,' said Mr Amies. 'How could you possibly wear trousers with 24 inch flares without high heels? You must have this to get the balance. I don't agree that you wear three inch ones, but it depends on what you are wearing. The baggier the trouser, the higher the heel — that's the rule.'

Mr Amies announced this as if he had just dispensed the Amies third law of motion.

It was important to know how the fashion cycle worked. Mr Amies says it takes 10 years. 'You wouldn't want to move it any faster than that,' he said. 'I think men like to keep a suit for three years.' Then there was the natural lag — he had noticed with a certain amount of horror that many men in Australia were still wearing shoes with pointy toes, a fashion of almost a decade ago.

But the real truth is this. The fashion dictators are not winning. The scungy look may even triumph. The annual 'Gold Book' issue of Tailor and Men's Wear passes this sad comment: 'Superficially, the Australian street scene might tempt one to conclude that the male in this country is as fashionably attired as anywhere, but statistics prove that the contrary is true — that the Australian male has considerably less in his wardrobe.'

And so they revealed that Australian males per head, spend only $32 a year on men's wear, and that goes for the lot, even underpants.

If you go to the statistics, the sale of suits hasn't gone up since 1964-65. We have picked up a little in sports coats, had about 20 per cent increase in shorts sales over the period. Overcoats are down 30 per cent, and men are spurning singlets; sales there are down by more than half.

But, fashion lovers, since 1964-65 sales of jeans have more than trebled, and they are still going up. So perhaps that's what Mr Amies should have been wearing — a pair of pre-aged jeans and a suitably battered denim top. Who knows, that gear might survive even as long as Oxford bags.

Source: out and about with Batman: Turn-up for an old style, The Bulletin, 7 October 1972. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
