The Majesty of World Golf

5 min read.

First published 25 Nov 1972

A report from the World Cup contest at Royal Melbourne Golf Club, where Bruce Crampton's agonisingly methodical putting was contrasted with Roberto de Vicenzo's joyful, chatty approach to the game, while running like Herb Elliott was required to stay close to any gallery.

PRESSMEN respond most kindly to being loved. After a quarter of a century of chillsome press boxes I have never seen such gracious facilities as those at the Royal Melbourne Golf Club for the World Cup contest.

You know the vast marquees that Toorak knights and such erect on their lawns when their daughters get married? Well, they had one of these, only bigger. Nor did we have to walk on the damp grass. Our marquee had a floor raised three feet off the ground. There was a tree growing up through the centre of the marquee and this was used for decorative effect and not a branch was allowed to be wounded.

Three-quarters of the marquee was devoted to communications. There was an enormous board which detailed the activities of every player. There was a branch of the army signal corps equipped with radio and within 30 seconds of a hole being finished the score was on our board. There was a flock of television sets bringing the entire scene close-up to our chairs. The other quarter was like a leagues club: bar provided by Dunhill-Rothmans, floor-to-ceiling plastic windows with view across the course, wall-to-wall carpet, charming lampshades.

Next door the Dunhill-Rothmans people had what they call their 'special events van' worth $78,000. This also contained a complete radio station, four teleprinters, wire service for photographs, television and emergency power unit. So, should Melbourne have been wiped out by atomic attack, it would have been possible still to give the world the progress of the World Cup.

Of course the wise and efficient thing to do would have been never to leave the bliss of the wedding marquee. When one went out into the cruel air, the actual watching of the game proved the hardest and most competitive of all sport watching. It is said that test cricket is played with a slow, inspiring majesty. However, world golf far out-slows, out-awe inspires and out-majesties that game. Many competitors took six and a half hours to do 18 holes and occasionally two hours to play only four. One lady who was watching on television complained that she saw the players hit up the fairway. She went away, did her washing, hung it on the line, returned and they still hadn't finished putting that hole.

Even so, great athleticism was required. The biggest gallery followed Messrs Crampton, Dunk and the two Japanese, and at times it numbered over 1000. You'd want to see them hit off at the tee. All right, as soon as they had done that, you had to be in a good position to watch them hit their next shot up the fairway. But, if you were going to see anything, you had to take off like Herb Elliott.

There were many ladies present. They were in tweed skirts, Pringle twin sets, steel-studded golf shoes, ankle socks and they could really move. They had steel-shafted shooting sticks and as they galloped down the hill it was like the charge of the Bengal Lancers.

So if you failed in that race you were pretty well done. You had to stand at the back and watch the game through a periscope like a U-boat commander. The periscopes were provided free to all by our friends at Dunhill. However, on the Sunday the south-westerly was blowing with such gale force, that one's mirrors rattled.

The golfers themselves made a grim safari. Neither Crampton, Dunk, Kono nor Murakami ever smiled, or ever displayed emotion. It had all the atmosphere of a death march.

Oh, the march was a complete safari. There was the army communications gentleman who carried a radio set on his back. There was a chief petty officer in full Royal Australian Navy uniform who carried a banner giving the scores. Indeed the full resources of the army and navy were assisting the operation. I couldn't understand why the RAAF wasn't there. Surely helicopters could have been provided to take the golfers from tee to tee.

The gallery was well behaved. Cameras were not allowed unless you were a photographer with proper accreditation. It was the putting that took an eternity. Crampton would look at the lie of the ball from the north, he would look at it from the south, he would pace it out, he would kneel down, he would have a practice swing and then there would be an eerie silence, like out in the middle of Simpson's Desert while we all held our breaths and waited respectfully. He would putt. And miss. 'Haaaaaaaaa,' as we all breathed out again.

The fun man to watch was Roberto de Vicenzo who upset things by constantly playing as if he enjoyed the game. He swung the club round in a circle as he walked, he laughed, joked, even chatted to us common spectators. At the 16th he appeared to sky a ball way off into the rough. When he got there, glory be, the ball was safely on the edge of the fairway and a pretty girl was standing alongside it.

Said Roberto: 'You good girl. You kicked it on to the fairway. I will give you a kiss.'

'I did not,' said the girl hotly.

'I will give you a kiss just the same,' said Roberto.

The golfers were much prettier than us spectators. I liked Gary Player's ensemble: pale blue cardigan, gay check trousers, red skivvy, red peaked cap like a B52 pilot and white shoes. Almost all the golfers wore those. And you must wear a single glove on your left hand and that too should match. For example, Hahn Chang Sang of Korea wore violet trousers and a single violet glove.

The triumphant golfer Hsieh Min-Nan of Taiwan looked as smart as anybody. He wore a beautiful pink and black striped shirt with pink trousers. At the finish Taiwan's champions were anything but enigmatic: they threw their arms around each other in glee. It is interesting how this custom of hugging is spreading. One time you saw it only in soccer. Now they even do it in Australian Rules football.
