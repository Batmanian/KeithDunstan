Doing a Biggles

5 min read.

First published 09 Dec 1972

A report on the RAAF Flying Club's two-day Tiger Moth fly-in at Point Cook, where 12 of the expected 21 vintage biplanes assembled for a formation fly-past, their owners as passionate about original detail as veteran car men, and the whole squadron barely moving against a strong Geelong Road headwind.

BACK in the dreamtime, circa 1942 to 1945, the RAAF had 800 Tiger Moths. The Tiger was the last of the real aeroplanes — open cockpit, wind in the face, petrol tank up aloft, two wings, canvas, wire, all that sort of thing.

Starting them up imposed a considerable problem. One had to swing the propeller and on a bitter June morning at around 6.30am this could take so long, one could get the idea that the bloody thing was really powered by an elastic band and had to be wound up.

Unlike 99 per cent light aircraft of 1972 it was fully aerobatic. Yet doing slow rolls against the torque of the propeller and the wind resistance of two wings called for real strength of arm. Ah, but what a thrill it was to be upside down at 3000 feet, hanging by the straps, with cigarette butts, lolly papers, bits of pencil and such falling past your nose. They used to cruise at about 66 knots and on a summer's day it was heaven going a-durk, a-durk, a-durk around the countryside of Victoria beating up haystacks, chasing sheep and such. One even has the memory of chugging along into the wind, looking out to the right and, good heavens, being passed by the Spirit of Progress.

After the war Tiger Moths were relatively unwanted. Gentlemen fliers preferred the upholstered comfort of Austers, Cessnas, Pipers and such. You could buy a Tiger for 150 pounds or more. But wait, in the galloping nostalgia that has overtaken us in the seventies the Tiger is something to be desperately desired. Prices start around $3000 and they are rare indeed. You see them, but they are about as common as white elephants or well-preserved Hispano Suizas.

The RAAF Flying Club at Point Cook has just held a two-day picnic fly-in. The picnickers arrived from all over Australia, an armada of over 100 aeroplanes. Originally there were to be 21 Tigers, perhaps more than had been gathered in one spot since the grand days of 1945. Alas, the meteorological forecasts were so formidable nine did not come. Tiger Moth pilots when faced with the possibility of having to drive home against a 60mph wind with a six-knot ground speed tend to give up easily.

But we did get 12 and it was interesting to note that Tiger Moth owners love their machines with the passion of veteran car men. They have the same concern about the purity of detail; all the Tigers were superbly preserved and decorated.

There was the Tiger from Berwick, which was painted a brilliant yellow with black Maltese crosses on the wings. It was called Lilli Marlene. Another had a chequerboard pattern on the wings in the style of the old pre-war Hawker Demons and Bristol Bulldogs.

Others had the camouflage and wartime roundels of fighter aircraft so that their owners could indulge in Walter Mitty dreams of really doing a Biggles with long scarf flapping in the wind.

The climax on the Sunday afternoon was a fly-past by the 12 Tigers in formation. The organiser was squadron leader Kevin Duffy who led the formation. Kevin Duffy already had put on quite a turn. He had a Rolls-Royce Merlin-powered Mustang. It used to be outside the front gate at the RAAF station in Toowoomba and it came to Melbourne a total wreck. The engineering team at Point Cook restored it so it is now fully operational.

However, there was a complication. Was it a RAAF military aircraft, or a domestic machine? So Kevin Duffy was unable to get a clearance to fly it during the fly-in.

However, he opened up the old Merlin to full power, took it down the strip as if taking off and actually raised it on its oleos. All the old RAAF men looked on, with tears of nostalgia running down their cheeks. A character on my starboard side took minutes to explain the pure bliss of the noise of a Rolls-Royce Merlin at full power. It was as if he were describing the singing note of a Stradivarius.

But to get back to the Tigers. Squadron leader Duffy called together the pilots and the crews in the operations room. He explained the plan. They would take off in formation and they would land in formation. There would be two wing spans between each aircraft and they would fly at 1000 feet, speed 65 knots. 'I want you to watch the aircraft in front of you like grim death,' he said.

Takeoff time was 4pm. All the Tigers were wheeled into position and placed in four flights of three, line astern. All this required some organisation. Naturally, they all had to be hand-started and it was rather like cranking a dozen De Dions and getting them all going at the one time. We couldn't take off until all machines were going.

But what a lovely choir they made, the dozen engines making their historic noise: a-durk, a-durk, a-durk. So we all took off, an extraordinary sight — a squadron formation of little bi-planes like something out of the 1914-18 war.

Your correspondent was a passenger in Lilli Marlene. Sporting the Red Baron's old markings it looked a little odd as if it had lost its way in a cloud and inadvertently joined a formation of Camels and SE5s.

So we made some majestic sweeps across the countryside and as we came in low over Point Cook, the historic station, where military flying began in Australia, we respectfully tightened our formation.

At one stage we were out by the Geelong road. A very strong wind was blowing. Our Tiger formation barely seemed to be moving. I looked down and we were being passed by cars on the Geelong Road. Lovely it was. Just like old times.
