Grease Up to Your Ducktail Hairdo

5 min read.

First published 23 Sep 1972

A report on Harry Miller's production of 'Grease' at the Metro Theatre and its fancy-dress first night, with reflections on Melbourne's nostalgia obsession and the observation that the city is still so far behind the 1950s it barely needs to reach backwards.

IN MELBOURNE we are being nostalgied practically out of our minds. It is bad enough having the pubs filled with old gaslamps, saddles, harnesses and Ned Kelly helmets but show business is also on the nostalgia kick. At Her Majesty's Theatre we have dear, lovable No, No, Nanette, which pines for the twenties. Now Harry M. Miller has given us Grease at the Metro which pines for the fifties.

Grease takes us back to Elvis Presley, greasy ducktail hair-dos, high school gangs, rock-'n'-roll, Bill Haley and his Comets and the Andrew Sisters. In addition we get the full spectrum of fashionable 1972 on-stage swear words plus a simulated copulation; so much so, that some nostalgic members of the audience might even have been weepy for the lovely old nineteen fiftyish days of Sir Arthur Rylah and his teenage daughter.

These days a Harry Miller show always has to involve the audience. Harry asked first-nighters to wear 1950 era gear. I looked round for my old double-breasted Arthur Calwell discharge austerity suit, but I couldn't find it. However, it was easy, seeing that my wardrobe had never graduated from the 1950 era: I came as I was.

Actually everyone was a little confused as to what was correct. Some confused the 1950s with the 1920s and wore strange pieces of fur. Some confused the 1950s with the 1980s and wore almost nothing at all.

Some looked like refugees from an Indian reserve. Jeans were terribly popular and there was so much leather abroad one tended to see the audience as a medieval army. One young chap wore a white satin suit with stitching around the lapels, an open-necked black and white cotton shirt. He had platform shoes with high heels and, glory be, he carried a bag.

There were those who turned up in their old school sweaters, but to complete the picture one felt they should also have worn their school caps. Except for the theatre critics in black tuxedos nobody looked really nineteen fiftyish. Pringle twin sets with pearls, sensible skirts and ankle-strap shoes would have been just the thing.

On every seat there was a brown paper bag. This contained a bottle of vaseline haircream and a comb. Most of the bottles were left on the seats at the end of the show. It is easy to understand the enthusiasm of vaseline manufacturers for a greasy hair revival. It would take at least three bottles to subdue some of the Afro hair-dos that were present at Grease.

It is de rigueur for Mr Miller to have a first night party. I remember for Hair it was a top-secret affair. He had a series of buses lined up outside the Metro and we all went off to a secret destination. He served champagne aboard the buses and threw us off the scent by driving first to the Arts Centre, then to Spencer Street station, then to Festival Hall, finally ending up in an abandoned milk factory in Fitzroy.

For Grease we all went to an art gallery in Richmond called the Pinacotheca. That too was in a one-time factory. Maybe Pinacotheca wasn't secret, but Mr Miller appeared to have his joke just the same, for the 'how-to-get-there' instructions on the back of the card were completely inaccurate and the guests spent half the night driving around Richmond looking for the place.

There were security guards at the door to inspect all invitations and inside you could find almost every radio, TV and newspaper man in Melbourne. There were projectors slung from the roof which showed old rock-'n'-roll movies. There was a popcorn machine and there were characters dispensing hot dogs and, wait for it, Harry-M-burgers. To get the true 1950 era feeling I thought he should have provided mercury-free flake in thick, crunchy batter.

As someone pointed out on the night, it was a bit awkward trying to be nostalgic for the fifties in Melbourne, because we are really just catching up with them. We have almost reached the 1950 standard for freeways. We have started to dig an underground railway which everyone else our size had done long before 1950. We last bought a new tram in 1956. We have been building a city square for an unmentionable time, and the planning for that started back in the fifties. Our city council system, actually, is more nostalgic than that. It is modelled on the 1870s.

But while we are being nostalgic for the nineteen fifties, there is a new show being filmed in San Francisco titled American Graffiti which is nostalgia for the sixties.

You can imagine what a great show this would make around here. We could have Jean Shrimpton as the star in the mini skirt. We could hearken wistfully back to the days of Sir Robert Menzies and John McEwen, to fruitful royal visits, Dawn Fraser, the days when we won Davis Cups, and Melbourne used to win football matches, when there were no R movies, when newspapers were not so vulgar as to use words like 'shit,' when we used fahrenheit, a bottle of beer was about three and six, school bills about 35 pounds a term and P & O still used to have a ship to London every other week. And the Oriental, and the cathedral pubs were still there and... and... sorry, I can't go on, I'm starting to blub.

Source: out and about with Batman: Grease up to your ducktail hairdo, The Bulletin, 23 September 1972. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
