Evolving the Mayoral Egg

5 min read.

First published 09 Sep 1972

An examination of Melbourne's arcane system for electing its lord mayor through the Civic Group's private Sunday-night caucus, prompted by the first-ever dropping of an incumbent after one term and the quiet elevation of a former RAAF fighter pilot in his place.

IT IS just a year since Councillor Alwynne Rowlands was elected Lord Mayor of Melbourne. One can remember the proud words he gave to the Herald: 'I am conscious that there has been criticism of past lord mayors for lacking drive. You can be sure I will not be afraid to tread on toes or hurt feelings. I think we can expect a little more noise and activity from the town hall, at least for a while. The city belongs to all of us. I don't want the town hall to be a house of secrets.'

Now the best kept secret is why Councillor Rowlands, a distinguished surgeon, has been dropped as lord mayor, the first man ever to be dropped after only one term. What has he done? The suggestions made around town are that his sense of public relations was not good enough. He did not make enough noise or tread on enough toes. This is terribly unfair to his three predecessors who were even less noisy and un-toe-treading.

Perhaps we should explain to you how the city council works and elects its lord mayor. The council really deserves an 'A' grading from the National Trust. It is a most interesting example of 18th century democracy.

Now we elect our lord mayors in a most curious way. Actually 'elect' is too strong a word. As we mentioned once before it is a process of evolution, like the production of an egg. The citizens of Melbourne do not get a say, the ratepayers do not get a say. The chief argument in favour of the system is that Melbourne has done things this way since it was incorporated as a town.

The Melbourne city council is a very nice club. To get into it members have been known to spend $10,000 or more. The Civic Group has all the power and is a team dedicated to the thought that as much power and business should be pushed into the close city limits as possible. Theoretically it has no political label. Perhaps you could describe it as rather to the right of Liberal and little to the left of Louis XIV. Easily the most popular residential area for Civic Group councillors is Toorak and South Yarra.

To elect the mayor the Civic Group meets on the Sunday night immediately after the council elections. Normally it is a most gentlemanly arrangement. The mayoral job comes up by turn like the presidency of Rotary or Apex. There's seniority, there's the understanding about who has the money, who has a good social charity pushing wife — all that sort of thing. Almost by tradition there has been no need for the untidiness of an election. The Civic Group candidate is nominated and he becomes the lord mayor.

But wait a minute, you're saying: What about the rest of the council? You're joking. The Civic Group has 24 members, the Labor Party has seven and there are two independents. There has never been an ALP lord mayor. So after the Civic Group nominates, nobody else bothers. They haven't the votes in the chamber. On the Monday morning, the Civic gentleman is formally announced as the new lord mayor. The egg is evolved.

In recent years the club has not been quite so happy. There have been heavy confrontations between council and parliament (or should we say council and Sir Henry Bolte?), like demands that the council should pay out heavily for the underground railway. Until 1965 it was de rigueur that the egg would automatically get a knighthood. Sir Henry stopped all that. Now the egg is merely blessed with a companion of St Michael and St George. No doubt, Councillor Rowlands will even miss out on that, because he has not completed the prescribed course.

So you see, the whole happy, matured in the wood system is beginning to spoil. We have had a split within the Civic Group, and a behind doors election.

Barry Jones MLA recently pointed out another vintage angle to council democracy. He said the boundaries for the wards had not changed since 1939. Half the councillors were elected by 71 per cent of the voters. The other half by 29 per cent. He said ward size was apparently determined by ratable value of property not by number of residents. So we get safe Civic Group wards like Hoddle with 1673 voters, and Batman with 1817 voters.

Of course there is the problem which the Melbourne city council has been edging toward for the past 100 years: the spreading of its field, the taking in of surrounding councils like Fitzroy and South Melbourne. Most of the smaller councils, though poverty stricken, don't want to be swallowed up. Minor mayors enjoy their glory. Imagine the horror with members of the Civic Group. If they take in councils like Fitzroy and South Melbourne they're inviting in members of the ALP.

The new lord mayor, Councillor Alan Whalley, a former RAAF fighter pilot, now a city jeweller, is a hearty, talkative character, and he has already had plenty to say. From now on, he says, it will be 'soul-searching and shirtsleeve work'. He wants cocktail parties: 'A quiet drink and a chat with ratepayers.' He wants to appoint a public relations officer to improve the image of the council. He wants better traffic flow, one-way streets and ring roads.

One wishes him good luck. It will be most interesting if he can bring the council back to the 20th century. But then the lord mayor doesn't have that much power. His job is more that of a chairman and an official greeter. As a former mayor once said, It is a reverse Frank Hardy situation: 'Glory without power.'
