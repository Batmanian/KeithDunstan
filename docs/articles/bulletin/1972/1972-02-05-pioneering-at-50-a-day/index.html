Pioneering at $50 a Day

4 min read.

First published 05 Feb 1972

A visit to Naringal, a stately homestead near Ballarat run by the Rowe family for American tourists, offering colonial pastoral life complete with silver candelabra, kangaroo-spotting, and a tour of the family cemetery.

THE stately homes business has taken off in rather grand style in Victoria. In England it was the poor dukes, duchesses, marquesses and earls who were having trouble keeping their castles and vintage mansions going. Here it is the landed gentry who are fighting to save their stately properties. The Victorian Tourist Development Authority now has on its book 16 properties where you can actually stay as a paying guest, observing life on a fine property, and there are ten where you can go for a day. Some offer boomerang throwing, cow milking, wildflower jaunts, bird photography, mountain climbing, trail riding, trout fishing and even wool spinning. The most distinguished of the stately properties is Naringal, about 33 miles from Ballarat. Naringal has gone, with quite some success, after the American trade.

I would like to quote from their handsome colour brochure: 'Naringal, an historic Australian sheep station steeped in the traditions of our virile history. See the fabulous scenic beauty of Victoria's Western District. See the most beautiful grazing country under the Southern Cross. You can settle into comfortable quarters, relax and enjoy the sophisticated style of living of the pioneer families . . .' The brochure also said I could be flown up there by twin-engined Norman Islander aircraft 'over the towering cliffs pounded by the surf of the Great Southern Ocean' or I could be picked up by 'luxury limousine' from Tullamarine airport.

Actually, I went there in my own Holden, and Naringal really was something to see. The homestead is white, yes, stately, and very large indeed. It is surrounded by a brownstone fence and five acres of landscaped garden. The lawns are easily as well kept as those at Government House and there is a swimming-pool as well as an en tout cas tennis court. It reminds me of a stately home in Hampshire which was a mile from the front gate to the house and as one drove it was possible to inspect the deer and the pheasant. At Naringal it is nearly a mile from the gate to the house and here one inspects the kangaroos, the emus and the pheasant.

The owner is a white-moustached, elegant and charming gentleman, Mr. William T. Rowe. His great-grandfather, also William Rowe, took up the land in 1841. He did it in the grand style; arrived by boat from Staffordshire, then walked in from Williamstown.

Bill Rowe and his wife, Betty, do things very well. As soon as you put down your cases you are invited into the living area for a drink. You are shown your room. Your bedroom is about the size of your living-room at home. It has a large private bathroom. On a table near your bed there is a bowl of fruit, a fine old ivory-handled fruit knife and fork, also a bone china finger-bowl filled with water, with a rose petal floating in it.

Drinks are served before dinner, which is at eight. We sit down in a beautiful, large dining-room which has a silver candelabra on the table. The menu consists of pâté washed down with a good red, hot roast turkey and ham, and pavlova served with a sauterne — port and Cuban cigars come later.

Bill Rowe said that so far 70 American guests had been through, but there were many U.S. tours booked for the future. 'Mostly they stay 24 hours,' he said. 'You know, all rush. They have to see all Australia in four days. We're lucky to get them for that long.'

For the daytime activities, the visitors are taken on a tour. They go to the Rowe family cemetery. Great-grandfather Rowe is buried there and most of the Rowes since. 'Americans and children are fascinated by cemeteries,' said Bill Rowe. They go to the bark hut. They go to the immaculate shearing shed and see a sheep being shorn. They can also go riding or swimming or touring.

The charges for all this are $50 a day including food, tours and all drinks plus another $20 a head if they have to be transported to and from Melbourne. After 12 months the whole idea is starting to work. They are known by travel agents in London and New York. When you depart you get a card with a piece of wool attached. It is an example of the pure merino wool grown at Naringal.

Worth having . . . we all hope.
