Outracing the Race of the Year

5 min read.

First published 21 Oct 1972

A report on the Ocean Racing Club of Victoria's new Melbourne-to-Hobart yacht race, launching on Boxing Day in direct competition with the Sydney-Hobart, with a defence of Victoria's deeper ocean racing tradition.

RELATIONS between Melbourne and Sydney have always been full of mutual understanding and nice co-operation. We do hope this pleasant relationship continues. There will be two yacht races this new year. Please let me explain.

Sydney is actually a sea-faring town and the Sydney-Hobart yacht race is of almost mystical importance. It starts on Boxing Day. Everyone who has anything that floats gets on the water to wave farewell to the ocean racers. The very nice people at Vaucluse, Watson's Bay and the rest have their champagne and chicken parties to watch the farewell from their balconies. In the RSL clubs some people even tear themselves away from the one-armed bandits for five minutes to watch it on television. It is as important to Sydney as the Melbourne Cup is to Melbourne. Newspaper and radio coverage is enormous. Last year the Australian even brought out a six page yacht race supplement.

Well, the Ocean Racing Club of Victoria is organising its own annual Melbourne-Hobart yacht race. You may well ask when it is taking place. Answer: The yachts will leave at 7.30am on Boxing Day.

When I first heard about this I felt a pang of terror. I visualised the fleets of Melbourne and the fleets of Sydney meeting somewhere off the east coast of Tasmania. It would have been like the fleets of Nelson and the men-of-war of France clashing in the English Channel. You see it has to be admitted there has been a little feeling between the interstate yacht clubs.

The Sydney-Hobart yacht race is organised by the Cruising Yacht Club of Australia. It is in effect the Cruising Yacht Club of New South Wales and those in the Cruising Yacht Club of Victoria always thought it ought to be called that. Well, things have changed a little and we have the Ocean Racing Club of Victoria and this body is organising the Melbourne-Hobart yacht race.

The Victorian yachts will sail down the west coast of Tasmania. The west coast has a much more formidable reputation than the east. The winds blow in a splendid unbroken sweep all the way from the Cape of Good Hope.

Your correspondent asked one senior yachtsman whether he would be taking part: 'Oh yes, that trip would be great fun wouldn't it? Like standing under a cold shower and tearing up $20 notes at the same time.

'That coastline is littered with wrecks. As far as I know there are only two lights on the whole coast and the charts are bloody ancient. I've got too much sense. I'm staying home.'

We put all this to Ron Elliot, secretary of the Ocean Racing Club, and he denied utterly that they were setting up in competition with the Sydney-Hobart. 'We will also attract boats from Adelaide,' he said. 'You have to look at it this way. If we take part in the Sydney-Hobart, first we have to sail to Sydney. That means leaving about the 14th and by the time you have finished the race and returned home you'll have been sailing for a month.'

This is true. Melbourne yachtsmen in the past have needed a very secure male liberation position, because not only are they away for a month, but they miss Christmas entirely. Now with the Melbourne-Hobart race they will be home for Christmas.

Ron Elliot also made the point that Victoria had a deeper and broader tradition for ocean racing than New South Wales. 'I think we do more ocean racing,' he said. 'We have the Melbourne to Portland which is a very tough race. We also have the race to Devonport. That attracted 42 entries last year.'

Of course, there is another delicate point. The Sydney-Hobart is a relatively recent affair. It began on Boxing Day, 1945. Victoria had its first race to Tasmania — Melbourne to Stanley — back in 1907. Four yachts took part, Thistle, Rambler, Shamrock and Elida, and they raced for a trophy presented by Rudder magazine.

Thistle crossed in 36 hours and won by a day and a half. Mr Doug Newlands, who took part in that race at the age of seven, said his 17-year-old sister and his mother were also aboard. This information still startles modern yachtsmen. Even as late as the sixties it was not done to let a woman even inside a yacht club.

The commodore of the Ocean Yacht Club of Victoria is Stan Gibson, one of Australia's great yachtsmen. He has sailed in 14 Sydney-Hobart yacht races, and he has faced all sorts of conditions in a little 27-footer called the Four Winds. He now has a 38-footer, Four Winds II, and of course, Four Winds II will be in the Melbourne-Hobart.

Stan Gibson says he has researched the weather back for 10 years and it is traditionally mild off the Tasmanian west coast at new year. He says there are at least three good charts and many more lights than most people think.

'This race won't be any more rugged than the one to Portland,' he said. 'The only thing that could check us would be south easterlies, but we're more likely to get westerlies. We won't interfere with the other race in any way. And I'll tell you what, with the weather we'll get we'll be into Hobart before them.'

So we do hope Sydney understands all this. Our race with less than 20 yachts will have nothing like the prestige of the Sydney race which attracts more than 80.

Nor do we have any hope of attracting such a distinguished entrant as the prime minister of Great Britain. But we musn't despair. Couldn't we invite Mr McMahon to take part? The race around the wind-swept rocky coast of West Tasmania would suit his bare-chested, rugged image very nicely.
