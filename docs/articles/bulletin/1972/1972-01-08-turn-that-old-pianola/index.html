Turn That Old Pianola

6 min read.

First published 08 Jan 1972

An interview with Jack O'Hagan at his Elwood home on the fiftieth anniversary of 'Along the Road to Gundagai', in which he reveals he picked the town's name off a map without ever having visited the place.

AS IT IS the 50th anniversary of the song 'Along the Road to Gundagai' it seemed a good idea to call on the songwriter Jack O'Hagan at his home at Elwood, Melbourne. He is now 73, and, as he explains his legs aren't much good but his mind is still moving at high speed.

Jack's first successful songs were for the silent movies. Actually, he was probably the first songwriter ever to create for the movies. In 1920 he wrote a song for a film called 'The Affairs of Anatol,' which was showing at the Auditorium, now the Metro, Collins Street. The O'Hagan song was played as a prelude. Next venture was 'In Dreamy Araby' for the Rudolf Valentino film 'The Sheik.' Jack remembers: 'It was sung in the theatre by an Italian. The Italians pronounced it "In dreamy Araby . . . sland of mystery." The audience came out with "dreamy Araby . . . land of scent." He'd get enough open Araby and of misery.'

That song went well. Jack believes if only the film hadn't already done its tour six months before in America he could have made a fortune with it over there.

Jack O'Hagan found out early that it was very difficult to market anything that sounded Australian. This was a problem all his career. Even when he was writing musicals in the 'thirties some entrepreneurs preferred that the origins sound English, American, anything but Australian.

But 'Gundagai' had a different beginning. In 1921 he took it for granted that it was better to stick to U.S. themes, so he wrote a song titled 'Down Carolina Way.' He took it to Hugh D. McIntosh, the great entrepreneur of the day. He was capable, too, of using very colourful language. Jack O'Hagan said: 'I took my song to Hugh McIntosh and he exploded. "Why the bloody hell don't you write a song about Australia? Aren't there enough bloody people writing about America already? Get out of here and do something about your own country."

'Well, that set me thinking,' said Jack. 'I went off and wrote "Gundagai".'

Incredibly, 'Gundagai' first appeared as part of the pantomime 'Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves.'

'Really, Mr. O'Hagan,' I said, 'how could you fit Gundagai, Murrumbidgee, blue gums and everything into "Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves"?'

'Oh, no trouble,' continued Jack. 'You could put anything into a mime in those days. They were a sort of fruit salad. "Gundagai" was sung by the principal boy, Mona Magnet.'

'You having me on. You made that name up.'

'No, I didn't. It's true. Mona Magnet was a star of the day. Very good, too. She gave a marvellous rendition of it. Kicked the song off, really.'

'Gundagai' was an instant hit, as Jack O'Hagan explained. Things seemed good for a songwriter then. The game hadn't been killed by radio — there was a flood of one and two shilling sheet music being sold then, and almost immediately it went into pianola rolls as well. The following year Peter Dawson recorded it and it has been recorded 28 times since. The 29th version, sung by Barry Humphries, was done to fit the market.

'That's a funny story, actually. I was looking for a place with an interesting name. At first it was going to be "Along the Road to Bundaberg." Good song name, Bundaberg. I didn't know a thing about the place, but it sounded good. But then I really needed a river. I went to the map and found it was on the Burnett. Now I ask you, how do you fit Burnett into a lyric?

'I wanted a big river with a good name so I went back to my map and . . . Ahhh. The Murrumbidgee. The very thing. I ran my finger along the river and found Gundagai. Just sounded good. I had no knowledge of the place whatever.

'Honestly, most songs are written that way. Stephen Foster wrote about the Swanee River without ever seeing it and I was talking to Oscar Hammerstein when he was out here. You know, he wrote the lyrics for Jerome Kern's "Ol' Man River." Well, he did that before he ever cast his eyes on the Mississippi.'

Jack O'Hagan did not actually see Gundagai for 35 years. He was invited for the Gundagai centenary celebrations, and at a Rotary dinner he revealed the awful truth. Bundaberg could so easily have been immortalised instead. At the end of the talk a Rotarian came up to him and said: 'I don't bloody well believe you. How could you write all that stuff about the blue gums growing and Murrumbidgee flowing if you'd never seen it?'

'Gundagai' really took off as the theme song for the 'Dad and Dave' serial, the biggest thing in radio during the thirties, four nights a week on 58 stations.

But 'Gundagai' was such a good thing, Jack decided to write another. Why not a love song for Dave and Mabel?

'My Mabel waits for me, Underneath the bright blue sky, And the dog sits on the tucker box, Five miles from Gundagai.'

Jack O'Hagan remembers: 'I rang up George Edwards, the producer, and told him I had a new song. He replied, "Look, Jack, I can't use another Gundagai song, I've already got one." I asked him, "Just let me sing it to you over the phone?" He listened and replied, "You'd better come right over." So the Tucker Box song was written into the script.'

Then in 1942 he was playing golf in Melbourne when some words started flowing through his mind: 'When a Boy from Alabama Meets a Girl from Gundagai.' That was the third big Gundagai hit.

Altogether he has had over 200 songs published, and they run into millions of copies. His most recent was 'God Bless Australia' written to the tune of 'Waltzing Matilda.' Woolworths sold over 30,000 copies of the record, then Ampol sold another half million through their service stations.

It is fascinating now to look at the songs he wrote about Australian heroes like 'Hustling Hinkler,' 'Amy Johnson, Kingsford Smith — Aussie's Proud of You,' 'Our Don Bradman' and 'Our Marjorie Jackson' — the Lithgow Flyer.

Even now he itches to go to work again. 'Now take Evonne Goolagong,' he said. 'Why didn't someone write a song about her when she won Wimbledon? With a name like Goolagong. Think what you could do with that! I reckon I could run it up in an hour. Be a smash hit.'

Source: out and about: Turn That Old Pianola, The Bulletin, 8 January 1972. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
