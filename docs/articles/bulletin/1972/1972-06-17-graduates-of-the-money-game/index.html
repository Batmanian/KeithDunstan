Graduates of the Money Game

5 min read.

First published 17 Jun 1972

A chemistry graduate turned cab driver — one of 14 unemployed out of 18 in his cohort — prompts a proposal for a 'Bachelor of Materialism' degree, with George Frew, Bruce Small, David Wynn and Norman Shierlaw as lecturers, Sir Reginald Ansett as dean, and Sir Henry Bolte conferring degrees.

THE PRIME minister made one very nice remark on his Asian travels. Indeed the figures he gave in parliament might even be of interest to less well-advanced countries. He said that the Commonwealth Public Service had accepted only 309 graduates so far this year. Actually the public service employed 309 graduate clerks, and the number of rejects was double that. This, he thought, was a most satisfactory situation. As for his own department, he had been very selective in the matter of graduate deliveries as well as some charming bits of walking catalogues as well.

But you find the best taxi drivers. They have more time to talk about their specialised subjects and you can find the most extraordinarily well-informed, friendly taxi driver with long dark hair, speaking thoughtfully from behind the wheel.

'How do you like taxi driving?' I asked.

'Very interesting,' he said, 'a real study of life. You wouldn't believe the intriguing people I have met in this taxi. Pity though, I can't do what I'm trained for.'

'Yes, I graduated in chemistry with honours last year. Couldn't get a job anywhere. I wasn't the only one, 18 of us graduated in the course and 14 are out of work. But I'm in an interesting position really. I have been offered a research grant which would take me on to a doctorate. But that would put me on a higher level and make me even more unemployable than I was before. It's interesting isn't it? You could even develop a new Newton's law of motion: the greater the qualifications the less chance of getting a job.'

'So what do you do?' I asked.

'Oh, I stick at taxi driving. I like the job, really,' he replied.

Oh yes, if you pick up a taxi now at the rank outside Flinders Street station you can take your pick of conversations: arts graduate — expert on the classics, poet, lawyer, civil rights specialist, engineer, nuclear physicist, or chemist. So far, doctors have not reached this stage, but the day may not be far off when your cab man could be a graduate psychiatrist — undoubtedly an ideal man to listen to your troubles.

A number of vice chancellors in Melbourne have pointed out, very correctly, that it is not the function of a university to be a job factory and it would be quite impossible to accept students on the basis of the number of career jobs likely to be available in, say, five years' time. With great nobility they have stated that a university should be a seat of higher learning, a place where learning can be acquired for its own sake.

This is entirely as it should be but as one who has witnessed the sufferings of unemployed students, I wonder if we couldn't have a course, a sort of enclave in the university, which would be brutally practical. Admittedly we are turning out bachelors of commerce and of economics, but even these courses are inclined to dwell on the beauty of pure theory. I suspect we are already getting a bachelor of commerce taxi driver.

I have worried for some time over the title of our new degree. Bachelor of Materialism would cover it and our graduates would be entitled to put BM after their names.

However, we would not go to Oxford or Cambridge for our lecturers. We would not look for fancy degrees at all. Our lecturers would simply have made a pile of money in business.

We would have to examine candidates very carefully, but a number of possibilities come quickly to mind. For a good young lecturer I like the idea of George Frew, the man who put up that $4 million Old Melbourne Motor Inn and who has been rapidly gaining personal ownership of the Commodore motel chain.

As another senior lecturer why not Bruce Small of the Gold Coast? Not only has he shown skill in handling a difficult local council but he has made several fortunes in his lifetime, first out of manufacturing with Malvern Star and then out of real estate.

David Wynn of Wynvale Wines also interests me. His business was taken over for $7 million. The whole business of taking over and being taken over at the right price, while still maintaining control of your business is a special art in 1972 and in my view a top faculty lecturer.

What about the ins and outs of the share market? I think I would like a man who has been through it all literally — in one side and out the other. How about Norman Shierlaw, the man whose family owns 200,000 shares in Poseidon. For students doing a thesis on the mining boom he could provide admirable guidance.

However I still have to find a dean of the faculty. I think the choice is obvious — Sir Reginald Ansett. In the past year he has proved himself the master of business in Australia and he could even call in Sir Henry Bolte for the conferring of degrees.

For this new course we would make our students look daringly different, even kinky. They would wear their hair cut in the nostalgic manner of the thirties — short back and sides. They would wear shirts, ties — suits even. Then, seeing this course is a bachelor of materialism there would be none of the leisurely nonsense usually associated with universities — four months off at Christmas and all that sort of thing. BM's would work straight through. The only time off would be for special money making assignments, as approved by Sir Reginald.

If we could get 50 graduate BM's a year we could fix the sluggish Australian financial situation as fast as you bat your eyelid.
