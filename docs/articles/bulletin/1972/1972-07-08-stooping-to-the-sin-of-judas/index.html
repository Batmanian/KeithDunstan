Stooping to the Sin of Judas

5 min read.

First published 08 Jul 1972

Victoria's expansion of legalised gambling from Tattersalls to Tattslotto and football pools prompts a survey of the clergy's silence compared to their outrage in 1953, and a tongue-in-cheek call for a transport lottery to cover the railways' $36 million annual loss.

AH YES, we remember very well the year Victoria stole Tattersall's from Tasmania. It was in September 1953. The Reverend Crichton Barr at Scots Church said: 'A lottery is equal in potential ruin to persistent drunkenness. It is an appeal to naked greed.' Archdeacon Schofield at St Paul's said: 'It is the utter negation of brotherly love. It is the sin of Judas.' Dr Irving Benson said in the Wesley Church: 'Do they believe that this bad thing will promote the moral vitality of Victoria? They must know that it will swell the tide of paganism.' Then the Reverend Dr A. H. Wood added: 'No self-respecting government would stoop so low as to give over the raising of public finance to a private lottery.'

Wouldn't they indeed? It is sad to have to report that here in Melbourne we have sunk even further. We now have Tattslotto. Yet oddly enough this time the clergy have been almost silent. Perhaps it is the picture of old George Adams which still appears on all Tattersalls material. His beard makes him look so trustworthy, a sort of cross between Edward VII, George V and Bob Hawke.

Tatts has been after this Tattslotto for a long time. Its director Alan Lovell came back from Europe hugely impressed. He reported that the drawing of lotto on TV in West Germany had an audience of 25 million. It caused a beautiful sepulchral silence through the German nation similar to the running of the Melbourne Cup.

Alan Bell thinks the old Tatts lottery is the best deal. Both have several smaller prizes. The computer finds that your chances of winning a prize in the old Tatts are one in 78. Your chances in Tattslotto are one in 108 and you pay more for it. Except of course you have the added excitement of participating by watching the draw on television.

We get that now on Thursday nights in Melbourne and naturally there is a gorgeous looking hostess to draw the lucky number — Lucy Kiraly, 22, who we are told can speak four languages.

But even Lucy is hardly as gorgeous as the prose in the full page Tattslotto advertisements. The ads suggest ways you could go about spending your $50,000 first prize money.

There are pictures of a Rolls Royce and an Alfa Romeo and the blurb starts thus: 'Perhaps sir would like a new limousine? Notice how the world calls you sir when you are rich. Something solid and British or maybe something racy and Italian? On second thoughts why not have both... Paintings? Yes, you've been blinded by white walls for too long. And that gallery dealer, the one you feel you have to apologise to for leaving empty-handed when he tells you the asking price is $3000 — won't it be nice to tell him to wrap it. Travel? Of course you'd like to do the world slowly. And when your snooty friends tell you they're touring the alps, won't it be nice to answer: "So am I — the Swiss Alps."'

Well, what hope do you have? To find out for you Alan Bell at Melbourne University fed the details into the computer. He found that the chances of winning first prize on a dollar ticket are one in 3.8 million. The chances on ordinary Tatts for 60 cents are one in 200,000.

In fairness to Tattslotto you get four chances for a dollar which reduces those odds to one in 950,000. Ah, but don't you see? The first prize does not go off every time. It might come up only once every three or four weeks.

But Tattslotto is not all. Remember back in 1969. Sir Henry Bolte gave a famous press conference in London. He told reporters that Victoria was a more successful state than New South Wales and when asked whether he believed that fruit machines had broken down the moral fibre of the people of New South Wales he said: 'I do and I'm convinced of it. And I've got facts. Home ownership is greater in Victoria. Our savings are $100 a person higher.'

Sir Henry is still against poker machines but meantime he is finding other ways to swell the tide of paganism. His next 'sin of Judas' will be football pools starting in August.

We now have the name for this pool. One newspaper has suggested that it would be 'footylotto.' But now seeing that Tatts has the honour of running this one, it is to be Tattspools and you can take it for granted there will be an Aussie Rules football on every ticket.

The choices for gambling are now magnificent. You can have your choice of horses, dogs, humans or cold, prosaic numbers.

There was a time when the best known local institution was the corner pub. But pubs are tending to die rather than proliferate. The best known meeting place is now the local TAB — cold and clinical.

So you have the TAB, Tattersalls, Tattslotto and football pools. Sir Henry has now dropped very broad hints that soon we will have a separate footy pool based on local soccer.

All these things provide a slice for the government and we are moving steadily toward the blissful day when we will have a full lottery economy.

After the soccer pools it may be difficult to know which way to turn next. I think I would suggest a transport lottery with lucky numbers and lucky train and tram rides.

This would be a great help. After all, our trains lost $36 million in the past year. A couple of swift lotteries could knock that off in no time.
