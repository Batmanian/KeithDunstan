Bazza Arrives on the Silver Screen

5 min read.

First published 19 Aug 1972

A preview of the forthcoming film "The Adventures of Barry McKenzie" at Sydney University finds Barry Crocker as a lovable ocker innocent amid rapacious Poms, complete with a chunder song, a colour chunder scene, and a supporting cast including Spike Milligan, Peter Cook and Dennis Price.

YOU APPRECIATE that there is a huge unsatisfied demand at a top government level for a noble Australian film which will show us for what we are — a cultured, clean-living, hard-working people, in a land of broad, clean beaches where the sun always shines: protest free, harmonious labor relations, awfully enlightened and generous with our black brothers... that sort of thing.

You will remember the horrified cries when Summer of the Seventeenth Doll went to London. People thought the play was 'sordid,' 'nasty,' and 'showed off Australia in an unfavourable light.' The One Day of the Year was another disagreeable show. Mr C. E. Barnes MHR described it as part of a scheme to lessen the achievements of Australian soldiers in two world wars.

But at last we have a film which will really please Mr Barnes, one that shows off the genuine Australian in a well-rounded, exhilarating light — The Adventures of Barry McKenzie.

As you know, Bazza McKenzie is the creation of Barry Humphries, and the first step to ease him more completely into our lives was the release last week of the Bazza record. On one side is the love theme from Bazza, a haunting, moving song that brings an Earl's Court tear to your eye. On the other side you have the song about the adventures of Bazza McKenzie. The second verse goes:

He's got a great big grin
And a great big chin.
Give him half a chance,
He'll be in like Flynn,
His great big fist will flatten any Pom.
He's got a great big thirst,
You can do your worst,
When they tap the keg he'll be in there first.
It's no use mate you'll never win,
He's a better man than you are,
Gunga Din.

The film should open in Sydney and Melbourne in mid-September. There was a sneak preview last week at the Union Theatre, Sydney University. The idea was to test it for reactions on ordinary Australians. Your correspondent, who is no film critic, but very ordinary, adored it.

There were a number of cultural firsts. One was the first chunder on wide screen colour. Bazza (Barry Crocker) in a strait jacket chunders over the head of the psychiatrist, Meyer De Lamphry (Barry Humphries).

There is also the chunder song:

I've had liquid laughs in bars,
And I've hurled from moving cars,
And I've chucked when and where it suited me,
But if I could choose a spot
To regurgitate me lot,
Then I'd chunder in the old Pacific sea.

Actually the chunder scene is quite sensitively done — guaranteed not to offend. The material used was a composition of potato salad and sliced carrot.

Over the years there have been several attempts to bring alive comic strip characters — L'il Abner, Dagwood, Rip Kirby — none of them with much success. Ah, but Barry Crocker as Bazza is beautiful. He shows off remarkable cultural achievements, like being able to open a tube of the chilled article with one hand and give himself a swift amber transfusion with the speed of light. He wears the big hat, the double-breasted R. G. Menzies suit and he is utterly unlike any Australian in Earl's Court today. He is the mythical Australian seen through the eyes of The Sunday Times.

His language too has a curious Frank Hardy flavour, containing sufficient ingenuity to take over the world. Some examples: 'She bangs like a shithouse door in a gale,' 'I'm as dry as a nun's nasty,' 'I'm as dry as a dead dingo's donger,' or 'I hope your chooks turn to emus and kick down your dunny.' And when an arrogant Pom asks Bazza about his convict origins he disarmingly replies: 'Go and dip your left eye in cocky shit.'

This may sound a little over eloquent, but Crocker comes out of it as an oddly lovable character. You will be pleased to hear that even under some very distressing circumstances he fails to lose his virginity. In one scene he visits his Australian mate, Curly, in his Earl's Court flat. Curly has a nude female lying on the bed. In the correct Australian manner she is never spoken to or even noticed. The men get stuck into their ambrosia and have a drunken, amiable wrestle. Silver screen purists will discern a great parallel between this wrestle and the nude wrestle from Women in Love.

Bazza is the sweet, honest Australian innocent who is mercilessly treated by the rapacious twisted Poms. I understand from producer Phillip Adams, they needed a special truck filled with rubbish to give Earl's Court the ideal English atmosphere. They even had to gather dog 'William the Thirds' to put on the pavement.

Spike Milligan is the repulsive concierge of the Kangaroo Valley hotel: 'Bathroom in the basement, toilet on the fifth floor, lavish breakfast 6am to 6.15am. No visitors after 7pm. We require three weeks rent in advance. That's 43 guineas, Sir.'

Peter Cook is the campish BBC producer, Dick Bentley, the vice squad poofter-finder. But the Hollywood Academy Award for the finest supporting role could go to Dennis Price, who as Mr Gort, inveigles Bazza to his den, puts on his old school uniform and hands him a cane.

As he bends over, bottom up, he says: 'You'd better thrash me, Sir. Go on, don't spare me. I deserve it, Sir. Flay me. Do as you will with me. I'm naughty. Look, I've spilt ink on your study floor. Give me a taste of your iron discipline.'

And Bazza says: 'Struth, I'll never understand the flamin' Poms.'

How wise the Commonwealth government was to ban Barry McKenzie for being obscene then to subsidise him through the Commonwealth Film Corporation with $250,000. This film can't fail. It will be the biggest success since The Sentimental Bloke.

Source: out and about Bazza arrives on the silver screen, The Bulletin, 19 August 1972. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
