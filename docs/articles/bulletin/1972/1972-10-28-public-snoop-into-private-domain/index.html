Public Snoop into Private Domain

5 min read.

First published 28 Oct 1972

A tour of Melbourne's post-football display home season, cataloguing the year's fashion for Spanish-style brick arches and adobe, the proliferation of royal and explorer names, and the deep psychological satisfaction of snooping through someone else's house for free.

AFTER the football season Melbourne dies and becomes curiously limp. The newspapers are filled with non-news. Taxi drivers complain they cannot get fares and it is a bad time even for show business.

Ah, but the nurseries look forward to this period with great happiness. In the two or three weekends after the Grand Final they do their finest business for the year. Gardens that have been totally neglected for five months are dug over again and there is an enormous trade in azaleas, blood and bone, dahlia corms, fuchsias and such.

It is also the opening of the display home season. You must appreciate display homes are in the remote fringes of the city in areas with unlikely names like Heatherton, Lalor, Coomaroo, Hopper's Crossing and West Melton. They are inevitably in the sort of places where children's parties take place, particularly when you have to do the roster.

But display housing is Melbourne's most popular post-footy pastime. It provides the ideal Saturday-Sunday outing in a city where there is nothing else to do. It also fills a deep psychological need. Nine out of 10 people who go to look at display houses don't really want to buy anything. There is just that beautiful satisfaction of looking through a house that is not theirs, of getting a free snoop into another domain.

The thing to do is to plan your course on Saturday morning. Both the Sun and the Age now have enormous property guides filled with sketches and how-to-get-there charts. They are not always called display houses: sometimes they are 'Exhibition' or 'Exposition Homes'. Exposition is rather a noble title and you only hope that the exposition home at Lower Templestowe with the 'walk-in robe/dressing room off master bedroom with antique brick feature wall in lounge' is the equal of the Grand Paris Exposition of 1868.

To be honest I have been a student of display houses for many years. Before, the builders used to have their standard models and they never changed much. Now they all have their own architects and just as with new cars or new refrigerators it is utterly essential to bring out new models every season. Like cars they all have names.

Eagerly going through my supplements I note that there is a Tewkesbury, a Knightsbridge in the Regency style. There is a Philip II and a Philip III, naturally in honour of His Royal Highness Prince Philip.

There is also a Barrington, '12 2/3rd squares, built-in wardrobes in bedroom, fully tiled bathroom, large lounge and dining.' I am unsure about that one — it could well be a good drinking house named in honour of Barrington McKenzie.

The pioneer bit is also in. You can get the Endeavour. I would have thought something similar to Captain Cook's cottage in the Fitzroy Gardens might have suited this one, but no, there are wide verandahs and landscape windows. And if you don't like the Endeavour, you can go for the La Trobe 'a new home with new ideas and new ways of capturing the good old Australian colonial era.'

Even more prestigious are the homes named after Australian painters. You can have the Drysdale. Immediately this gives one a vision of the Drysdale squatter's wife series, a tired beat up, weather-beaten shack with a country lavatory standing lonely against the horizon. But no, you get a very stately, two-storey, Toorak-looking place in vintage brick.

There is also the Lindsay. They give no indication which Lindsay is receiving the honour. However, if it is Norman then one would expect a very lusty home full of voluptuous, busty nudes all with shoulders and thighs like Ron Barassi.

But if you want the real truth this is the year of Spain. It would be no exaggeration to say that three out of four houses being built in Darkest Doncaster and Nicest Nunawading have gorgeous brick arches or the lovable adobe look. You can have the Granada, the Cadiz, the Copacabana, the El Rancho, the Seville, the Tijuana, the Isabella, the Vista Grande and even the Alamo. Davy Crockett perished at the Alamo. One hopes that they give out a coon skin cap with every purchase.

On a more business-like basis, there is the house called the Executive. Seeing that nearly everyone thinks they are an executive or are about to become one this is an ideal name for a house. Also there is the Graduate, slightly more expensive than the Executive. It is disappointing however that they do not also have a house called the Dropout for the 40 or 50 per cent who never quite get there.

You can get the Reno, another charming little place heavily under the influence of the Mediterranean. Presumably this is a house for divorcees. It will be interesting to see how the 1973 models develop. It would be nice to see a handsome place called the Alimony.

Some places are named after cars. There is the Kingswood and the Statesman. We don't as yet have the Toyota, the Mazda and the Datsun, but there is a Glenishi, of 'modern Japanese design.' I haven't been inside so I can't tell you whether it is bereft of all furniture. And believe it or not there is also the Bali Hi... 'Polynesian design with the carefree influence of the islands.' Yes, you sing 'Some enchanted evening' every morning under the shower.

There is also the Settler... 'a home with a touch of Australiana, in the true sense a homestead.' It does have a verandah on one side with some slim verandah poles. But personally I think the verandah should go right around: the roof should be galvanised iron and they should at least have a water tank and a windmill.

Over all there does seem to be a lack of inspiration in the use of names. It is a wonder that they have not thought of the War Service, the Overdraft, the Mortgage, the Double Mortgage, the Tattslotto, the F111 and the Opera House.
