Tourists Adore This Horror

5 min read.

First published 11 Mar 1972

The irony of Melbourne's two jails in 1972, with the old Russell Street Gaol opened to tourists by the National Trust while Pentridge in Coburg makes the news for prisoner riots and conditions John Galbally called an affront to human dignity.

IF YOU are an irony lover there is a choice piece of irony to be savoured this week. It is to do with our two jails. The Government has passed the Melbourne jail in Russell Street over to the National Trust. It has an 'A' grading and is 'to be preserved at all costs'. We even had a formal opening, performed by the Minister for Public Works, Mr Murray Byrne.

The jail is open seven days a week. 50 cents for adults, 15 cents for children. It's an ideal tourist attraction, the sort of thing tourists adore, our number one horror, dating from 1841. About 140 of our citizens were hanged there, including Ned Kelly.

The Trust has done a marvellous job of restoration. They did run into a few problems with the Health Department though. They had to install public lavatories. The Ladies is in one of the two condemned cells, now tiled and quite comfortable, and the Men's is in a similar cell, one gallery above. After coming out of there you look down directly upon the execution platform where Kelly and all the others died. Just to add to the richness of the occasion, the Trust has placed a fresh noose on the great crossbeam. Mick Jagger, you will remember, was also 'hanged' at the same spot.

However, the most startling feature for tourists is the flogging triangle installed in the open space under the stairs around which the prisoners used to bang their tin dishes when an execution was taking place. This is the real thing, a macabre Melburnian antique. It is on permanent loan from Pentridge. A triangle made of four inch wooden beams, it stands about ten feet high. There is a slightly inclined platform on which the prisoner places his bare chest. This is felt-lined — for comfort, you understand. Then there are straps for the wrists and waist so that the prisoner cannot move an inch.

In an illuminated box alongside, labelled 'By courtesy of the Governor, H.M. Prison Pentridge,' there are two cat-o'-nine-tails with a four inch wide belt to put around the prisoner's waist to protect his kidneys, and a leather collar to save his neck.

This is the gear that was last used on 1 April 1958 for the flogging of William John O'Meally. Actually flogging is still on the statute books. John Galbally in the Legislative Council last year made yet another valiant effort to have the old torture stopped but failed.

So now for 50 cents a time (15 cents for children) tourists can get a real insight into how it could be done in Victoria.

Meanwhile there are serious problems at the junior jail, Pentridge. This jail was begun in 1850 to relieve the intolerable overcrowding problems at the Melbourne jail. It too is an appalling old horror. In 1970 the L. J. Hooker organisation offered to do a swop. If the Government gave them the 62 acres at Coburg then they would build a fine new jail at Campbellfield. The Government was very interested for a week or two, but alas, it did not come off. The building of a new jail would have cost well over $4 million.

Yet for the past few months there has been a running story of unsettled conditions at Pentridge. For example, on 17 February, 200 prisoners refused to leave their mess hall. They banged their plates and yelled defiance for three hours. Finally police had to be called in to move them out. Prison officers say that some weeks before the acting Governor, Mr Roy Vorden, was pushed to the ground by a mob of drunken prisoners in the vegetable garden. The prisoners had been making home brew out of carrots, turnips and other vegetables.

Then on 26 February, according to the Age, 150 prisoners in D division refused to go to their cells. The jail officers called together 50 wardens and armed them with three-foot-long batons with which to charge the prisoners. One prisoner had his arm broken, others suffered cuts and bruises.

Much of the trouble at Pentridge is due to its antiquity. Some of the older divisions like C division have cells in the classic style, resembling something out of an old Ronald Colman movie. They have little ventilation and are really tiny. The prisoners are locked up for 14 hours at a time. There is no running water, no sewerage. In the old Arthur Koestler tradition, they have a slop bucket.

John Galbally, speaking on 3KZ's Labor hour, was very eloquent. He said: 'Pentridge is the worst jail in Australia and should be pulled down. It is a blot on Coburg and a blight on Melbourne and an affront to human dignity.'

So there's your irony. We have one jail making the news because it is open and the other making the news because it is very closed.

Perhaps we could make Pentridge a tourist attraction. It is not quite as convenient as the Melbourne jail, but it is almost as old. It actually has an 'A' grading from the National Trust and is deemed to be 'notable, worthy of preservation.' Who knows, if we could get enough tourists through there perhaps we would pay for a new jail, or at least get better justice.

Or will we only get a new triangle?
