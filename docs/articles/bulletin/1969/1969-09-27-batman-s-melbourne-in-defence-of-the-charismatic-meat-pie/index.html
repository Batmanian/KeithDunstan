In Defence of the Charismatic Meat Pie

6 min read.

First published 27 Sep 1969

Sydney editorial attacks on the Australian Tourist Commission for flying meat pies to Tokyo provoke Keith Dunstan to defend Melbourne's two million pies a week, propose a national pie secretariat modelled on the wool promotion board, and produce an imaginary 1869 dialogue in which Tina Carr explains to her husband Graham that the attic of air in his pie is her own invention to keep it hot and fresh.

IN THE PAST few weeks almost every newspaper in Sydney has assaulted the Australian Tourist Commission. The ATC sent a jet aeroplane loaded with meat pies, tomato sauce and juicy savs to Tokyo to help woo 3500 American travel agents. Sydney editorials claimed we were not creating a sufficiently couth image.

As a veteran pie-eater from, perhaps, the pie capital of the world, could I beg to disagree? Melburnians eat more than two million pies every week. We have in the city an extraordinary place called a pieteria. It is open 24 hours daily. Indeed, it has no doors just to prove that it never shuts. It is a deep comfort to pie lovers to know it is always there at any hour.

The big firms deliver pies in radio-controlled vans. Should a shortage overtake any section of the town, should there be a desperate pie shortage at, say, Dandenong or Moorabbin, cars can be directed there at once.

Furthermore we possess some of the world's finest pie machinery, and we even export pie-making machines. They produce up to 1200 dozen pies an hour. Think of that, every hour 14,400 pies. We are now exporting pies, looking for pie markets throughout Asia and the U.S. It is vital that we should improve what erudite leader writers call the charisma of the pie.

We have no true national diet. Many eminent historians say our national dish is goat and galah. To prove this Donald Friend painted goat and galah in a luscious mural on the walls of Lennons Hotel, Brisbane. But you can't get goat and galah at the Melbourne Cricket Ground. Some enthusiasts suggest steak and eggs, but the charismatic rating of this dish is particularly low. The only serious rival is the dim sim, which, I understand, was actually invented in Bourke Street in 1863. But, no, the meat pie is the dish on which we can build.

Oh, I know you are going to say there are pies the world over. This is true. But take the traditional English pie. It is a pork pie and always eaten cold. Normally it is eaten on a plate with a knife and fork, whereas, correctly, the Australian pie should be eaten in the open with sauce dripping through the fingers.

In America, they go almost entirely for fruit pies — cherry pies, strawberry pies, grape pies, pumpkin pies and what not. They know virtually nothing of the glories of pie 'n' sauce.

What we should do is form an international pie secretariat and promote the Australian pie in the same manner that we promote wool. I mean, look what the Cornish have done for the cornish pasty. By tradition a cornish pasty should be a portable meal capable of being dropped down a mineshaft without breaking or bouncing.

Oh, our public relations is starting to get under way. You will have noticed that Vin Browne's champion cycling effort a few weeks ago was sponsored by Four 'n' Twenty Pies. He had Four 'n' Twenty across his chest, on his cap. He was photographed everywhere eating pies, and there were full-page ads in our papers with the line: 'THAT'S OUR BOY. WHAT A CHAMPION! WHAT A FANTASTIC FEAT OF COURAGE AND STAMINA!' The message was that had he not been a pie lover he would have been unable to pedal 5234 miles in 24 hours to break Oppy's world record.

The Melbourne 'Herald' also has played its part. Several months back it sought out Roy Irvine, Melbourne's champion pie-eater, who claims to have eaten 16,000 pies. He recommends the inverted Napoleon hat approach: 'Take a meat pie in your left hand, open the bag and carefully rip down the right side. Bend the sides back to make a triangular container shaped like the hat Napoleon used to wear. To slit the side properly you should use the fourth finger of the right hand. That's the Toorak method. Hold the pie in both hands and eat from left to right, keeping the pie even. When you have consumed the pie, tip the container up and drink the gravy and tomato sauce which will have fallen on the bottom. Wipe lips and fingers with the bag and place it in a neat pile.'

This is the sort of information we need to create the charisma of the Australian pie. I have always felt that we need a national pie week and with every pie we could give a little pamphlet which would tell the whole history of pie-making in Australia. Pie week could be celebrated either during Grand Final Week or during the Melbourne Cup.

It just so happens that while cleaning out the woodshed, in an old trunk of papers I came across this authentic little play. It was Grand Final Week, 1869. Graham Carr, of Smith Street, Collingwood, was in a great hurry.

Graham Carr: Aw, step on it, Tina. I've gotta get to the footy. The Redlegs are playing the Maggies.

Tina Carr: I have only one pair of hands and I have no veges for your dinner.

Graham: Throw anything in — you always do.

Tina: I have nothing but this pastry and gravy.

(Tina passes over remarkably flat, oval-shaped creation.)

Graham: Eeeeeeeek! Wot you trying to do, scald a man? Gimme something I can hold.

Tina: I have only got the tomato sauce.

Graham: Mmmmmmmmm. Say, this isn't bad.

(Graham savages pie, sauce and gravy running all over his fingers.)

Graham: I do like this. It is a real meal in a moment.

Tina: Oh, dearest. You would like its taste of pure quality beef made purely from contested scours. But why is there a great vacant allotment up top between the meat and the oven-crisp, crunchy pastry?

Graham: Tina, is that vacant or what?

Tina: This is an insulation area of air to help keep the pies hot, always fresh and always tasty. My own invention actually.

Graham: You're a champ, Tina. All I can say is — oh, what a lovely pie.

(Lights are dimmed. Exit beaming Graham, taking bags of spare pies on his way to footy.)

This little play, which completely depicts Tina Carr's ingenious invention of our national dish, one trusts will be made into a full-length colour movie, starring Mick Jagger and Marianne.
