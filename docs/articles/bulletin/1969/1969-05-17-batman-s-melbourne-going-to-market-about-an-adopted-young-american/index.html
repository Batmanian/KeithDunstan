Going to Market About an Adopted Young American

5 min read.

First published 17 May 1969

East Burwood's new K-Mart, the first Australian outpost of the American Kresge Company's discount chain, opens to 5000 customers on its first morning, offering 50 departments and 40,000 items under one vast roof, complete with no-frills pricing, buzzer-controlled lavatories, and a smoking ban copied directly from the American originals.

THE LATEST ISSUE of the magazine 'Architect,' editor Robin Boyd, carries this line: 'We still take practically all our ideas ready made from the world we have lately joined and give back practically none in return. We copy now as much as our grandfathers copied the Gothic revival and our fathers copied Woolworth Gothic. Anyone with the well-being of Australian architecture at heart must watch with despair the slothful but inexorable approach to these shores of every cliché that sweeps through the international design journals.'

We have been the first to accept the supermarkets, the drive-in theatres, drive-in bottle departments, our steady Americanisation has gone all the way, from foreign policy to finger-lickin'-good chicken. At East Burwood, in Melbourne, we have now received the latest American baby, the K-Mart, a third-generation descendant of the supermarket and the suburban shopping centre. It cost $2.5 million and was billed as 'the largest one-level shopping area in Australia.'

The K is in honour of the Kresge Company of the U.S., which has built 280 in the U.S. The Kresge people are putting in 51 percent of the capital in Australia, and the Coles people are putting in 49 percent.

Now the theory of a K-Mart discount house is this: Unlike a Myer's or a David Jones' shopping centre, you don't graciously provide variety by bringing in your rivals. You take a high-density area of suburbia, put up a single building, and surround it with a huge area of bitumen. Inside that building there is just a big area, like the inside of General Motors'. There are no partitions, just pure beautiful space for naked selling. Everything that could be wanted by a human being, except maybe pot and dirty books, is there, from dog biscuits through to TV sets, from legs of lamb to car tyres, from plastic wastepaper baskets to gnomes for the garden. The theory is that colossal turnover means very low prices.

Your correspondent drove out to do his shopping. The K-Mart is a long, totally unadorned building surrounded by enough bitumen to take 750 cars. There was no sculpture, no garden, no grass, but there were some little trees and shrubs in some rock arrangements.

The story is the same as an ordinary supermarket. All cash, no credit, no deliveries, but if you buy something decent like a refrigerator you don't have to carry it to your car, and they will give you lay-by.

There were only two doors, and on one of them there was the message BAGS TAKEN INTO THIS BUILDING MUST BE OPENED FOR INSPECTION BEFORE YOU GO. In the general-goods area there was a solid bank of 14 cash-register check-out points, and more of them in the food department farther down. There were chrome basket trolleys in squadrons, battalions, and divisions. We were told with pride that the interior fittings exactly duplicated the interior fittings of the latest American K-Marts. Everything was at precisely the right temptingly reachable height, nothing too high, nothing too long, all one had to do was take.

Altogether, we are told there were 50 departments under one roof at East Burwood, 40,000 different items worth $1 million, and Melburnians were not shy. Over 5000 — to use the 'Herald's' words — stormed the store at 9.30 a.m. on the first day.

Say one wants to go to the lavatory. There are only two, and the Ladies' and Gentlemen's are beside each other. So you queue up together. There is a notice: 'Door release controlled by lay-by office. Push only when buzzer sounds. No merchandise to be taken into this room.' The lay-by girl is sitting about six yards away from the lavatory line. She runs a careful eye over you, pushes the buzzer, then you are free to go into the rather small lavatory.

After this experience one thought it would be interesting to ask her what this was all about. 'I don't know,' said she, 'but I can tell you I'm sick of pushing this buzzer.' She also suggested it was obvious there was insufficient toilet space and they would have to put in another. This was hardly satisfactory, so one asked at the information counter, why the buzzer deal? Here the answer was: 'I am sorry, but you see there are a lot of dishonest people in the world. Some people will go into that lavatory with all sorts of dresses and underclothing. They will put them on, and then put their own clothes on again over that. They walk out and we are unable to stop them.'

One noticed another fascinating piece of discipline. Smoking is not permitted in American K-Marts, nor is smoking permitted in the East Burwood K-Mart. One has to go to the cafeteria.

There will be four more K-Marts by Christmas, at Blacktown, N.S.W., at Ingle Farm and Kurralta Park, South Australia, and Morley, Western Australia. And your correspondent also spotted a news item the other day: it said that K-Mart had paid a closed sum for the two-storey motel on 2¾ acres at Belmont, Geelong. It was called Buena Vista. It looked like a young piece of Americanisation eating up the previous generation of Americanisation. Yet another Americanism.
