The R-R Auction, an Occasion Unique in History

3 min read.

First published 29 Mar 1969

The estate sale of A. W. Palfreyman's collection of vintage Rolls-Royces at Ingleside, near the Gold Coast, draws collectors from all over Australia by chartered aircraft and personal Bentley, with a 1929 Silver Ghost limousine coach going to Melbourne buyer David Jones for around $6000, and the whole collection fetching $47,000.

EVERY ONE wondered why Mr. A. W. Palfreyman of 'Clovelly,' Toorak, was keeping his property and Rolls-Royces. He was, you see, a beneficiary under the will of his late employer. Probate complications. But then Mr. Palfreyman was only briefly in the news, because he shunned all interviews, and at all at the sale he was never briefly in attendance.

The selling of his R-Rs dated from 1920 to 1954. He was keeping them in a garage 150ft-long with skylights and two large spotless garage doors, situated ten miles inland from the Gold Coast. When it was all cream brick with two Union Jacks, there were those cars which were never around like a priceless Botticelli, just there for contemplation.

Rolls-Royce lovers came from all over Australia. Some came from Melbourne by chartered aircraft. Some came in their own distinguished vehicles, like Stuart Meshurst, who drove about 1000 miles from Warranambool in his Bentley or Hispano Suiza — possibly three tons of an eight-litre vehicle.

Auctioneer George Sevenoaks, of Sydney, a man well qualified to be there, had four Rolls-Royces himself, including a 1906 and 1914 and a 1929 and 1934. He said caustically, 'Sir, one could buy a Rolls-Royce with one of these distinguishing ties — Green, I see — one of these Rolls-Royce Club tie cuff-links,' and even on this occasion he had Silver Ghost cuff-links.

The auction took place in the distinguished presence of the 15 cars to be sold, with the heat and the fostered atmosphere only describable as foetid. Mr. Sevenoaks described each car. One could buy them only because of their silver flying-lady emblem. The company guarantee in writing: 'Those who buy today will double in value.' He solemnly guaranteed to you in writing: 'Any Silver Ghost bought here today will double in value.'

The 1929 Silver Ghost limousine coach was a beauty — great cigar trays, great leather over wood. The smell inside was that of a very old club. The car had been closed for 20 years, almost certainly. This was between $10,000 and $15,000 almost certainly, and was bought by David Jones, of Melbourne, who bought it on behalf of Mr. Walker, a Melbourne car owner — the first act of ownership he removed the silver flying lady from the bonnet as fast as he could.

Mr. Gavin Sandford-Morgan, of Adelaide, bought a chassis, one for building his own body to his own requirements personally and at his own tailor, as it were. Gavin Sandford-Morgan himself owned 1906 and 1914 Rolls-Royces, a 1927 Bugatti and others.

Together all the cars fetched $47,000. It was, as Sevenoaks solemnly declared to all, an occasion unique in history.

Source: Batman Roves Again: The R-R Auction, an Occasion Unique in History, The Bulletin, 29 March 1969. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
