Thirsting for Spirits in the Theatre

3 min read.

First published 05 Apr 1969

Since Melbourne theatres won the right to apply for liquor licences in 1966 and not one has done so, Keith Dunstan mounts a campaign against the deliberate cruelty of watching four hours of Dylan Thomas drinking himself to death, the entire cast of 'The Season of the Seventeenth Doll' drinking real Carlton beer, and Ray Milland being driven to ruin by the banquet scene in La Traviata.

AT THE PREMIERE of Hal Porter's play 'Eden House,' splendid refreshment was never off the stage. On one occasion Miss Bettina Welch was most accused of having consumed enough gin to wash an elephant. Then there was the line 'Bernie doesn't drink.' 'Doesn't drink? The only thing Bernie doesn't drink is petrol.'

There on the stage at St. Martin's was a lovely little marble-topped drinks table. Everything was available there, gin and tonic, beer, whisky, the lot.

It was in February, 1966, that Melbourne at last gained sensible drinking laws. After 50 years of restrictive legislation we won 10 o'clock closing, but that wasn't all — among other things, Melbourne theatres were invited to apply for liquor licences. But how many theatres have applied for them? Not one.

We have been told that it is far too costly to run liquor bars for such short periods, but think of the pure happiness of some theatres in London, or New York, where one can go to a pleasant little theatre bar after the first act and have a whisky, then return refreshed ready for the rest of the play.

'Eden House' is just one of countless thirsty plays. I often think that theatre managements choose their shows deliberately to make people suffer. Remember 'Relatively Speaking,' with John McCallum and Googie Withers? For the entire length of that show, John, looking svelte in his cravat, blazer, and white slacks, was pouring bottles of drinks.

One of the worst ever was 'The One Day of the Year,' the beery story of Anzac Day in Sydney. During its two seasons the cast drank, on-stage, 540 bottles of beer and 900 cups of tea — three bottles and five cups a night.

One time I cornered Mr. Stefan Haag, formerly of the Elizabethan Theatre Trust, on this subject. He said audiences wouldn't suffer so much if they appreciated that most times real liquor was not drunk on stage. Take 'La Traviata' — the Trust used glasses that were tinted to look as if they contained champagne. In truth, they held only water. Do you remember Ray Milland's most famous role — 'The Lost Weekend'? Milland went to see the opera 'La Traviata.' He had to endure the banquet scene. Pretty soon Mr. Milland was not hearing a note; he was following the champagne glasses, and the poor man was desperate for a drink.

My worst thirst night was the opening here of 'Dylan.' For over four hours there was never a bottle out of his hand. The climax came in the last act when Dylan ultimately drank himself to death. Warned that he must never drink again, Dylan went to a New York bar and ordered 18 double whiskies. Can you imagine the helpless feeling of us thirsty ones in the stalls?

We should form a union and blackball all the theatres in town until we are allowed to enter into the spirit of the thing.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Thirsting for Spirits in the Theatre, The Bulletin, 5 April 1969. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
