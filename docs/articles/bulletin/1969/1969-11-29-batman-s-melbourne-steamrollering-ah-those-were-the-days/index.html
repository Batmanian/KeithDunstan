Steamrollering — Ah, Those Were the Days

5 min read.

First published 29 Nov 1969

The Melbourne Steam Traction Club's rally at Wantirna, featuring six fully operating rollers and traction engines including a magnificent 1906 Foden Compound Traction Engine owned by John Smyth of Ringwood, revives Keith Dunstan's childhood passion for steamrollers suppressed since councils replaced Aveling and Porter machines with soulless diesel rollers in the 1950s, and leads to a road test of the 1924 Fowler 6 hp Compound whose bad points include a wheel requiring 45 turns before it ponders altering course.

THE BAD DECADE was the 1950s. The local council of my area decided to dispose of its steamrollers. Career and social advancement for steamroller drivers was limited, so I switched. But you can never entirely sink one's upbringing. Even now there is a faint passionate stirring, a passion at the sight of a steamroller.

Secondly, a steamroller driver has to build up a head of steam. And that meant overtime. The first thing, a noble Aveling and Porter; not over-large, but it had everything. All the pistons, connecting rods, flywheels and such were exposed. So one could see bits and pieces going round and round, thrusting back and forth, throbbing up and down. When its steam-ticket man retired, the council replaced the Aveling and Porter with a diesel-roller — a horrid thing, everything hidden under the bonnet, as impersonal as a refrigerator.

It is an interesting comment on civilisation of the near-'seventies that no child has an ambition to drive a diesel-roller.

And at a time when nostalgia is rampant in the whole community — when there are societies for preserving old houses, old cars, old trams, old trains, old lamps, vintage carriages, even vintage telephones — nobody fights for the old steamrollers.

But you will be pleased to hear that there are now three such organisations, and last week we had our first Melbourne rally, organised by the Melbourne Steam Traction Club.

It took place at Wantirna, out on the Burwood Highway, and the show had six fully operating rollers and traction engines, plus a variety of other motors. You will observe that steam lovers are a repressed minority group. This rally was terribly difficult to stage. Civic groups don't understand the magnificence of these vehicles rolling majestically along our highways. So each machine had to be transported by low-loader at a cost of around $60 each. One could have flattened anything for them very nicely.

Nevertheless, at Wantirna the most beautiful machine of all was a 1906 Foden Compound Traction Engine, 7 hp. It looked immense and it stood there gently chunking, exposing an utterly marvellous, satisfying variety of moving parts. It had a long, flat roof, about 12ft. from the ground. It had lashings of shining brass and a boiler exquisitely decorated in the manner of one of the classic steam expresses like the Flying Scotsman. When I arrived, a young man was placing microphones in all sorts of appropriate spots so that he could record all its lovable hissings and chunkings for ever.

Its owner, John Smyth, of Ringwood, found it in a paddock at Wangaratta, where one time it had been used for stone crushing. He said actually it was designed for the road, a direct ancestor of the Foden semi-trailer. He explained further how this repressed community suffers for its hobby. Before they drive their machines they have to get a steam ticket at the Technical School. A second-class ticket takes two years of study, four nights a week. A first-class ticket takes three years.

There were all sorts of spectacular events at the rally. There was a tug-o-war between Mr. Smyth's Foden and 120 humans. At first, Mr. Smyth humoured the humans, allowing them to think they were winning the battle. Then giving a mere 40lb. pressure of steam he pulled them forward, effortlessly, in a quite humiliating manner.

There was a balloon race in which drivers were required to destroy balloons on the way. There was a ladies' steering race, in which ladies had to drive in a slalom event, both forward and backward. This event was a little unnerving.

But the most spectacular was the Slow Race. It takes great understanding of pressures and subtle control of the throttle to go slowly. It was a beautiful sight, six great behemoths in line, pouring out smoke, whistles squealing, groping their way forward.

It was easy to discern the extroverts. Some engines were burning firewood, which gives no visible smoke. Others were on coal, which pours out smoke in a splendid black, chundering stream. Steam-loving purists prefer the black. Indeed, it was disappointing that the Melbourne Steam Traction Club did not field bookmakers. Those of us who can never pick a horse to win would have appreciated a bet on something with the skill to come last.

At the end of the afternoon it was possible to realise an ambition of more than 30 years. Don Mickle, the club vice-president, offered me the wheel of the Fowler 6 hp Compound, built 1924, late of the Northcote City Council.

Could I give you the benefit of my road tests, notes taken on the spot?

GOOD POINTS: Automatic transmission extremely smooth. No gear pedal. Even better, no brake pedal. For stopping, just put into reverse. Remarkable ventilation. Sound squashing power. Corners securely with no drift and no rubber noise at two knots.

BAD POINTS: Necessary to turn wheel 45 times before it even ponders alteration in course. Suggest power steering. Sometimes goes backward when should be going forward. Takes 90 minutes to start on cold mornings. Parking? Lousy. Hard to fit in your garage.

Regardless of the problems, the society is anxious to expand. The going rate for pre-owned rollers and traction engines is $1000 to $1500, but they are hard to get. According to Don Mickle, only two Melbourne councils still possess a steamroller — Essendon has one and Moorabbin has another. Neither is in regular use. Of course, the club is dwelling desperately on these, but they are opposed by some gentlemen who believe they should go into parks for children.

The club believes that's a shocking fate for an old engine. They are stripped in a week, whereas a club man would keep it, love it, and put it on show with its warm steel heart still beating.
