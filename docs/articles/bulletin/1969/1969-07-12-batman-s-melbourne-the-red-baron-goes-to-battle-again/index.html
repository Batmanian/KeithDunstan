The Red Baron Goes to Battle Again

6 min read.

First published 12 Jul 1969

The Anti-Football League's latest campaign enlists Robert Joyce's skywriting plane, the Red Baron, to write the AFL battle cube and the letters AFL in half-mile smoke at 12,000ft above Melbourne's six simultaneous football grounds, only for the 50-knot wind to carry the message out beyond Dandenong to be seen by sand-belt golfers rather than MCG pie-eaters.

THE ANTI-FOOTBALL LEAGUE has made a new and somewhat sensational drive. You will remember in 1967 Douglas Wilkie, a foreign-affairs commentator on the 'Sun News Pictorial,' invented the idea of this league on behalf of the tortured thousands in Melbourne who could not stand the drenching of the air waves, the plastering of the newspapers with all this turgid information on football.

A badge was cast. It was in the shape of a red cube, symbolic of an object that would not bounce. The theory was that upon seeing the red cube one would realise that here was hope of an intelligent conversation free from football. Over 17,000 were sold and $7000 was raised for the Berry Street Babies' Home.

Once again the badges are selling briskly, there are bumper stickers available which proclaim PRESERVE WINTER SANITY — STAMP OUT FOOTBALL BALLYHOO. There is a Miss Anti-Football contest being run in opposition to the Miss Football contest, and if the Nauru football pools manage to take over, the AFL is contemplating running anti-football pools. This could be done quite simply. Instead of running the contest on the outcome of football matches, artists and art competitions could be used.

The Victorian Football League has its Brownlow Medal for the best and fairest player of the year. The AFL has the Wilkie Medal for its best and fairest, for the person who has done least to promote football during the year.

However, for the twelfth round of League football the AFL had an interesting coup. It engaged a skywriter. Now skywriting is a rare and noble occupation. Melbourne has been without one for ten years. The new firm, called Skyright, is operated by Richard Jenkins and Robert Joyce. They fly an aeroplane called a Champion Citabria leased from Campbell, Hicks Airways.

The theory was to send up the Citabria and proclaim anti-football propaganda in smoke at 12,000ft. The afternoon of the twelfth round was chosen as a masterpiece of military planning. On that day there were games at the MCG, Carlton, South Melbourne, Footscray, Essendon and Collingwood. Therefore, the skywriting could be seen by every football spectator in Melbourne.

Your correspondent managed to secure an exclusive interview with Robert Joyce before he took off. His aeroplane was painted bright red and called the Red Baron. He said the chief problem with skywriting had been finding out how to do it. He explained there were no books on the subject, and no skywriters anywhere ever revealed the secrets of their trade. And one could see the point. Too many skywriters on the job and the sky would become like the 'Herald' classified ads.

So this is how they did it. For days Mr. and Mrs. Jenkins worked out mathematically the size of the letters, the angles of turn required to make them. Then with Joyce up in the air and Jenkins down on the ground, they experimented by two-way radio. As Joyce created letter after letter, Jenkins told him when to make each turn and counted with a metronome.

Robert Joyce said: 'I found you couldn't go for too long. The concentration is fantastic. For a start everything is backwards. I mean you're up above, the readers are down below, so you have to create a mirror version of what they see. As you make each run you count. A letter is half a mile long and there are 20 counts for half a mile. And to get back to complete a letter sometimes your turns have to be fantastically tight, so you won't cut in half the letter you've just made. You have to do stall turns all the time. And if the sun is in the wrong position it's mighty hard to see the smoke.'

Yet Mr. Joyce is particularly proud of a NOON PIES that he created in Melbourne's sky. It hung around for one hour, which he claims is a skywriting record. He feels now that he is sufficiently skilled to advance to cursive script. Mirror versions of cursive script take real handling.

The Red Baron was fitted up as a single-seater, and in the back he had a tank of oil which was atomised and pumped out by an auxiliary diesel motor.

He took off at 2 p.m. It was a cloudless sunny day in Melbourne, but windy. He said wind did not matter for skywriting as long as the passage of air was smooth and not gusting.

He reached 12,000ft. over the Melbourne Cricket Ground halfway through the second quarter. First, with marvellous dexterity, he created in smoke the AFL battle symbol, the cube. Then in magnificent half-mile letters he did the AFL.

Regrettably, at 12,000ft. the wind was blowing at 50 knots. So by the time the Red Baron had completed his message it was way out beyond Dandenong, being seen by the golfers of the sand belt rather than by the pie-eaters at the MCG.

Yet all was not lost. Several days later the 'Sun News Pictorial' printed this letter: 'To me it came like a miracle. During the season my husband carries around his trannie like a wart. And on Saturday afternoon he had it blaring on the handle of his wheelbarrow. Well, just as we got to the forty-ninth barrowload of leaves, I reached breaking point, thoughts of murder or suicide surged through my tortured mind, and in my hopelessness I turned my eyes to heaven — and, O miracle, O happy day, there before my unbelieving gaze was THE CUBE! And as I stood transfixed, an A appeared and then the little plane created an F and an L. Having no beer cans to throw I waved my rake in triumphant gestures and the desecrator was struck dumb. After this great happening I now have the strength to go on living, even until the finals. Yours, etc., Mrs. Lesly Dingle, Mitcham.'

The Anti-Football League will also continue until the finals, relieving the tortured and the suffering. It is now trying to persuade one of the newspapers to produce a lift-out guide on the lines of the racing lift-out guide. This would list all the places on a Saturday where one could go, hear no football, see no football. It would list the pubs that did not have TV sets or radios in their bars, it would list pleasant runs in the country with routes designed to avoid all football crowds and give a lead back to folk culture, pottery-making, art shows in the hills, famous flower gardens ...

O! miracle. O! happy day.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The Red Baron Goes to Battle Again, The Bulletin, 12 July 1969. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
