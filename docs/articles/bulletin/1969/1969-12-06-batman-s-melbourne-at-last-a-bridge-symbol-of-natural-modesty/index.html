At Last, a Bridge: Symbol of Natural Modesty

5 min read.

First published 06 Dec 1969

The West Gate Bridge, costing $42 million and already half finished, gives Melbourne the thumbprint it has always lacked, with its main span of 1000 feet, height of 176 feet above the Yarra's piddling 200 yards, and eight 12-foot lanes quietly surpassing the Sydney Harbour Bridge on every specification except main span length, while Sir Henry Bolte, presented with a silver-plated axe at the 1968 ground-breaking, declared it blunt and denied being a hatchet man.

PHOTOGRAPHERS, when they have wanted to depict the ideal Melbourne, have always gone to the same spot — to the towpath south of the Yarra, just near the Swan Street Bridge. Here the Yarra curved and it was possible to get a very charming shot of the city skyline, including such noble piles as the T & G and the Manchester Unity buildings.

On a very still day you could get a lovely reflection in the water, showing the Manchester Unity and the T & G in duplicate. This was good for the covers of telephone books, city-sights brochures and coffee-table tomes.

But, frankly, we always felt guilty about it. We yearned for some specific object which would symbolise Melbourne. The Yarra scene wasn't good enough. In short, we needed an official thumbprint. Brisbane had the campanile San Marco tower of its City Hall, Sydney had its Harbour Bridge and Adelaide had its Colonel Light statue.

For a time we thought our Arts Centre might make the grade, but no, it's too subdued. Now at last we have something else and this might be the real thing. Right back to the turn of the century we have yearned for a bridge across the Lower Yarra to connect Port Melbourne with Newport and Williamstown. It is a miserable stretch of water, but right from the beginning high-masted craft have sailed up here to berth near Spencer Street, and the bridge would have to be an immense height above the water.

In 1906, two engineers, Messrs. H. V. Champion and J. H. Fraser, had a fascinating solution. They designed a marvellous framework, like a huge triumphal arch in steel, rising 200ft. above the water. They had a platform for cars and vehicles which was to be whisked across at high speed by electricity. The platform, said the engineers, would cause no hindrance to shipping as it moved across.

Ronald Searle could not have done better, and the champion bridge would have been a splendid thumbprint for Melbourne for a mere £18,000. But like our frustrated dream for a subway this was something that could not be solved in a mere half century. A very picturesque ferry continued to cart the cars back and forth at the rate of 1500 a day.

But the big news is that our Lower Yarra bridge is now more than half finished and really something to see. It is because of the natural modesty of Melburnians and their retiring nature that you haven't heard much about this. Also it hurts them cruelly ever to make comparisons with the proud possessions of other cities, but knowing your natural interest we force ourselves to do this. The bridge is costing $42 million and will be the biggest in Australia. Its main span is 1000ft. The Sydney bridge is 1600ft. The height above water of the Lower Yarra bridge will be 176ft. The Sydney bridge is 172ft. The total length of the Harbour Bridge is 3000ft. The Lower Yarra will be over 9000ft. Sydney's bridge has eight lanes 9ft. 10in. wide. The Lower Yarra will have eight lanes 12ft. wide.

Back in April, 1968, Sir Henry Bolte dug the first hole. The Lower Yarra Authority presented him with a silver-plated axe in honour of the occasion. Sir Henry, who always has the right word for the occasion, looked at the axe and said: 'I'm no hatchet man. Anyway, the darn thing's blunt.'

The bridge will be the first thing you see on approaching Melbourne and, like the Harbour Bridge, it will be obvious from all over town. The two main supporting towers will be 350ft. high. About the same height as the spire of St. Patrick's Cathedral. The piers, the main supports for the bridge, are the astonishing sight now. They go for about three miles and they are vast. The tallest of them are about 170ft. high, and at this stage each of them is alone. They look like individual United Nations buildings.

At first sight it really looks as if Melbourne is trying to outdo somebody for the sheer hell of it. The Yarra here is a piddling, dirty piece of water about 200 yards across, which certainly doesn't seem to merit a $42 million bridge. Even so, it does carry ocean-going ships, and 176ft. is the minimum clearance so, to do this with only a four percent gradient, the approaches have to be long.

The idea is that the bridge will take traffic around the city. There will be a superb dash from Dandenong, under the new merry-go-round at St. Kilda Junction, to Kings Way to Western Gate, as they propose to call it, and then out to Geelong Road. Now if you want to get out to the Geelong Road, you have to endure 54 intersections, abominable odours, and 11 sets of traffic lights. With the new bridge there will be none.

There has been an interesting breakthrough. We have been terrified of the toll road. Sir Henry Bolte has always reacted like aunty when someone first suggested to her that she should buy a washing-machine on time payment. Immoral. This is the breakthrough. The system will be the same as the Harbour Bridge. The Lower Yarra Authority is raising the money from banks and insurance houses and toll payments will pay back the money over 40 years. Not much is being said at the moment, but it seems the toll will be 20 to 25 cents.

One thousand men are working on the bridge now, and it is due to open in early 1971. So, in a city where barely a mile of genuine freeway exists, enormous changes are about to happen. The $19 million south-eastern freeway, which carves a fantastic path along the Yarra out past Scotch, out past the Kooyong tennis courts out to Tooronga Road, will be finished in May. The freeway out to Tullamarine is almost ready. Others are planned.

When they are all finished, there may be few parks left, it may be a dreary place for humans. But, who knows, it could be a splendid place for motor-cars.
