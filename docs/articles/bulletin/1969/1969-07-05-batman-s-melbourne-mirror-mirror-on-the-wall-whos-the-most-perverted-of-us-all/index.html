Mirror, Mirror on the Wall, Who's the Most Perverted of Us All?

5 min read.

First published 05 Jul 1969

Melbourne's Vice Squad charges the cast of Boys in the Band with obscene language and the Customs Minister Senator Scott bans Stig Bjorkman's film 'I Love, You Love,' provoking a film-festival seminar at the Palais Theatre where psychologist John Cowl, novelist David Martin, and broadcaster Corinne Kirby argue that obscene material does not corrupt and that the real censorship problem is Australia's broader pattern of repression.

FOR SOME TIME here in Melbourne we were alarmed. Brisbane was doing awfully well with raids on theatres and record shops, even with the banning of charming old Victoriana like Aubrey Beardsley. But now that the Vice Squad has been making visits to performances of 'Boys in the Band,' and charging them with using obscene language, we can breathe out with happiness. Our title was in jeopardy, but there is little doubt now that Melbourne is still the wowser capital of Australia.

And with the Customs Minister and the banning of Stig Bjorkman's film 'I Love, You Love,' your correspondent received 48 letters reminding him that he was a sexual pervert, and only two in favour. An extraordinary number said: 'If you are sincere in what you say I challenge you to put your daughter or wife nude on the stage.' And this letter is worth treasuring: 'Sir, I wonder how many Melbourne citizens are involved in these film festivals? Do we, the ordinary ratepayers, have to contribute some of our hard-earned income to support some of these extraordinary people? I happened to see some pictures of the mob at one of these events and I would hate to think they were represented as Fair Average Australian. Even the director of this year's festival speaks in a peculiar Australian accent, which further makes me think we should oppose all these pseudo-intellectuals, who are of so little importance in the community. I say let Mr. Bjorkman take his effeminate looks and his slushy mind back to where he belongs. Not in this land of sunshine and clean f.a.q. citizens.'

On the last day of the film festival, the director, Erwin Rado, held a seminar on censorship at the Palais Theatre. Some of the comments are worth recording. John Cowl, a consulting psychologist, called for the Commonwealth to set up an advisory board to work on all matters of censorship. This shouldn't be composed of f.a.q. citizens and psychiatrists, but a qualified group of scientists, artists, who could present real evidence on just how much books, films, and such do have an effect on various minds.

The evidence seemed to indicate that obscene material did not deprave or corrupt delinquents and had little effect on perverts. 'A sick mind can be set off by almost anything,' he said, 'shoes, horses, newspapers, a light. A German, one of the worst sex offenders in history, went to a performance of Cecil B. De Mille's "Ten Commandments." Immediately after the show he raped and killed a woman. The film, as far as I know, hasn't been banned anywhere. Haigh, the acid-bath murderer, was influenced and stimulated by an Anglican High Church communion service. When you have this sort of thing causing sexual offences, how can you blame books and films?'

David Martin, the novelist, said: 'The other day I was walking with a friend past a powerhouse, and there was a huge, charming chimney, very phallic, sticking up in the sky. My friend smiled, looked at me, and said: "Envious?" If Senator Scott had read my mind at that moment he would have advocated the removal of the chimney or at least its reduction in size. Beauty is in the eye of the beholder and so is obscenity.'

David Martin made the point that instead of being so terrified of the acts of love and tenderness on the screen we should look at all the violence. Corinne Kirby, the broadcaster, said: 'We live in a society that is extremely violence-orientated, and TV could not exist five minutes without it.'

Bruce Grant, political writer for the 'Age,' tied it all out that politicians found there was very little mileage in liberalism. It was far better to go for law and order. Allan Ashbolt, of the ABC, said censorship couldn't be looked at in isolation. It was all part of a clear pattern of repression in Australia. The Public Service Act restricted the freedom of speech of a quarter of the population. The National Service Act, in a curious ritualistic lottery, sent 20-year-olds off to a war that denied all reason. He called for a protest movement.

Of course, the big problem with 'I Love, You Love' was the sequence which depicted the seven-month-pregnant lady 'sitting astride the male, face to face with him, engaged in actions of a distinctly sexual nature.' Erwin Rado showed a blow-up of that delicate scene. He indicated the position of the knees of both parties. 'Senator Scott,' he said, 'says sexual intercourse was taking place. I assure you, from this, it was a gynaecological impossibility.' He challenged the Minister for Customs, Senator Scott, to screen the scene before an independent panel composed of a member of the Judiciary, a film critic, and a gynaecologist. If they found that sexual intercourse was taking place he would resign his job as director of the festival.

At the time of going to press, nobody resigned. We have no new board, but we are still doing much to maintain our international reputation.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Mirror, Mirror on the Wall, Who's the Most Perverted of Us All?, The Bulletin, 5 July 1969. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
