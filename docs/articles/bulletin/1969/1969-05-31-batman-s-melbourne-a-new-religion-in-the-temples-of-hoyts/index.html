A New Religion in the Temples of Hoyts

6 min read.

First published 31 May 1969

The $4.5 million Hoyts Cinema Centre, opening June 4 with its aluminium spike and Japanese-pagoda silhouette designed by architect Peter Muller under the influence of Frank Lloyd Wright, replaces the splendour of the Hoyts Regent — Melbourne's largest cinema at 3253 seats, whose mighty Wurlitzer organ was sold to a 23-year-old Sydney man for several thousand dollars at its last performance in January.

THE INVITATIONS are out for the opening of the $4.5 million Hoyts Cinema Centre on June 4. Originally it was planned that we would have, right here in Melbourne, a proper galaxy of stars — Steve McQueen, Sidney Poitier, and Faye Dunaway. We would have Genevieve Waite, the blonde South African actress, star of 'Joanna.' But, alas, none of them could come. So instead we put on our black ties and go to the opening which will be 'under the patronage of, and in the distinguished presence of, His Excellency Major-General Sir Rohan Delacombe, Governor of Victoria.'

Unquestionably, the Cinema Centre is different. In the grand era of the 'twenties and 'thirties we built our theatres to look like either Grecian temples or Indian mosques. Now, presumably, in line with our more modern financial tie-ups, we have made a sharp change in religion.

There are ten office floors which taper out as they go up, and on top there is an aluminium spike, like the spike on the helmet of a samurai warrior. The tower reminds one of a Japanese temple — you know, the traditional five-level pagodas, one roof incredibly above another, each representing fire, earth, water, and the rest. The 'design features,' we are told, 'reflect the influences of world-famous architect Frank Lloyd Wright, of whom the Cinema Centre's architect, Peter Muller, of Sydney, was a keen student.' The interior is by Neville Marsh, also of Sydney.

There are three cinemas. Cinema One seats 896, has purple decor, and cerise curtains. Cinema Two seats 832, has orange decor, and chrome-yellow curtains, and Cinema Three is brilliant turquoise and seats 738. Between them they will be able to cater for any type of film produced today — Cinerama, 70mm., the lot. Two of them have wired carpet right up the wall to the ceiling. This is said to provide an utterly dead background and improves the stereo immeasurably.

There is a splendid-looking restaurant. The interior is composed of slatted oiled timber, exposed brick, gold balustrading, terracotta tiles, lots and lots of marvellous good taste. But sentimentalists among us ask, where is the vintage Hoyts? Where is the Wurlitzer organ which should rise out of the bowels of Cinema Three in a pool of light? Where are the cupids, the long-haired, prancing, topless maidens? Alas, there is no organ, no sculpture, not even a Japanese stone lantern or a joss stick.

I tell you all this because the Hoyts Cinema Centre is to replace the old GHQ, the Hoyts Regent. The future of the Regent is a little muddy. There has been story after story that the City Council will buy it for $2 million, and an office-shop development will be placed on the site adjoining the city square. The building is doomed, but it hangs on because the gnomes of Queen and Swanston Streets, apparently, are not yet financially organised.

Meanwhile, the Regent theatre-lovers have been out in force. 'It is absolutely appalling to permit the destruction,' wrote P. L. Burns, of South Yarra. 'Elongated box-like structures spring to life all over our beautiful city devouring gems of the builders' craft which can never be replaced,' wrote Mrs. J. Connellan, of Elsternwick. 'How absolutely disgusting and revolting that the Regent theatre is to be pulled down,' cried Michael M. Gell, of Hawthorn. DO SOMETHING NOW TO STOP WANTON DESTRUCTION, wired Clive Bush, of South Yarra.

The crime of the Regent is that it's too big. This is the age of the mini-theatre. The Regent is the largest movie theatre in Australia. It seats 3253, and if you add the underneath you could house a whole brigade for sending to Vietnam. The exterior, actually, is pedestrian, but inside is utterly splendid. All around the balcony in pressed metal are nice young topless maidens in a design which is pure vintage Hoyts. There are other topless girls playing lutes, there are cupids, and under the dome there are Etruscan friezes, better than the Elgin Marbles. Then there are chandeliers, Corinthian columns, Ionic columns, and acres of scalloped burgundy curtains.

It opened on March 15, 1929, and the 'Herald' brought out a special supplement. 'The greatest theatrical event in the world for 1929,' it was. Said the 'Herald': 'Tonight 3500 people will witness a revelation. When you have seen the Regent, when you have sat spellbound by the pageant of stars on the screen, music, and stage, you will truly know the new entertainment that delights London and New York.'

They had Ronald Colman and Vilma Banky in a performance 'that will wipe out the memory of the finest show you ever saw,' the Regent ballet of 16, the syncopating stage band, the Regent concert orchestra of 45, and 'the mighty Wurlitzer.'

The Wurlitzer, price £30,000, had 284 stops, 560 separate movements, 2,500,000 electric connections, and every darned gadget from a full range of drums and cathedral chimes through to a grand piano, which used to play pneumatically on its own on the stage. The organist used to wear a white tuxedo, and the mighty Wurlitzer rose hydraulically and magically from out of the bowels of the theatre in a pool of white light.

Well, the theatre was burnt out in 1945, and everything was redone almost as it was, except that the mighty Wurlitzer was slightly less mighty and the three-ton chandelier up aloft was replaced with a job only three-quarters of a ton. The new organ came over from Perth and cost £27,000. No longer did the console rise out of the bowels: it was permanent in a box and, sad, the piano was now an upright and hidden from the audience. But Wurlitzer-lovers claim it was still a big deal — 1500 pipes, ranging from 32ft. affairs down to the size of a lead pencil, and it had the full, ghastly range of instruments — chimes, gong, chrysoglot, castanets, marimba, sleighbells, xylophone, glockenspiel, train whistle, and the rest.

The organ has already gone. The last performance was on January 12 before 700 Wurlitzer organ fans, and one of the last numbers was 'In a Little Spanish Town.' The purchaser was Alan Tranter, 23, who planned to install it in his house in Sydney. One gets the impression Hoyts didn't receive a mighty Wurlitzer price. Mr. Tranter said he bought it 'for several thousand dollars.'

Now all we have to look forward to is the opening of the Cinema Centre. We can only hope that we will witness a revelation, that we will sit spellbound by the pageant of stars on screen, music, and stage, and that it will wipe out the memory of the finest show we have ever seen.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: A New Religion in the Temples of Hoyts, The Bulletin, 31 May 1969. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
