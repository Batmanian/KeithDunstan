High Living in an Air-Conditioned Ice-Cube Tray

5 min read.

First published 02 Aug 1969

Three own-your-own apartment towers planned for Melbourne's city centre, including the almost-complete 17-floor Park Tower at 201 Spring Street, revive an Edwardian tradition of wealthy squatters keeping a city seat, now offered with natural-gas heating, a ceramic fountain in the lobby, and a $100,000 penthouse looking down on St. Patrick's Cathedral with a private rear door for tradesmen.

AS YOU KNOW, Batman has always been a vigorous no-progress-for-Collins Street man. This column protested against the pulling down of the old porticos. It protested against the removal of the marvellous old paving stones; it howled with rage over the removal of the knobbly old Victorian lamps and their replacement by horrid things like elongated parking meters. It has sobbed over the destruction of little buildings and cursed every new big one, particularly the Reserve Bank, 18 Collins Street, and now 24 Collins Street. Norman Mailer was best at describing such buildings. He said they were like white refrigerators, like matching white luggage, like portable radios, stereos, plastic compacts, like sugar cubes or ice-cube trays on edge. The latest, 24 Collins Street, is definitely of the ice-cube-tray school.

Batman jumped up and down. They were turning Collins Street into a ghastly tunnel. The shortsighted City Council should at least insist that every new building give up some public space to the citizens. But Batman's record has been beautifully clean. Your page has lost every protest.

Collins Street also has lost prestige in retailing. The shops have steadily moved across to Bourke Street. Hoyts Regent and Hoyts Deluxe are coming down, while theatre after theatre is going up in Bourke Street. One might describe Bourke Street as the 'hard lolly' street of Melbourne.

But wait, there is a new trend in the city. It is the OYO flat in the heart of town. Sydney already has acquired one or two city home-unit towers, but we wouldn't presume to be ahead in such matters. Actually it is merely the return of an old comfort. In the grander, more elegant days it was the done thing for the nice people of the Western District to have their country seat in the city. At those times of the year when the sheep weren't awfully busy and when the wool needed to be sold, they would spend three months or so at the country seat, elegantly. They either lived at Melbourne Mansions, ruthlessly torn down to make way for the CRA portable radio, or at Cliveden Mansions, East Melbourne, torn down to make way for some Hilton white luggage.

Now there are three ice-cube trays being built, or about to be built, all devoted to the cult of Own Your Own. There is 15 Collins Street, 22 floors, due to be finished next year. There is 99 Spring Street, 24 floors, which will also have offices, about to be started, and there is 201 Spring Street, 17 floors, which is almost complete.

The OYO at 201 Spring Street, with a stroke of inspiration, is called Park Tower. It said: 'Park Tower, the first development of exclusive luxury flats in the City of Melbourne, has been designed to provide the discriminating home-purchaser with superior centrally located residential accommodation. Superbly situated uptown, this elegant and imposing building is close to an exciting array of restaurants, theatres, shops, clubs, which contribute to the social and commercial stature of this beautiful city. Park Tower's large picture windows and balconies overlook the picturesque parkland area, which is merely the foreground to a panorama extending as far as the Dandenong Ranges to the east and sweeping across to Port Phillip Bay and the river area, You Yang ranges, etc.'

The architect, Mr. K. Popper, very kindly offered to give a Park Tower guided tour. It is in a good position, fronting the gardens and immediately opposite the Burke and Wills statue. As you know, the statue was put there so that John O'Hara Burke could for ever, longingly, keep an eye on the Princess Theatre, where his girlfriend used to perform.

You go up a spiral staircase to the first-floor lounge, which is large with big glass windows, looking out eyeball-to-eyeball with John O'Hara Burke. There is also a patio with a ceramic fountain by Anne Graham, who sculpted the fountain at the Southern Cross Hotel. The lounge is the greeting place for our tower, and it works on the American security system. From here one calls one's squatter or mining friend, say on the 13th floor, and if he or she thinks you are desirable the door of the lounge magically swings open for a few seconds and you can proceed to greater heights.

Upstairs there are 76 flats, heated and cooled by natural gas. Mr. Popper pointed out that they chose natural gas because you could get separate metering of every apartment. So while you're back home on the range you don't pay. The OYO prices vary from $21,000 for a one-bedroom flat, $47,000 for three bedrooms to $100,000 for the penthouse.

Frankly, I was attracted to the $100,000 17th-floor penthouse. The kitchen was nice with a beautiful large window. One could look down on St. Patrick's Cathedral, almost pat Parliament House on the head, and gaze away wistfully to the Dandenongs. The study had a view of all Melbourne's matching white luggage across to Port Phillip Bay and the patio was large enough for your oleander tubs, azaleas and monstera deliciosa. This penthouse also had the added advantage of a private rear door for the tradesmen.

Do call, you will usually find me there between February and May.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: High Living in an Air-Conditioned Ice-Cube Tray, The Bulletin, 2 August 1969. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
