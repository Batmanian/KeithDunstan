Vice Is a Four-Letter Word

10 min read.

First published 18 Oct 1969

A letter from a bewildered Czech migrant who cannot discover which words Melbourne's vice squad will prosecute leads Keith Dunstan to defend the antiquity of Anglo-Saxon swear words against their proposed suppression, propose National Trust classification for the better ones, and then travel to the Society of Experimental Test Pilots' symposium at the Beverly Hills Hilton, where Neil Armstrong, Alan Shepard, Wally Schirra and Gordon Cooper mix star-power lunches with talk of zero-gravity welding and the quarantine YMCA's Sears Spanish furniture.

DEAR DAME BATMAN: I am here from Prague two year. Big crimes in Prague are treachery against State, robbing money and murder. Melbourne I think different. Big crime not murder but taking all your clothes off. Another very big crime is using profane — how you say it — sex swear words. I see men convicted by very important judge, big ceremony, for using swear words. I am frightened. I go to Town Hall and ask 'What are these swear words I cannot use? Can I have a book of them.' They say no. I ask at police station. 'Please tell me some swear words.' Big sergeant push me out door. Please, my English not good. I may use these words in mistake and police lock me up. Bad words very big crime in Melbourne.

Magda, Carlton.

Dame Batman replies: Cheer up. Your problem can be solved. I know the forbidden words. But they are difficult to list here. However, they are allowed in every public bar, the prize-fighting ring, the dogs and the football. I would suggest that you stand in a bar at about six o'clock, or even better get behind the goals at Victoria Park, and you will hear every word. Truly gifted Australians can increase a 50 percent swear content to almost 75 percent. This may amaze you, but the idea is to inject the swear word mid-syllable. 'You bloody ball.'

You see, we really adore swearing. You have picked out the case 'Boys in the Band.' I think you were surprised that it went for over a year with swear words in Sydney but would not tolerate it in Melbourne. There is a difference between the two cities. We object very strongly to the stage representing the type of life that goes on outside. After all, everybody knows the vulgar things that happen elsewhere. We think that the theatre should be morally uplifting and depict refinement. We have to think of the nice type of tourist that comes here. Then there are the young people. We don't want them to pick up words too early that they get at the football.

This moving exchange reached this journal this week and I publish it out of public interest. Yet the problem is not that complicated.

Some promise sex, others adventure. The Oxford doesn't believe it is a contraction of bottom but rather from the French, bombe, the belly of a cask or other vessel. References back to 1387. Many dictionaries come near to the MCG meaning, but only Bailey spells it out: 'A man's yard.'

What are the vice squad to do? They will be Bowdlerising on the universities, the law libraries, the schools. There will be a public burning of all Bailey, all Ash, all Oxford.

But before they do this, could one put in a plea. These words have a magnificent antiquity. They have been passed down lovingly almost in their original form over five centuries. They are far less vulgar than the newspaper equivalents like 'sexual intercourse.' Indeed, some of the words could well merit a grading from the National Trust. F---, for example, could get an 'A' — to be preserved at all cost. P---- might only warrant a 'B,' yet be classified as 'highly significant.' The important thing is to make the most scholarly decision and perhaps this makes one a B-flat, if you follow.

Let me make this case with a quote from 'The Four-Letter Words,' by A. P. Herbert. It begins:

Let us banish the use of the four-letter words

Whose meanings are never obscure.

The Angles and Saxons, those bawdy old birds

Were vulgar, obscene and impure.

But cherish the use of the wheedling phrase

Which never quite says what you mean.

You had better be known for your hypocrite ways

Than be vulgar, obscene and obscene.

It's a High Old Life for the Astronauts

SOME OF THE world's finest aviation men are meeting at the Beverly Hills Hilton, California, to listen to lectures on space. 'One of the nice things about being here,' says Neil Armstrong, 'is being able to speak to an audience who understands what you are saying.' The idea of this get-together, officially referred to as the 13th Symposium of the Society of Experimental Test Pilots, is for an exchange of thoughts on what is happening in the aerospace world; so 1600, the cream of the aerospace profession this side of the Iron Curtain, are here at a reduced 25 percent room tariff, plus $20 registration fee and extras, listening to Neil Armstrong going through film of his first journey to the moon.

Down in the coffee shop Jules, in brown overalls, moves down the bar and hoists a second wedge of coconut cake. Not that Jules is characteristic. Everyone's going around saying: Have you seen them? Meaning Neil and Mike and Scott and Walter and Rusty and Gordo and Pete and all the rest. First-name basis, naturally. One reason for the question is they are so difficult to distinguish from anyone else. Someone has forgotten, for instance, that John Glenn was the very first man to orbit. Or was it Alan Shepard? No, he only flipped over for five minutes. Alan Shepard's doing very nicely now. He owns a bank in Houston and there are some who say he is the richest astronaut of them all.

Approximately 1300 miles away at Houston and precisely a $25 ride from the town — a 100 percent price increase in two years — NASA's simulation test on Apollo-12 goes ahead in desultory fashion, while up in the VIP viewing box behind thick glass someone is explaining why the astronaut's clean-cut image is all-important; for one thing, at $350 million a shot the public want to feel secure their money's spent in the right way, though there's enough criticism now about half-starved masses and education for the ignorant and houses for the homeless, and how the space program is a waste of taxpayers' money. Would you believe that a Methodist minister demanded and got an apology for one astronaut saying, 'Son of a bitch' loud and clear on air? 'Taking the language of the gutter into space' was his actual phraseology.

For another, their private lives get no publicity, and any interviews they do give, on Fridays only, are in their offices and strictly on space. Deke Slayton, the astronaut who has never been up, starts with seven astronauts and 79 or so staff, now has 49 astronauts and 300 staff, and says, 'There are strict rules about personal appearances, yeah, we don't allow any of those mothers' meetings and schools' PA, they wouldn't do, and all we let them do are national things like the National Boys' Brigade, and when I say they gotta go to one they gotta go, they don't argue with me.'

'I am disturbed,' says this flight director in the glass VIP box, 'that people think they lead an isolated clinical life, why these boys are like super-stars, living like Hollywood used to, you know, they work hard, but they play hard, too, the wildest parties, they're nice normal guys. I'll tell you this has opened all kinds of doors which would never have been opened before, offers from companies, everyone wants to have us at parties, our status is terrific.'

Ah yes, Alan Shepard at the opening of Houston's Symphony is the most photographed man in white-and-black striped tuxedo, and all the matrons rush for his opinion of the new conductor Antonio Almeida. Almeida at the reception, standing there by Dr. Charles Jones (he's Humble Oil company), hears him say, 'He may turn out another Bernstein, who knows?' But merchant Lawrence Marcus (he's Neiman-Marcus) says 'if at 41 he hasn't got it, he hasn't got it.'

'By the way, Walt, you know, astronaut Walt Cunningham, was coming but his children have mumps. The whole concert is dedicated to the Moon Flight and the people connected with it, and over there at the after-concert reception eating tomato-in-aspic is a party of behind-the-sceners. Even they are socially terribly acceptable and desirable here in Houston, though, of course, it is pointed out, you can always tell a NASA man. He has one of those long thin ties, goes without a jacket and wears short-sleeve shirts, grey pants and, yes, crew-cuts his hair. Rather last decade if you know what they mean.

NASA at Houston, all 1600 acres of it, was built on land given by Dr. Charles Jones who is Humble. The public, the glorious taxpaying public, come to keep an eye on their investment, usually Sundays, but they are welcome any day. A huge exhibition room is devoted to space equipment and pictures and at this moment there are half-a-dozen people wandering, the receptionist has disappeared so don't ask questions, and a couple of overalled NASA workmen stagger through with space scenery.

'Is that the thing that went to the moon, Al?'

'Well, yeah, sure, you can tell by the marks.'

Actually, the thing that went to the moon is destroying itself by stages in the module that came down, and the command module is being restored at a NASA base somewhere. The next thing is to save the thing that is destroyed and refurbish it for another flight. This will save taxpayers millions. But Al is not to know.

And another thing he does not know is how very YMCA are the quarters where the astronauts spent their quarantine time. For 21 days, 23 people lived with Sears mock Spanish furniture, open-living plan with a kitchen one end and film screen the other. For recreation: television, radio, table tennis and they did get in some glossy 'Playboy' magazines. Someone's painted a wall white for the next quarantine filmshows. And as Armstrong, Collins and Aldrin walked through in suits made by Playtex, the corset people, someone sticky-taped the doors and windows, and there's a good deal of that, too.

And so, to return to the California symposium and Wally Schirra, Commander Apollo-7, Mercury and Gemini flights, who is saying, 'I tell you, you'll never get so many astros under one roof together again, specially paying their own way.' They came from Houston in their white-painted T.38s, their Candy Coated Training Aids, 1300 miles to good old LAX, no one says Los Angeles, and their arrival causes a security problem: what to do with those red envelopes, each one containing one red identification card, and all the generals and brigadiers and captains pin them to the right of their medals, and all the civilians tuck them in with their Biro pens.

It is all Schoolboys' Own. There is Fish Salmon, first to loop-the-loop in a helicopter. Tony Le Vier, first to fly the U-2. He took up the C.5 Galaxy, the world's largest aeroplane, big enough to carry a Boeing 707 minus wings. Bill Lear, who is all of Lear Inc., and makes those executive jets at $13 million a copy — Sinatra's got one — is, on October 27, going to the President at 3.30 p.m. and will stay all evening if necessary telling him how terrible air traffic control is in the U.S.

Actually, it all turns into a pretty drunky three days, everyone careening up and down all six lifts on all eight floors, glass in hand, lectures slotted in between, lunches at $6 a time with speeches; a ladies' tour and luncheon, breakfast departure from Gaslight Club Patio; a poolside reception where everyone rolls up to any astronaut in sight and says, 'It sure is swell to meet you, will you introduce me to Scott, Walt, Wally, Neil, Mike, Pete, Dick — ?' And so on. The climax is a $20 banquet, strictly non-Press. The menu is melon, beef, and chocolate surprise, one glass of French wine each and a cocktail. Charles Lindbergh is there, and Bob Hope. And everyone says how moving it all is, they'll all be back for the 14th symposium.

Astronaut Gordon Cooper revealed the only news: that, in space, five-inch blocks of steel could be welded permanently, ball-bearings made perfectly, 'Whee — zonk — into the mould it goes,' and, as calcium reduces up there in Zero G, anyone with arthritis would benefit from a quick trip. They need not, however, actually go into orbit.
