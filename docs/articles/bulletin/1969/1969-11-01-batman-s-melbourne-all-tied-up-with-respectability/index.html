All Tied Up with Respectability

5 min read.

First published 01 Nov 1969

The backlash against hippy informality has produced a black-tie boom in Melbourne with five formal invitations arriving per week and 130 dress-hire establishments now listed in the pink pages, where Mrs. Belle Benjamin reports the dress-hire business has trebled in three years, leading Keith Dunstan to propose a union of non-formal-wear wearers called the Angry Penguins with Max Harris as patron.

MUCH HAS BEEN written in recent times of the spread of hippie-mania, of the urge for uni-sex, of the passion for hair down to the shoulders, for beards, for bare feet, tattered jeans and shirts open to the waist. Our universities look like refugee camps and a tie is not to be seen. Look out for people who do not wear ties. Throughout history the tieless ones have caused most of the trouble.

But how much has been written about the backlash against all this? In a sort of psychological terror that guests might turn up in bare feet and jeans, we are seeing the biggest black tie-white tie boom since the days of Prince Albert. During September and October your correspondent received as many as five invitations a week, marked 'black tie' or 'formal.'

The worst manifestation of this is a dream of respectability for picture theatres. Almost every movie show in Melbourne now has a black-tie opening. 'Oh! What a Lovely War,' with everyone from Larry Olivier to Michael Redgrave, fought back with black tie at the Cinema Centre. But there was a subtle difference. 'Oh! What a Lovely War' was largely a film for doves and the audience did not include the leaders of the armed services. 'The Battle of Britain' was entirely a hawk affair. Every military man and ex-air-marshal on the roll was present and the invitation said, very firmly, 'black tie with medals.' So down there in the dress circle there was the pleasant clinking of DFCs and Pacific Stars.

A little over a week before, we had the opening of the Trak cinema with Jack Lemmon and Catherine Deneuve in 'The April Fools.' Trak is our latest glory in 3142. Trak is a parody on the way the nice people say Toorak — Trak, never, never Too-rak. So the $2 million new shopping centre, theatre and office building is striving for tone. It even has a coffee-restaurant called The Upper Crust, where nice people can look through plate-glass windows at nice people shopping in Toorak below. The first night at Trak, of course, was black tie.

Perhaps one can go along with black tie for first nights at the theatre or the opera. It is a compliment to the actors. But is it necessary to give the same compliment to a reel of celluloid?

Of course the real hell of these picture-theatre openings is that they are not money-fetching affairs, and they are on an impossible night like Monday. So either one has to cart the black-tie gear into town and back into town by 8.15. But it's not only theatre and movie nights that call for formal dress. Charity balls invariably are black tie, 21st birthday parties are black tie, staff dinners are black tie, and there is an astonishing trend toward holding black-tie private dinners. Anyone who wants his guests to feel they are taking part in a big deal now insists on black tie. It goes with the candles and the best silver.

If you require evidence: ten years ago there were only two or three places in Melbourne where you could hire formal gear. Now the Melbourne pink pages list over 130. Chester's Formal announces: 'You Meet a Hire Class at Chester's.' Brian Ferrari insists that his suits are being constantly updated and 'there are no out-of-date full silk.' Our most startling evidence came from Mrs. Belle Benjamin, of the Direct Supply Clothing store. She says the dress-hire business in Melbourne has trebled in the past three years. 'We get mayors, mayoresses, bank managers, big insurance people — they all come to us,' she said. 'Say a big bank in town is having a staff function and it's formal, I would bet that 75 percent of the men who sit down to dinner would be wearing a hired suit. I remember our boys would have their own dinner suits.' That made me laugh, because I had seen them all coming in here. 'Twenty years ago young people didn't wear a black tie the way they do today. Now they're all doing it. We do a big trade with Jewish people. They're very popular for bar mitzvahs.'

It costs you $10 to hire a white tie, tails, waistcoat, studs and all the bits. The charge for black-tie gear is around $7. And, of course, what the hirers and men's-wear stores are striving for is planned obsolescence.

This isn't easy. Tails haven't changed all that much since the 1830s, when they were derived from the riding coat of the 1780s. Dinner jackets have remained much the same since the turn of the century. They came from the U.S., based on the smoking-jacket.

Fashion calls for brocaded jackets, lacy shirts, with coloured dress shirts just starting to be all the rage. As for ties, they can be one inch or three and a half inches. What's more there is a swing toward patent-leather shoes.

So you see it is all part of a plot. The time is ripe to organise a union of non-formal-wear wearers. We will refuse to wear black tie at dinners or movie theatres. We will ban the white tie altogether and we will organise a public burning for the appalling morning suits that, inevitably, will be worn again during the spring racing carnival in Melbourne. There was a suggestion five years ago that the Melbourne Cup was becoming less formal, but this isn't true. It's getting worse than ever. The ideal name for our union would be the Angry Penguins. Perhaps Max Harris would agree to be patron.
