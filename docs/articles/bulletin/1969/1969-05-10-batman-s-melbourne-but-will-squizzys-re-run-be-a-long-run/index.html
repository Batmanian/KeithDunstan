But Will Squizzy's Re-Run Be a Long Run?

5 min read.

First published 10 May 1969

Nigel Buesst's $2000 documentary film on Squizzy Taylor opens at the Carlton cinema, drawing on old photographs, cartoons, and interviews to portray Theodore Joseph Leslie Taylor as a blackguard, flashy dresser, and public-relations genius who outwitted Melbourne police for ten years before dying in a 1927 gunfight with Sydney criminal John D. Cutmore.

THE BLURB for the film "The Rise and Fall of Squizzy Taylor" is quite uncomplimentary about the gentleman. It describes him as "larrikin, pickpocket, blackmailer, incendiarist, bootlegger, sneak killer, jury squarer, witness basher, stool-pigeon, housebreaker, dope pedlar, urger, two-up racketeer, race rigger, standover man, and in every role a character and wit. A devastating documentary of the life of Australia's greatest blackguard."

Your correspondent was a little disturbed about this. After all, we had carefully nursed Edward Kelly as our best-loved blackguard. So it was important to seek out Nigel Buesst, photographer, producer of the film. He said Squizzy Taylor was much more impressive than Kelly. With the aid of helicopters and modern communication the police would have fixed Kelly in a week. Squizzy Taylor, until his death in 1927, outwitted the Melbourne police for ten years.

The film opened last week at the Carlton cinema. It runs for 50 minutes. It cost $2000, and involved Buesst in an ingenious tying up of old film, old photographs, cartoons, interviews with those who still remember, and production shots of all the choicest shootings and murders. Actually, the acting in the film is done by amateurs and is appalling. But the clever Nigel Buesst gives all these sequences in the negative and the bad acting only gives the film a marvellous bizarre charm.

It is remarkable that someone hasn't disinterred Squizzy Taylor before. Nigel Buesst, now only 30, is thoroughly post-Taylor. He used to live opposite the very house in Carlton, where John D. Cutmore, the Sydney blackguard, and Theodore Joseph Leslie ('Squizzy') Taylor, Melbourne blackguard, destroyed each other in a 19-shot gun battle in 1927. In 1965 the wreckers pulled down this house, which maybe could have won an 'A' grading from the National Trust. Nigel was astonished at the crowds which wandered through, looking for bullet holes in the walls, asking to be told the whole story of Taylor with his revolver.

Nigel Buesst decided then he would make the film. The research he found far from easy. People under 30, he said, had never heard of the man. Those over 30 at the very mention of the name came over 'all peculiar,' like one old gentleman who, upon being confronted with the camera, said: 'If you turn that thing on I'll smash it and you. I never knew Taylor, and so what if I did?'

Apart from the murders, apart from Taylor's activities as a blackmailer and vicious standover man, Nigel Buesst turned up some good material on Squizzy the Character. He was 5ft. 2in., wore diamond rings, and was a very flashy dresser.

He really became a public legend when he absconded from bail, and for a year he went into smoke. But all the time he made sure of his public relations, even writing letters to the newspapers. He got around town in various disguises, turning up at the Caulfield races in flapper dress. Another disguise was a schoolboy cap and blazer, reportedly Scotch College. Squizzy chose the more genteel schools. He gave himself up on September 21, 1922, but not before first advising all the newspapers of his action and making sure that the photographers would be present. Superbly tailored and wearing a bowler hat, he drove to the Russell Street police station in a limousine complete with chauffeur.

There were other charming incidents. There was the time the horse-racing bookmaker threw him down the stairs at the Victoria Club. Next morning the bookmaker awoke to find his garden all torn up. There was the time Lady Stanley, wife of the former Governor, decided, altruistically, that she would reform him, and she arranged a meeting at the Menzies Hotel. Squizzy presented her with an expensive silver casket, but it was inscribed 'To one who understands from one misunderstood.'

He used to keep a cutting book with accounts of his exploits, and he used to decorate it with terribly bad rhymes, such as: 'While you live, live in clover, For when you're dead, you're dead all over.'

The best interview in the film is with Sir Eugene Gorman, who said of Taylor and his associates: 'I found nothing to admire in them and I find nothing to admire in them now.' But he made one exception on Angus Murray. He was a charming, gentle, sensitive man who had effaced a life of crime. Murray was hanged for the murder of bank manager Thomas Berryman. Sir Eugene defended Murray, who, he said, was famed for never carrying a loaded gun. It was Richard Buckley, who later escaped hanging, who did the shooting. Sir Eugene, in the best line in the film, said: 'Before his death he wrote to me thanking me for what I had tried to do. He said: "There is nothing I can do for you beyond promising that I will not forget you in that other country in which even the most hardened of us are compelled to believe."'

The film also shows early shots of Squizzy Taylor's second wife, Ida Pender. He met her when she was 16, dancing at the Palais de Danse. She had red-gold hair, and veteran journalists like Norman McCance claim she was beautiful. She was incredibly loyal to Taylor, always refusing to believe anything bad of him. At the funeral she put a heart made of white flowers on the coffin.

Nigel Buesst tried very hard to find Ida Pender. Where did she go? Was she still alive? By his estimate, she would now be 65. Since then, many conflicting reports have come in. One said she twice married again and was still alive. Others said she died about four years ago. All agreed that she had a lovely red-haired daughter, who had no knowledge of her mother's former associations.

The film makes no hero of Squizzy, but it does try to put over the view that Squizzy was a character, and Melbourne took a childish delight in its gangsters and their wickedness. The film 'Two Thousand Weeks' had a sad start in our town. The Squizzy film, on a much smaller budget and in a much smaller theatre, could do better.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: But Will Squizzy's Re-Run Be a Long Run?, The Bulletin, 10 May 1969. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
