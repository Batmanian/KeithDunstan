This Ozone by Any Other Name Would Smell More Sweet

4 min read.

First published 07 Jun 1969

Mayor John Staughton's $30,000 promotion campaign to revive St. Kilda's image as a cosmopolitan resort, complete with 4000 colour brochures comparing it favourably to the French Riviera, prompts Keith Dunstan to point out the suburb's unidentifiable patron saint and suggest it needs a new devastating name to rival Surfers Paradise.

BACK IN THE 1880s St. Kilda was the elite watering place for the colony. One could purchase a tenpenny return railway ticket to St. Kilda, which included a bathe, bathing dress, and towels at Captain Kenney's Bathing Ship for Gentlemen. There was also scrupulously private swimming for ladies at Captain Kenney's Ladies' Baths under the personal superintendence of Mrs. Kenney and the Misses Beatrice and Maude Kenney.

St. Kilda had all the glories of Blackpool and even finer places, but after World War I it lost prestige. One was told to smell the ozone, but the ozone was really the by-product of the effluent society. It became a suburb for cheap boarding and rooming houses. In the first six months of 1966 more than 400 prostitutes and men living off their earnings were arrested in St. Kilda. On November 28, last year, 35 men were convicted in St. Kilda court on gutter-crawling charges, that is, of having loitered in wait for a prostitute.

The most grievous tragedy came in 1965. The St. Kilda football team decided there was no future for a team in this aged suburb so it departed to young, populous, virile Moorabbin.

In short, St. Kilda's image was shocking. Last year the mayor, Mr. John Staughton, flew to Surfers Paradise to see an old ace at the public relations business, Alderman Bruce Small, mayor of the Gold Coast, and to look over his cutting book.

Then, a week or so ago, the mayor slipped into gear. He announced a $30,000 promotion campaign for St. Kilda. A PR firm is in full command, and he told the world: 'If only 24 visitors can be attracted to our city each day, it is the economic equivalent to acquiring a new manufacturing industry with an annual payroll of $100,000.'

Thereupon he handed over to the brochure of 4000 colour pamphlets. The brochure claims St. Kilda is the Cote d'Azur of the south. Hearken to this prose: 'St. Kilda is a city of infinite variety. Just two miles south of downtown Melbourne on the shores of Port Phillip Bay, it responds to the invigorating sea airs with a joie de vivre to which one easily succumbs. Sun-worshippers frolic in the shallows or cook themselves nut brown on the long, wide beaches.'

'You can dine bistro-style on onion soup and escargots while rubbing shoulders with the elite of Melbourne's art world, go native in a South Seas atmosphere ... or live it up in the Greek manner to the music of the bazouki. When the sun goes down — and what a sunset — you can wine, dine, and dance to the melodies of an Italian orchestra, take in the floor show of a nightclub, or indulge fancies at a bare-topped cabaret.'

The brochure says that St. Kilda is representative of the Australia of today. 'The dynamic child of the 20th century ... young and enthusiastic enough for life to gather people and customs from the farthest corners of the earth.'

But the name, one feels, is a problem. The Southport area craftily sold itself on a name by picking out Surfers Paradise and Gold Coast. But St. Kilda can't even produce its saint. The supreme authority on this subject, 'The Book of Saints,' compiled by the Benedictine monks, says of St. Kilda: 'This saint gives its name to an island off the Scottish coast, which has a well known as St. Kilder's or St. Kilda's well. But the saint has not been identified.' Either the council should find a more reliable saint or give the place a new devastating name. One fears it would have to be devastating to outdo Surfers. Baretop Beach? Permissive City? It is not something that could be decided in a minute.

The mayor says that St. Kilda is to have a $750,000 seaquarium, it is to be the headquarters for a hovercraft service for Port Phillip, and he claims more exciting projects will be announced in months to come.

Your correspondent commends the city for its enterprise, but before another brochure is produced could one point to the magnificent booklet that was produced by Frederick Wimpole, proprietor of St. Kilda's George Hotel, in 1881. His guide was placed in the hands of every visitor and found, too, on the tables of the great hotels of the European capitals.

He wrote: 'One point of consequence to visitors of not very robust physique is the fact that the hotel is not directly facing the sea, and the cold south winds cannot prejudicially affect the air, while the hot winds blowing from the north are greatly modified by passing over the extensive lake, used for yachting purposes, known as Albert Park Lagoon. The soil of the district being sandy and porous, no malaria can arise.'

Source: Batman's Melbourne: This Ozone by Any Other Name Would Smell More Sweet, The Bulletin, 7 June 1969. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
