A City That Loves to Be Disdained

5 min read.

First published 04 Oct 1969

British architectural critic J. M. Richards, editor of the 'Architectural Review' and guest of Robin Boyd and Neil Clerehan, tours Melbourne's new buildings and dispatches them in turn — the AMP tower 'dung-brown,' the Masonic Centre 'too dressed up,' the Arts Centre 'too many gimmicks,' the National Mutual building 'too fancy,' the ICI building 'fine but could be anywhere' — to the city's deep delight.

OUR SENSE OF inferiority is so deep there is a great unsatisfied thirst for the unkind comment about our town. I remember the day Nat 'King' Cole arrived. He had just stepped out of his aircraft at Essendon and the reporters asked not about Los Angeles and the state of Hollywood but 'What do you think of Melbourne?' Nat 'King' Cole paused for a time and said: 'Well, you sure have lovely rooftops.'

Actually there is no point in saying nice things. Visitors who remark: 'I think Melbourne girls are the world's most beautiful' never get a run. We love to be kicked and abused, and every glowing line of abuse that appears in the London newspapers is always given consolation inches. When Charles Osborne wrote in the 'Observer' that 'Melbourne is a grim pit of boredom.' We liked that. Sam White said in the 'Evening Standard': 'Melbourne is an outrageously snobbish city.' Couldn't have been better. J. B. Priestley found Melbourne a big, rich city where absolutely nothing ever happened. We admired Terence Stamp tremendously after he announced: 'Really, Melbourne is a bit of a giggle.' Or did we prefer Max Bygraves for his line: 'What a dreary town. You couldn't even get a parachute to open after 10 p.m.'

So you can imagine how eager we were to greet Mr. J. M. Richards. Every newspaper sought him out. Mr. Richards is editor of the august 'Architectural Review' in London. He is the writer on architecture for 'The Times.' His books are treated at Melbourne University with almost biblical solemnity.

He arrived in Melbourne on a Sunday, his lecture tour financed by Australian Consolidated Industries. First we admired him for what he said about Sydney and Canberra.

'Sydney,' said he, 'is a mess, you can't find the buildings. The AMP building is a horror — an ugly building that is too prominent.' He liked the Australia Square and the Water Board buildings, but the Royal Exchange building he dismissed with contempt. He didn't see one good building in Canberra, and he was particularly eloquent about the $8 million National Library: 'It looks like a phony classical temple without all the classical temple edgings. It's a disaster as a piece of architecture.'

In Melbourne, Mr. Richards was in the care of the distinguished architects Robin Boyd and Neil Clerehan. Robin Boyd said that in London Mr. Richards had asked him what sort of tropical rig he should wear in Melbourne. During the length of his stay, this city, which is listed in geography books as having a temperate climate, produced ideal training conditions for visits to Mawson Island. One day Mr. Richards was so cold he went back to bed after breakfast.

But that afternoon Neil Clerehan arranged a grand tour by car so that Mr. Richards could criticise as we proceeded. The Treasury buildings and Parliament House elucidated no comment, but we paused in front of the ICI building, first of Melbourne's glass houses. Neil Clerehan recently wrote that this was the 'apotheosis of the international style.' Mr. Richards peered at the ICI. 'A fine building, but it could be anywhere.'

We went to Victoria Parade and stopped by one of our newest glories, the $4 million Masonic Centre with all its columns and lashings of marble. Mr. Richards was curt and unhesitating: 'Too dressed up. Someone here is trying too hard.'

We took him around the very nicest parts of East Melbourne, even showing him the glories of the city residence walls and exquisite black cast-iron. 'Quite charming,' said Mr. Richards.

We took him to the Shrine, once described as that remarkable compromise between the Parthenon and the Cheops Pyramid. Mr. Richards thought it was aging quite nicely, but it was not a building to his taste.

Also not to his taste was the Melbourne Arts Centre. 'The architect tried too hard and put too many things in it. It is too full of gimmicks and materials — there's not enough feeling inside of repose. It's too busy and it's impossible for one to be relaxed.' He liked the bluestone, he liked the squares and he disliked the fluted columns in the banquet hall. But surely he liked the marvellous butcher's shop front window with the water running down the glass? 'A gimmick,' said he.

Seeing that he commented on the Sydney AMP building — we had to show him ours. We explained that this was the creation of the U.S. firm Skidmore, Owings and Merrill. 'San Francisco office?' he asked. Mr. Richards peered at the huge dung-brown tower with its curious lesser grandstand building on the corner. 'It's all right. I can't understand the reason for the eaves and the slanting-type windows on the lower building. The finish is good. At least it doesn't have the vulgarity of the building in Sydney.'

We showed him our marvellous National Mutual Building with its courtyard out front. 'Too fancy for my taste. The gold bands make it look cheap.'

We showed him the new Legal and General building with its awning like a cap. He liked it. We took him to admire that marvellous Melburnian skyline with the CRA, the Princes Gate, Shell, AMP, St. Paul's, Uncle Tom Cobley and all, and he said: 'Very confused. No building has any relation to the one next to it. That skyline could be anywhere in the world.'

A little nervous that he might get over-enthusiastic and like something, we asked: 'Are there any buildings that you like or particularly approve of?' He quite liked the Royal Insurance building, better known as the Black Stump, but he thought the city may become over-gloomy if there were too many similar buildings. He liked 60 Market Street, a severely plain, unadorned office tower, but otherwise his attitude was everything we could have desired.

You may well ask about the architecture of Mr. Richards himself. Period: Post-World War I, neo-Georgian. Style: Good taste. Plain, dark blue suit with dark blue tie. No special Euro treatment. Only fault, which really bars him from a medal — slip-on brown shoes jar with the upper-floor dark blue suit treatment. Finish: Medium-length sideburns, beautifully maintained.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: A City That Loves to Be Disdained, The Bulletin, 4 October 1969. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
