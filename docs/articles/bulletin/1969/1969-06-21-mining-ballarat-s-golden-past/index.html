Mining Ballarat's Golden Past

6 min read.

First published 21 Jun 1969

Ballarat's Sovereign Hill committee, led by architect Ewen Jones and Ballarat Historical Park Association president John Trevenen, is re-creating an 1850s gold-rush village on the site of the old North Normanby mine, where tourists will pan for gold, enter working shafts by candlelight, and watch a ten-head battery crushing rock, with Bentley's Hotel from the Eureka uprising destined to be the centrepiece.

WHEN ONE comes to select favourite vintage cities, Ballarat is still the jewel of them all. The trams are under sentence of death, but, mercifully, nobody knows when the execution will take place. Ballarat trams are good trams, I understand they were designed by Walt Disney, some time early in the century, and their quaintness rating is far superior to Melbourne trams.

Sturt Street with its garden centre is still as noble as ever. I walked up and down paying proper tribute to all the statues, nodded to Peter Lalor, shrank back a little from the martial splendour of King George V, bowed to the towering Queen Victoria and the bird bath appropriately filled with water by courtesy of the Women's Christian Temperance Union.

Burke and Wills are there, although they never passed through Ballarat. Thomas More looks down upon all, and, of course, there is the inevitable, dewy-eyed, almost cuddly statue of a junior Robbie Burns.

Yes, Ballarat is beautiful. Yet, there have been some shocks. In the old days, as one drove under the railway line, the first sight of the city used to be the old orphanage with its glorious cast-iron balconies and immaculately kept garden. It has been pulled down and replaced by a group of buildings distinguished only by their ugliness. Remember, too, the historic bluestone Commonwealth Bank at the corner of Sturt and Lydiard Streets. The bank, in a monstrous piece of vandalism, has been pulled down and replaced with an object that looks like a butter box.

Ballarat also has been caught in the national game of pulling down awnings, but, thanks to the National Trust, six have been retained, including that of the George Hotel, which probably has the most delectable, photographable three-tiered balcony in Australia.

In all other respects Ballarat is moving very swiftly and efficiently in the history race. Ted Millett, who used to be assistant director of the Department of Supply, has opened Montrose Cottage, a bluestone building which dates from 1856. He and his wife have restored it exactly as a miner's cottage was in the 1860s. Mr. Millett has such a flair for exact detail that he used an 1857 recipe for the lining of the walls. It began: 'take two penn'orth of lime-green cow manure and mix with boiling water.' They actually took 80 cents' worth of lime-green cow manure and mixed it with boiling water. It worked out very well indeed.

But the really fascinating exercise is the effort that is being made to preserve the memory of Ballarat, the Golden City. From 1853, 643 tons of gold came out of the Ballarat area, but barely a morsel of gold worth looking at has been won since 1918, and until recently the only evidence for the tourist that this was the Golden City was a papier-mache gold nugget. Even mullock heaps were becoming so scarce they were getting the blessing of the National Trust.

But now an alert committee has taken over Sovereign Hill, which does sport a genuine mullock heap, and the object is to re-create up there the whole gold-mining era. I went there with the president of the Ballarat Historical Park Association, John Trevenen, and the architect Ewen Jones. Already, for the tourists, there is a 68ft. poppet head, constructed the authentic way. It is a splendid affair, right over the shaft of the old North Normanby mine.

All around the park there will be examples of old-time mining. They will see a horse-powered whim shaft — an affair rather like a ship's capstan with rope coiled around a drum with the horse walking in a circle. They will see windlasses, puddling machines, and a ten-head battery crushing rock with all the appropriate, marvellous, historical, ear-splitting roar.

Water from a 3in. main will be turned on on special occasions, and here there will be a fine old goldfields scene just like something out of an S. T. Gill watercolour, hundreds of citizens all along the water's edge panning for their fortunes. Tourists will be able to buy miners' rights in the old format. It would be most interesting if there were a tourist uprising in 1970.

This is a stunt that is expected to be a winner. Upon buying his miner's right, the tourist will peg out his claim and he will get a dish of earth which has been salted with a little gold dust. So, right there, wearing gumboots, feet in mud, dirt on his pink hands, he will be able to experience the age-old thrill of discovering gold.

Right now Ewen Jones is working on the construction of the gold-mining village, a long winding street to be just the way it was in the days of Eureka. It will be even paved with boards in the old authentic style to stop the Cobb & Co. coaches from being bogged in the mud.

There will be, among other establishments, a saddler, a stable, a blacksmith, an apothecary, a baker, a paperhanger, a Chinese joss house, a general store, and a police station.

The key piece will be Bentley's Eureka Hotel, and the hope is that one of the breweries will take this over and run it as a licensed hotel. Ken McKenzie, the historian on the committee, feels the tourists will be curious about Bentley's, which contributed to the uprising. Just before Eureka, the licensee, Bentley, hit a miner named Scobie over the head with a shovel and killed him. Bentley was a former convict and he was able to handle not only the corrupt police but also the corrupt magistrate, and he got off. The miners were so furious they burned the hotel.

Also in the street there will be the Victoria Theatre with the United States Hotel alongside. It was at the Victoria Theatre that the lovely Lola Montez did her daring spider dance.

Already they have 20 horse-drawn vehicles, including a horse-drawn ambulance, a fire engine, a brougham, a four-in-hand, a phaeton, and a Cobb & Co. coach. But the most precious relic is the original Eureka flag, the old Southern Cross. Collectors over the years have offered thousands for it. The Ballarat librarian, Mr. Austin McCallum, has it locked in his safe.

The big hope is that the Park will be at least partly open in time to capture the Christmas trade this year.
