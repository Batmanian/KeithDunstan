Getting to Know the World's Really Rich

6 min read.

First published 27 Dec 1969

The official opening of the AMP Society's new 26-storey William Street tower, built over the demolished St. James Building by Skidmore Owings and Merrill with local architects Bates Smart and McCutcheon, reveals 26,400 square feet of travertine marble, a staff basement with five billiard tables, a gymnasium, four table tennis tables and a squash court, and a $50,000 sculpture by Clement Meadmore that has already been defaced by graffiti enthusiasts treating it like a urinal.

THE STORY STARTS long ago when Mr. Whelan moved into the old St. James Building opposite the Menzies in William Street. The St. James Building was perhaps the best office building of the land boom. It housed all the right people, such as Sir William Clarke, R. G. Casey sen., the Creswicks and so on. In more recent years it was not quite so prestigious. It housed solicitors, importers, and down below there were vast subterranean cellars and six wholesale liquor firms. On a hot day it was utterly splendid making your way from firm to firm. It was as good as going to the Royal Show and pretending you wanted to buy livestock. The Church of England sold this site to the AMP Society for $3 million, the biggest land deal in Melbourne's history.

AMP then promised us a $20-million, 26-storey building in Melbourne and second only to Sydney's Australia Square as the greatest chunk of office space in the country. This Sydney project, we should note, was the one which AMP paid for but wasn't going to let Sydney think it did anything better for the junior colony of the upstart.

The project received very little enthusiasm from local architects. The chosen architects were Skidmore, Owings and Merrill, of New York, Chicago and San Francisco. Bates, Smart and McCutcheon, of Melbourne, were left to do the drawings, plans and supervise construction. As they went into action, our worst fears were realised. A tower began to rise covered with a dung-coloured Mudgee conglomerate stone. As one architect commented: 'What a good choice — Yarra Mudgee.' But, actually, the building was not bad. There was a lower, lesser building, L-shaped and called the St. James, and this was very curious. All floors had balconies and slanted on an angle toward William Street. The St. James looked as if it were trying desperately to look like a grandstand at the MCG.

Well, the AMP building had its official opening last week. Your correspondent has to admit he had been prejudiced. Maybe it was too much Colonel Sanders' Finger Lickin' chicken, Red Barn hamburgers and other American projects. But the AMP tower is one of the best things that has been done in Melbourne. Furthermore, that MCG grandstand bit is the interesting part. There is an arcade of shops, like medieval cloisters, and massive in Yarra Mudgee, which is tremendous.

Unquestionably, it is the insurance men who are the real rich of this world. We thought the Shell oil men were fairly rich the way they finished their building, but it was nothing to this. We are told that there are 26,400 square feet of travertine marble. I'm not clever enough to put that in simple terms, but it looks like acres and acres of the stuff. As you enter the foyer the open space is vast, cathedral-like, ceiling 25 feet up, and travertine everywhere. There were two sparrows flying around inside — they'd be trapped for the rest of their lives. Carpet, oh yes, 10 acres of it, in strips 36 miles long.

But the extraordinary part is the staff amenities. Would you care to start selling life insurance? This is what you get: The basement is all devoted to the staff. Go down in the lift, and step out. The walls are still travertine, the carpet is wall to wall and air conditioned. On one side behind plateglass is the carpeted billiard room with five billiard tables. Down the corridor is the gymnasium, the table tennis room with four tables and the squash court. There is a staff shop, not crude and cluttered like Myer, more of the charm of Brooks Brothers, or I. Magnin. Here they sell everything — clocks, watches, pens, socks, blankets, sweets, cigarettes, Christmas gifts, and, as one buys, the piped music comes through.

The staff cafeteria is huge. It is panelled in something that looks like black bean, and there is carpet throughout. It is superior to any reception room in Melbourne and on a par with the Mayfair Room at the Southern Cross. A grill costs 35 cents and a salad 29 cents. Then as you look out of the dining-room through the plateglass to the lifts you see a huge tapestry rug with a wave and surfboard-rider motif.

And this is what is very interesting about the AMP Society. They have given us some sculpture. The CRA people made big noises about the sculpture they would provide in Collins Street and then, at the last moment, after contacting an Italian sculptor, apparently became terrified and did nothing. The National Mutual people promised a sculpture for their square, but changed their minds and instead provided a hideously designed garden.

But the AMP had the courage to engage Clement Meadmore, now in the United States, and pay him $50,000 for a large-scale sculpture to go in the square between the tower and MCG grandstand. Your correspondent is very old-fashioned. He is devoted to mid-Victorian, big-breasted maidens, circa 17 stone, holding up big dishes with lots of splashing water. Clement Meadmore's sculpture is composed of sheet steel in a cross between a J and a question mark. It looks exactly like a shaft for an air-conditioning system.

It was allowed to rust for some months before it was brought to the AMP square and the theory is that, given time, it will rust to the colour of an old walnut. Eric Westbrook, director of the National Gallery, has been enthusiastic. He says not to look for a meaning. He claims it is a great piece of sculpture and it should be considered in relation to the building. You should learn to appreciate the superb purity of the design, and if you don't like it the first time go back and look at it again at various times of the day and in different conditions of light.

Well, it's true the Meadmore sculpture is a fascinating contrast to the prodigious straight lines of the tower, and you do get interesting shadows, fascinating curves and goodly shapes at different times of day. Yet could one suggest that rusty metal gives a cold response and not the warm heartbeats inspired by 17-stone mid-Victorian maidens?

Also in the foyer of the building there is a sculpture in polished stainless-steel by 24-year-old Michael Young, of Melbourne. It looks like a maze placed on its side, and in the marble purity of the hall by the elevators there is a perfect balance and one can understand. It looks very good indeed.

But what about the Meadmore? Eric Westbrook says we should get the same impact from this as from anything by Rodin. But the raving nitwit vandals of Melbourne already have taken over. Scratched deep into the rusting metal are the names Macka, Teena and Barrie, Linda and Tony, some with great hearts all around them. The graffiti enthusiast has to treat every object like a urinal.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Getting to Know the World's Really Rich, The Bulletin, 27 December 1969. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
