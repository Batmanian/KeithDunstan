Could Enzymes Lift That Veil off Dull Elections?

4 min read.

First published 09 Aug 1969

The proliferation of mystery ingredients in household products — Puradent, Hexachlorophene, Gardol, Enzolve, Politogene — leads Keith Dunstan to propose that the Labor, Country, and Liberal parties adopt the same scientific marketing language for the coming election, with Politogene, deep-acting enzymes, and OK-100-15 as their respective selling points.

YOU MUST HAVE noticed that mystery ingredients are on the increase. As a devoted student of mystery ingredients I was always fascinated by the toothpaste which contained Irium. It is a sadness, which remains with me, that I never discovered the exact nature of Irium. The toothpaste which contained Irium was bound to leave teeth longer in one's head than other toothpastes.

Sadly, Irium doesn't seem to be around any more. There is a new mystery ingredient. We now have 'the only toothpaste with Puradent — the unique double-action ingredient.' I have refused to use Puradent. I believe in the sincerity and nobility of the men who discovered Irium. They claimed it had all the cleansing actions necessary.

The leading rival for the toothpaste with Puradent is the one which contains Hexachlorophene, and there is no question that as one goes to the office there is real satisfaction in knowing that one's teeth reek with Hexachlorophene.

But the most advanced trend is that housewives, who have observed, personally, man's advance to the moon, are interested in receiving chemical information. For example, Colgates have been giving us a toothpaste with Gardol, and this, we are told on the packet, is the trade name for sodium n'lauroyl sarcosinate. We regular n'lauroyl sarcosinate users at the Four Square store appreciate this little courtesy.

The Kelloggs people were perhaps the first to realise the value of the scientific approach. Very early they told us that every two ounces of Corn Flakes contained .55mg. of thiamine, .8mg. of riboflavin and 5.5mg. of niacin. And I must admit that in the early days I was disturbed. I would say to myself as my son went off to school: 'There he goes, riddled with thiamine, high on riboflavin, hooked on niacin.' But now I have such faith in the manufacturer I believe it is all for his own good.

Oh, I could go on and on about mystery ingredients. We have Drive, the hungry detergent with Enzolve. We have Bon Ami with Bluolene and Ajax with Bromine bleach. There is VO5 now with Mirial, the hairspray discovery, Vim with Chlorogen and Tact with Trichlorocarbanilide, the anti-bacteria deodorant. Moretin Plus has the new Synergised Pyrethrum formula and RIT spray-on starch contains Glide 9. Cold Power with Bacteriostat provides a whole new dimension in cleanliness.

Then we have 'controlled suds' and 'regulated suds' and we have Spree, 'the ultra-blue detergent with green power crystals.' When you have ultra-blue with green power, unquestionably your wash, at last, is getting a racist hurry-up.

Yet, perhaps the most intriguing of all are the detergents which contain 'deep-acting enzymes.' These days everybody has got to have enzymes, 'which soak out stains and stubborn dirt.' 'Specially formulated,' says the packet, 'to dissolve protein stains such as blood, egg, chocolate, gravy, milk, nappy stains, perspiration and stubborn dirt around collars and cuffs. The enzymes work with a natural biological action.'

I'm a little worried about our wash, because I thought enzymes were the chemicals in the stomach which helped to dissolve one's dinner. It would help if packets carried our dictionary definition of enzyme: an organic secretion which hastens the transmutation of food and other substances.

And have you noticed how everything has to be New! New! New! New! holler those eloquent voices on the radio. All the detergents are New, such as New Ajax and New Vim. We have New Rinso, New Pink Camay, New Solvol and the most terrible of all, New Lifebuoy. Now why would anyone ever want to change Lifebuoy? With its superb sunset-pink colour and overpowering masculine odour, here was a soap that was unique.

There is a possibility that there will be a swing the other way. Could one predict that in 1970, or later, the advertisers will be shouting Old! Old! Old! Use Old Ajax, use Old Lifebuoy. Use the tried-and-trusted mystery ingredients as created by the great vintage scientists of the thirties.

But I wonder if one isn't being naive. If so much money is being spent on this type of advertising, then it must be correct. Should it not be applied in other fields? Mr. Whitlam has announced that the ALP will need $100,000 for its coming election campaign.

How about this for a slogan: New! New! New! The New Australian Labor Party with Politogene, the scientific energised formula which welds together the industrial and rural forces of the nation. Or how about 'Get With the New Country Party with deep-acting enzymes which can dissolve 500 million bushels of wheat.' I will leave it to you to suggest something for the Liberal Party. Could one suggest the Liberal Party with OK-100-15, washes away indecisions.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Could Enzymes Lift That Veil off Dull Elections?, The Bulletin, 9 August 1969. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
