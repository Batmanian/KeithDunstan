My Wordy Reunion with the Instant Opinion Machine

5 min read.

First published 19 Jul 1969

Malcolm Muggeridge visits Melbourne to commune with Barry Jones and Hector Crawford, dispensing opinions at his customary rate — Australia is doomed, California and Scandinavia are both like hell, Parliament will be killed by television — while the vegetarian ascetic finds unexpected rapport with fellow non-drinker Barry Jones, though neither can warm to Marshall McLuhan.

YEARS AGO in Melbourne we had a team of six ladies and gentlemen. Every newspaper chief-of-staff had their names in his right-hand drawer, with their telephone numbers for all occasions, daytime, night-time. There was an Anglican dean, a Presbyterian, a gentleman from a taxpayers' association, a housewives representative, a female social giant and Percy Cerutty. They could be relied on for instant publishable opinions about anything.

Yet never before have we had an instant-opinion machine like Malcolm Muggeridge. We have had him in Melbourne communing with Barry Jones and Hector Crawford. Could one say that Melbourne would love him here for ever? The instant opinions have flowed unceasingly: 'The two places in the world most like hell are California and Scandinavia. Both allow themselves complete sexual licence. Both abound in lunatics. We are obsessed with erotica. Men do not value human life. If you show Parliament on television you will really kill it, and that wouldn't be a bad thing, would it?'

'I think that Australia is absolutely doomed. The notion that 12 million people in this vast rich area can go on wantonly prospering and expect the world not to take note and take reprisals is just fatuous.' The camera will be killed by television. The TV programs about the Queen and her family have exposed them as a nice family. 'The Queen is a very pleasant middle-to-upper-class lady, with a talkative retired navy-type for a husband, and a wayward sister.'

Your correspondent met Malcolm Muggeridge at Crawford Productions. He hasn't added a wrinkle since his last visit, and the awkward thought came that perhaps there was something in the vegetarian pure life. His skin had the same colour as the old Johnson's baby-powder advertisements. He may develop into another Shaw, eating diminishing quantities of vegetables and expressing ever larger quantities of opinion well into his nineties.

He told us about his asceticism. 'I have always had a propensity this way. I found this side of the Hindus, when I was in India, very attractive. Self-indulgence is a horrible thing. Non-drinkers, of course, are very intolerant of non-drinkers. I found they would get particularly annoyed when you would accept only very small quantities. It was better to refuse it altogether. It's not so difficult in America as it is in Britain. In America, if you tell them you are doing something for your health, well, they have such a worship of the human body they understand immediately. It is awful being sober at a party, the talk all becomes so superficially jovial. I find not going to parties a most interesting game.'

It was beautiful, and even surprising, the rapport between Muggeridge and Barry Jones. The two non-drinking, non-smoking ascetics got along very well. Someone asked what he thought of Marshall McLuhan. 'Yes,' said Muggeridge, 'I did an interview with him on television once.' When was that, Barry? He replied, '1967,' and it came back straight away — the precision of the Jones electronic brain.

'Isn't he marvellous,' said Muggeridge. 'I wish I could carry him round all the time. He has all the information on everything.' But McLuhan wasn't at all worthy of such admiration. 'There was no intellectual substance between us. I found it impossible to make any rapport: an expert on communication — who can't communicate. I find it difficult, too, to read his writings they are so badly written.' 'Barry reads and understands him,' said Hector Crawford. 'A bit,' said Barry, but one had the impression he was quite ready to answer all questions on the life cycle of McLuhan.

For the first time Malcolm Muggeridge was puzzled. 'I can't understand how such a dreary man ever became so much the vogue. But I get the impression it's almost over. The two idols of the American campus, philosopher McLuhan and Andy Warhol, are sinking.'

And as for Prince Charles: 'The crowning was an utterly insignificant and unimportant event, a solace to the English who like to have these ceremonies.' And on heart transplants and the famous TV confrontation with Dr. Christiaan Barnard: 'In some ways it was the most interesting and distasteful program I have ever been on. At that time I was in a minority, but now events have moved my way and there are more people who know what I meant that day.'

He told how cities all over the world looked the same, how people behave the same. How students took their lead from the United States, wore the same ridiculous clothes and even mouthed the same slogans. 'In Britain,' he said, 'we still keep the Tower of London and the Beefeaters, but this is only for the American tourists.'

Hector Crawford was beginning to make noises of impatience. But one remembered that Barry Jones bought General MacArthur's bed at the Menzies' auction. Was he sleeping in it? 'Yes, I am. And very comfortably, too. No bad dreams.' Clearly he was sleeping like a Johnson's baby. Barry Jones beamed.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: My Wordy Reunion with the Instant Opinion Machine, The Bulletin, 19 July 1969. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
