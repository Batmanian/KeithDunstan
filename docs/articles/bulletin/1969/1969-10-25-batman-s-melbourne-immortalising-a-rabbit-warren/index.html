Immortalising a Rabbit Warren

5 min read.

First published 25 Oct 1969

The National Trust's acquisition of Barwon Park, the 42-room bluestone mansion near Winchelsea built by squatter Thomas Austin who released 24 rabbits from the clipper Lightning in 1859, leads Keith Dunstan through Eric Rolls's account of how the English rabbit adapted magnificently to antipodean conditions, the Duke of Edinburgh's record bag of 86 rabbits in a morning, and a proposal for a bronze memorial to those first two bunnies.

JUST LATELY the National Trust has pulled off one or two major triumphs. Their book, 'Historic Homesteads of Australia,' at $14.50, the latest coffee table tome, has a foreword by the Prime Minister. And if that wasn't a top prestige move it had a launching in Canberra by Sir Paul Hasluck, plus minor launchings in lesser capitals by State governors.

But the National Trust recently had another triumph which went almost unnoticed and unrecorded. The late Sidney Batson left his property, 'Barwon Park,' near Winchelsea, to the Trust. There is only one proviso — 'Barwon Park' will remain the property of his sisters, Lillian and Mabel, during their lifetime. The Batson family has held 'Barwon Park' since 1910, and it now has 319 acres plus a marvellous 42-room bluestone pile called 'The Mansion.'

You haven't heard of 'Barwon Park?' Good grief, they don't teach anything important at schools these times.

Well, Thomas Austin, the squire of 'Barwon Park,' was one of our first great rich graziers and has received much of the credit for launching the rabbit in Australia.

He was so rich that in 1869 he built this whopping bluestone mansion. It is panelled in solid cedar. It has a huge classic staircase surmounted by corinthian columns. It has stained-glass windows, great cedar doors two inches thick and a living-room said to be a replica of a living-room in Buckingham Palace. There are lashings of cast-iron and in one piece there are woven the family crest, motto and the initials of Thomas and Elizabeth Austin. The cedar came out from England in a specially chartered ship.

Thomas Austin was a pioneer in many ways. He believed one of the sad features of colonial life was the paucity of decent creatures to shoot at. In 1859 he imported aboard the fast clipper Lightning 72 partridges, five hares and 24 wild rabbits.

After Mr. Austin had held his first partridge battue, the newspaper 'Bell's Life in Victoria' commended him for the wonderful thing he had done. No country in the world was so sparsely stocked with game, said Bell. The kangaroo was a useless animal, no good for eating and not much use at the hunt. Mr. Austin was raising deer and every kind of game. Bell continued to make flattering comments. The huntsmen, after all, were the backbone of the country. The Duke of Wellington had proved this at Waterloo.

It was marvellous how the English rabbit adapted itself to rugged antipodean conditions. Eric C. Rolls, in his recently published book, 'They All Ran Wild,' writes that the 'Geelong Chronicle' of May 2, 1862, reported that the success of the partridges was amazing, his pheasants were laying and his rabbits numbered in thousands. 'Unfortunately,' he said, 'they prove highly destructive to the young trees planted in the various plantations intended for the pheasants and partridges.'

Rolls also gives some figures published in 1867, reporting 'last year's shooting on "Barwon Park": Rabbits, 14,253; hawks, 448; eaglehawks, 23; native cats.'

Prince Alfred, Duke of Edinburgh, had three visits to 'Barwon Park.' The young prince adored shooting things. He personally shot 42 possums one night in Adelaide, and when told of the rabbits of 'Barwon Park' he could hardly wait to get there.

The 'Illustrated Australian News' of December 27, 1867, cooed, 'I hear wonderful stories of the Prince's shooting powers among Mr. Austin's preserves.' A dozen guns went out on the property and in three and a half hours they shot 805 rabbits. The Prince, it seems, had his own gun and two loaders. The rabbits were driven up against a wire fence and in one place where they were cornered he dropped 68 in ten minutes. While he was doing this his guns became so hot that they blistered the hands of the loader and he could only hold them by the stocks. All this pleased the Duke so much he decided to stay the night, interrupt the schedule of his royal tour through the Western District, and have another go the next morning. This time they killed 300 rabbits and the Duke's bag was 86. The 'Argus' pointed out, however, that it wasn't just crude slaughter. It was all done in a most sporting manner and magnificent shooting.

There came from Sydney some appropriate verse:

The Duke of auld reekie —

Pray don't think me cheeky

To nickname is one of my habits —

Has been out with his gun,

And has shot every one

Of four hundred and sixty-one

rabbits.

They died quite elated,

Being shot by a live Prince, you see.

And I hear 'tis said now,

That the upper crust vow,

The chief diet shall rabbit pie be.

The shoots at 'Barwon Park' were always very grand with appropriate beaters, dogs, etc. Eric Rolls reports that they held no respect for the sanctity of Sundays. On Easter Sunday, 1868, one of the party poked a bearded face from behind a shrub and was taken for rabbit. He was still alive when he reached hospital, but after that the Geelong register appeared uninterested in his eventual fate.

Thomas Austin wasn't the only man to bring rabbits to Australia, but he deserves some of the credit. He even helped in 1866 by sending several pairs to Queensland to establish a sort of northern branch of the family.

The National Trust proposes to preserve 'Barwon Park,' to operate the property to defray expenses and keep it as a tourist attraction. Somewhere on the property there should be a nice sculpture, done in bronze, in honour of those first two rabbits that came out aboard the Lightning.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Immortalising a Rabbit Warren, The Bulletin, 25 October 1969. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
