The Queue — A Kind of Cherished Tradition

6 min read.

First published 23 Aug 1969

Melbourne psychologist Leon Mann, now lecturing at Harvard, publishes his research on Melbourne's football-final ticket queues, finding that 25,000 people once waited a full week in mud for 12,500 tickets, that veterans regard queuing as a cherished ritual earning Agincourt-level kudos, and that organised scalpers flew Tasmanian students to Melbourne at $1 an hour to stand in line before reselling tickets at $35 apiece.

MELBOURNE does not hold any queue world records but, nevertheless, our queueing has great quality. When the time comes to issue the tickets for the football finals there is a doggedness, a patient yearning persistence in our queueing, which is hardly equalled anywhere in the world.

And if there is such quality in our queueing it is only natural that we should produce world queue experts. On this page, September 24, 1966, we told you some of the findings of Dr. Leon Mann of the Psychology Department at the University of Melbourne. Since then Dr. Mann has gone on and on and now he is lecturing at Harvard. Indeed, next October he intends to publish his findings in the 'American Journal of Sociology,' the paper to be titled 'Queue Culture; The Waiting Line as a Social System.'

As a cricket-lover one is shocked at the way Dr. Mann indelicately refers to the MCG as the Melbourne Football Stadium, but he describes with accuracy queue conditions in 1965, '66 and '67. The queue of 1965, he believes, was the most remarkable. 'For in that year 25,000 people waited for 12,500 tickets, some of them for over a week in mud and drizzling rain. Queuers erected a shanty town of tents and caravans outside the stadium.'

But one of his interesting discoveries was the acceptance of queues in Melbourne. An increasing number of veterans regarded them as a kind of cherished tradition or ritual. He proved this by pointing out that even during the regular season, when it was possible to get choice seats two hours before the start of most Saturday games, long queues formed outside stadiums on Friday.

Not only did Melburnians tolerate queues, they actually seemed to enjoy them, and he gives a quote by a woman in the 'Age' of August 16, 1967: 'People are always knocking queues, what I would like to know is what people like myself would do without them.'

Leon Mann had teams of research assistants. In 1967, after a dreadful cold wet night, they interviewed the all-nighters, to discover even then that 26 percent were happy with the queue system. In 1966 when they tested 122 queuers on a mild evening 47 percent reported satisfaction.

Dr. Mann, in his paper, made it sound exciting. He wrote: 'For several days in August the attention of Melbourne and its mass media is focused on the brave queues outside its stadium. To be able to claim in football-mad Melbourne that one has stood through the night and obtained tickets earns the kind of kudos and respect that must have been given to those who fought at Agincourt.'

He gives a profile of the queuer: 'The typical queuer is male, not yet 25 years of age, lives in a working-class suburb, and probably has absented himself from work to wait in line. Together with three friends he has waited for at least 15 hours to get tickets to watch his club play in the finals. He cannot explain why he likes football but has always followed his club.'

He has written fascinating stuff on the aggressiveness of our queues. There have been cases of violence, but these are rare. He says that during the early hours of August 15, 1967, four separate brawls broke out in the ticket lines and five men were taken to hospitals. But our queues generally were meek. They would accept intruders. At the stadium, whenever outsiders approached the head of the queue they were intimidated by cat calls and jeering. Ordinarily this worked during daylight, 'the pressure of concerted disapproval inhibiting all but the boldest,' but it was less effective in the dark.

And, he asks, why doesn't the queue act in unison to dismiss the queue-jumper? Well, at the front they don't care much about the people who push in behind them. For the others there was the thought that the intruder might be desperate and a struggle might cause injury and damage. There was the fear, too, that if they all acted then the queue system might disintegrate completely.

He has written some interesting material on queue customs. Provided one stakes a claim, provided one gives evidence of accepted periods of 'queue suffering' it is quite all right to leave the queue for various periods. 'It is an accepted practice to stake a claim in a queue by leaving some kind of personal property. One can keep a place in a line with a labelled box, folding chair, haversack or sleeping bag for quite long periods. The object stands for the person and his place, symbolism reminiscent of burial customs of the ancient Egyptians.' In the Collingwood queue of 1966 irate latecomers who noticed that many people in the middle of the queue had not made an appearance for most of the day, spontaneously seized their boxes and burnt them.

Dr. Mann has noted two kinds of professional activity in our Melburnian queueing. There were groups of highly organised people, hired at a fee to wait and buy tickets for large business concerns, and small operators who resold their two tickets to the highest bidder, often university students trying to pay their tuition fees. In 1967 'two days before ticket sales opened at the Melbourne Stadium, 20 students flew from Tasmania to the mainland to join the queue. The airline company which hired them to buy the tickets also provided free return flights, accommodation at a leading hotel and taxis during their stay. An advertising agency engaged Melbourne university students to stand in line for $1 an hour each, each to be given prizes. People asked for $50 for $5.60 tickets and had no difficulty in getting their price.'

And, on another occasion outside the Richmond ground, Dr. Mann noticed another unique presence: 'queue counters.' Some people concerned at the number of people left wanted to know how many people were in front of them. Queue counters were boys and they earned 10 cents a count.

He could cite some evidence of humanity. In 1967, five days before tickets went on sale, the Collingwood secretary, Gordon Carlyon, received a letter addressed to Mr. Alfred McDougall, c/o Queue outside the Collingwood Football Ground, Collingwood 3066. Mr. Carlyon threaded his way through beds and tents on the sidewalk to deliver the letter.

Dr. Mann was in Melbourne briefly last week. It is interesting that he did not dawdle to take part in a queue or see the finals. He has now returned to Harvard.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The Queue — A Kind of Cherished Tradition, The Bulletin, 23 August 1969. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
