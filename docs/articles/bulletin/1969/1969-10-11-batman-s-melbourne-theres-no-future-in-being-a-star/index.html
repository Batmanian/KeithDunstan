There's No Future in Being a Star

6 min read.

First published 11 Oct 1969

Melbourne's new tabloid evening newspaper Newsday launches with 450,000 copies on its first day, its columnists including Zara Holt and Harry Beitzel, and a column named for John Pascoe Fawkner — prompting Keith Dunstan to compare it with the 1933 Melbourne Star, the Argus's tabloid evening experiment that sold 26,000 on its first day, rose to 62,000, and folded after three years.

THE FIRST to attack 'Newsday' was 'Architect,' the journal of the Victorian chapter of the Royal Australian Institute of Architects. Months ago it condemned the masthead: 'One may forgive the lack of striking originality in the name, but is it absolutely essential to pinch, also, the style of "Newsweek"?'

Yet, very likely, only one newspaper in this country has ever had an original name and that is the Woomera 'Gibber Gabber.' Furthermore, this is an era when newspapers perish more easily than they are born, so we looked forward to the arrival of a new evening sheet with some excitement.

The 'Herald' prepared for battle weeks beforehand. Sub-editors and reporters were called on before dawn. The first edition came out before noon instead of 1 p.m. The make-up of the leader page was pushed forward half a century and the finance section was enlarged and revamped. The quotes, now easier to read, went on to the back of the first section, where Garth, Tiffany Jones, Peanuts and all the comics used to be. In the financial capital there could have been some psychological significance in this.

'Newsday' was actually announced in the 'Age' on September 13. We were told this was the most exciting development in Melbourne journalism in 30 years. We would have 'a new, vital, alternative newspaper every afternoon.' Day by day more and more information was released. There would be 'a fast kaleidoscope of people flashing through the small tabloid pages in short, sharp, pointed stories.' The stars would be 'the incomparable Zara Holt,' Jennie Ham, former top umpire Harry Beitzel, 'forthright, provocative, right' Ken Knox, John Pinkney, 'a jewel of a writer,' Bert Bryant and more.

The 'Age,' in a dignified leader, said: 'For 115 years we have published the "Age." It is a paper as solid as its name.' And referring to 'Newsday,' it continued, 'we hope you will accept it as a lusty child fighting its way into an outside world that includes formidable competition.'

Your correspondent went back to pack memories of the last great Melburnian evening adventure. 'There are some intriguing parallels. The "Star" of 1933, launched one month in the year later than "Newsday," was the child of an aged parent, the "Argus." "Newsday" has come out of a new building after the "Age" left Collins Street for Spencer Street. The "Star" came out of an almost new building after the "Argus" left Collins Street for Flinders Street.

Your correspondent went back to 1933 and investigated the old papers. The 'Argus' announced it all rather discreetly in June that year. It said 'The "Argus" is expecting.' It was modest about giving the precise birth-date, 'but the arrangements for the lying-in are well in hand.' And it went on: 'There is a generally accepted theory that elderly people produce the most brainy offspring. If this is correct, then the infant "Star" should be a wonder child, for its mama at birth will be just on 90.' The 'Argus' admitted that this wasn't the first child. It had a little brother, the 'Mail.' It died after less than three years back in the 'eighties. '"Poor" circulation and malnutrition seem to have been its trouble, like those of so many newspapers,' said mother.

News of the new newspaper was out day by day. It would have the latest equipment, the best machinery and the decision to be a tabloid 'in harmony with the latest developments in evening-newspaper practice.' There would be unvarnished facts, fearless and frank opinion, special attention would be paid to sporting columns, the brilliance of finance, women's interests, all would be there — stage, cinema, literature. The price: 14d. a copy. Leicester M. Cotton, a brilliant young Australian, pictorial editor of the 'News Chronicle' in London, would bring with him the latest ideas in pictorial journalism.

The 'Star' published on October 30 sold 26,000 copies and reported that there was a complete sell-out, with the newsagents still clamouring for more. There were congratulations from the Prime Minister, Mr. Lyons, from the Lord Mayor, Mr. Gengoult Smith, and the Minister for Lands, Mr. Dunstan. It was 32 pages, news in narrow columns on the front page, sport on the back. The lead story was a speech by the Prince of Wales in Pendennis about Prince George coming for the Melbourne centenary celebrations.

'Newsday' on its first day printed 450,000 with the people still asking for more. Like the 'Star' it had a lead royal story — 'Prince Charles and Princess Anne coming here.' Like the 'Star' it had news on the front page, sport on the back, and women's stories in the centre section. But there the similarity ends. 'Newsday' depends heavily on bright, lively features, columnists and bylines, with 52 pages. The first 'Star' with 32 pages had few features, no bylines, but 128 actual news stories as opposed to 51 in 'Newsday.'

Batman thinks it is terrific to have another evening newspaper in Melbourne and wishes them all possible fortune. The columnist is named John Pascoe Fawkner. Frankly, Batman never got along with Fawkner and we think they're a little cheeky taking that name, however we shall try to get on as long as they raise no nonsense about who was the original founder.

By November 18, 1933, 'Smith's Weekly' was writing stories on the ferocious competition. Somehow the 'Herald' seemed to know everything that was going in the 'Star' before it appeared. It 'jumped' all the 'Star' promotions, and even special photographs appeared simultaneously in both papers. There were problems of false informations, so 'Star' reporters, when they telephoned their stories, were instructed to use a special code word before the telephone girls took them down. 'Star's' final circulation that year was 38,000. It rose year by year to 62,000 until it folded on April 9, 1936. The editor wrote: 'The "Star" pioneered a form of journalism unknown before in Melbourne. Accuracy, brightness and fearlessness have been its watchwords. The latest word in journalistic techniques have been found in its columns. Tonight the presses will print the last copy, the delivery cars will speed the last "Star" to some corner of our city — the night will see the passing of something which will leave the people poorer.'

Source: Batman's Melbourne: There's No Future in Being a Star, The Bulletin, 11 October 1969. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
