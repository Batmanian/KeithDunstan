Let's Not Cry Over Spilt Beer — Do Something

5 min read.

First published 22 Nov 1969

New international hotels are rising across Melbourne — the Southern Cross extension, a 40-storey Austin Anderson project, a 37-storey William Street proposal — while more than 30 old pubs have closed since 1938, including the labyrinthine Port Phillip Club, the Occidental with its bar later hired for the film 'On the Beach' where Ava Gardner leaned against it, and the Lord Clyde, prompting Keith Dunstan's call to preserve one last authentic Melbourne pub with dung-coloured lino and a haunting smell of urine.

THE THEORY IS that there are so many businessmen here now, so many conventioneers, so many tourists that Melbourne is desperately short of first-class hotel beds. We should have some big and handsome new hotels. People talk. But one could say it's purely the subtleties of business life. The big operators say expressly that it's hotels and offices not the ordinary hotel outside, but Melburnians themselves aren't so sure.

I do not say that the hotel here is not a big place. Paris has hotels too, but even there we would not say one describes a living place. Well, the new can-built hotels have 500 Hilton rooms and the Hilton Total Affairs are building more. There are now 38-age mansions all in Paris groups, too. The tourist business is building. These are buildings, Collins Street, 20 storey groups to come.

The Southern Cross. Austin Anderson Constructions have talked of a 40-storey hotel in the centre of the city and Master Hosts Motor Hotels have announced a 37-storey hotel for William Street. We could have 3000 extra first-class hotel beds within three years. One only hopes that the swinging city will be able to keep them filled.

All these hotels will have first-class restaurants, capacious convention rooms and at least one promises a swimming-pool on the roof. But in all this there has been no mention of what is happening to the lovable old indigenous Melburnian pub.

Dear old Menzies is now a deep hole in the ground, where Mr. Watts will shortly pour a concrete raft for the construction of the BHP building. The Melbourne Hotel, on the corner of Exhibition and Bourke, a lovely place for a counter lunch, has just closed its doors for ever.

More than 30 hotels have closed in the city area since 1938. Perhaps our children will never know the warm, friendly, close-contact drinking conditions of the famous 'forties.

Just pause for a moment to think of the pubs that have gone. There was the labyrinthine Port Phillip Club, which used to have the punkahs in the main bar. On hot February days they used to groan back and forth, making ripples on the beer, and oysters used to be 2s. 6d. a dozen.

The Occidental, in Collins Street, had a huge ornate bar. We used to lean on it, eat sausages and mash or curry and rice. That bar was bought for the bar scene in 'On the Beach,' and if ever you see that film again please note Ava Gardner leaning against it.

While you are still crying I will give you names of pubs that have gone. The Victoria Horse Market, which used to open early for the marketeers to get a drink before six in the morning. There was Phairs, said to be a haunt of Squizzy Taylor's mob. There was the Lord Clyde, the Yarra Family, the Kerry Family, the Cathedral, the City Club, the Devon, the Astoria, the Saracen's Head, the Swanston Family. They called them Family hotels because they were the ideal place to bring the family. There was Ushers, the New York, the Busy Bee, the Apollo Inn, The Bull and Mouth and, alas, Mario's and the House of Commons.

But the deeply worrying point is that those hotels which haven't been wrecked are being remodelled to compete with the new places. Wall-to-wall carpet in the public bar is now absolutely de rigueur. According to the Australian Hotels Association, if you don't do this you've had it.

Next you have to lay on the atmosphere. We are getting atmosphere broadly under four main headings. There is the Ye Olde Hollywood Village Inne, lead-light glass, gas-style lamps, lots of timber. There is the Ship Inn look with capstans, rope ladders, sailcloth, compasses, anchors and the terrific wall-to-wall carpet. Category three comes in two sub-sections. Either you get the outback bit 'Sentimental Bloke' style or the Captain Ben Bolt highwayman look. In either case it is good to have lots of leather, harness, saddlery, with plastic flintlock pistols on the walls. Category four calls for the sensitive, artistic look with whitewashed walls and paintings offered for sale by local artists.

What are we to do about this? Could we not start a fund to preserve a genuine old Melbourne pub to be kept for ever, precisely as we remembered it. This page will call for expert advice from its readers. But for a start we would hunt the country for the traditional dung-coloured lino to go on the floor and matching dung-coloured lino for the bar. The hotel would need the dear old yellowing tiles, the lovable chipped Fosters and VB mirrors, the cardboard signs for Penfolds and Tintara, a clock with letters for some wine company in place of figures. The windows would be painted over with grey paint and barred. There would be an arrow indicating 'IT'S HERE' and there would be a gentle, haunting smell of urine.

There would be a marvellous simplicity of the purest kind. Those who recall the famous forties and furious fifties will remember that between 5.30 and 6.00 p.m. there was such an urgency about all action there was no room for obstruction. So there was a starkness, an absence of clutter, not a seat, not a stool, not even a ledge. Could we recapture this in one last monument?
