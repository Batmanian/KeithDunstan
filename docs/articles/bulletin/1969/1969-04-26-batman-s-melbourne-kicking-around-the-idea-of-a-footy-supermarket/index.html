Kicking Around the Idea of a Footy Supermarket

3 min read.

First published 26 Apr 1969

The Victorian Football League's 1930 Coulter Law forbids the sale of players while the trade goes on regardless — North Melbourne reportedly paying $2000 for a Box Hill centreman — leading Keith Dunstan to propose a used-footballer market modelled on American baseball, where entire teams are owned by millionaires and Dalgety's could auction the top players.

IN MELBOURNE we have had what has been described as a 'football sensation.' The secretary of the Victorian Football Association, Mr. Fred Hill, alleged that North Melbourne paid $2000 for a Box Hill centreman, named Geoff Bryant. There has been some splendid mystery stuff, reminiscent of the protection days of Al Capone, about how the money in $10 and $20 bills was handed over in the dark outside the Box Hill office.

You see, in 1930, the Victorian Football League passed what is now known as the Coulter Law, and it forbade the sale of footballers. The 'Herald's' sporting editor, Jack Cannon, commented: 'It is about as relevant to modern football as leather lace-up jackets and knickerbockers. The only reason the bigger and wealthier clubs have not been caught breaking the Coulter Law is that they have got so much experience they leave no fingerprints.'

In view of your correspondent there is far too much coyness here over the sale of footballers and the game is losing its best opportunity for publicity. In world Soccer the price value of footballers is used as publicity. When Britain's Everton goes on tour, the advance PR men talk of the $5-million team, and we get a list of the cash prices paid for each player. The day may come when the same thing will happen here.

Back in 1967 it was marvellous what went unsaid over the 'miracle' clearance of the champion full-forward Peter Hudson from New Norfolk, in Tasmania, to Hawthorn, Victoria.

In England players can get a transfer fee of 10 percent, and in Italy 15 percent. This could explain the sadness of the present case in Melbourne. Mr. Bryant, the ex-Box Hill player, was quoted recently as saying wistfully: 'I never heard any mention of money.'

Personally, I am rather taken with the American system. There, it is the custom for organisations, or kindly millionaires, to own entire baseball teams. Mrs. Joan Payson, with her brother, had been left about $270 million. So, in 1961, she established the New York Metropolitans, better known as the Mets. That year the Mets bought 22 players at a cost of $1,800,000.

This could be arranged on exactly the same lines as the used-car yards. There would be regular list prices for standard players, so much for rovers, ruckmen, wingmen, and so on. The Automobile Club would provide an inspection service so that for a fee you could have your footballer looked over for condition before you bought him. Naturally we wouldn't expect the very top footballers to go this way. Some of the imported models might be very expensive and the best method would be to have them auctioned — I'm sure one of the big firms such as Dalgety's would be only too happy to do the job.

On behalf of Box Hill I would have advertised: 'Geoff Bryant, 22, muscular, blond, one owner, mark-free, used in centre only by kindly Association team, guaranteed for three months or money back, $2000, terms available.'

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Kicking Around the Idea of a Footy Supermarket, The Bulletin, 26 April 1969. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
