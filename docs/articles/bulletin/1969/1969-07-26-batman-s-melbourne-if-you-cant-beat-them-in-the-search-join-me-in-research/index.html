If You Can't Beat Them in the Search, Join Me in Research

5 min read.

First published 26 Jul 1969

The arrival of Hong Kong flu prompts Keith Dunstan's Batman White Paper on the winter disease, tracing its escalating nomenclature from Asian flu through Russian flu to the present, noting research scientists' consistent inability to find a cure, and contrasting their diffidence with the supreme confidence of Mrs. Beeton and the 1836 'Magazine of Domestic Economy,' whose cures involved turpentine flannels, garden snails, and stream frogs.

A REPORTER on the Melbourne 'Herald' has just given an account of his attack of Hong Kong flu — coughing fits, kidney pains, long uncontrolled shivering, delirium. It can only be described as a major scoop. Hong Kong flu has been pretty hard to come by until now. The 'Sun,' the 'Age,' and every other paper were clearly embarrassed by not being able to produce a case first.

Yet we are warned constantly of the approaching menace, and our newest pop group, called the Hong Kong Flu, is giving the disease a singular honour. Although, I understand, there are at least 73 varieties of the influenza virus, Hong Kong flu is the first to have a pop group named after it.

Therefore, it is time for another of the famous Batman Research White Papers. It is a rewarding study to go back through the newspaper files and read the stories on what I prefer to call, for scientific purposes, the 'winter disease' — too many other names have been invented to confuse the issue, as I will illustrate.

The first major attempt to give the disease more glamour came in 1956. The Japanese in Tokyo, who had already invented the mini-transistor radio, then added to their prestige by discovering Asian flu. In 1958 we had the beginning of the Echo series, and we started to get Echo 6. There was competition for this also, and I think it was John Crosby who told the story of a Hollywood scriptwriter who flew to New York to get Echo 6, just so he could be first back with it in Los Angeles. Echo 6 then disappeared. There is little point in having last year's disease.

By 1959 the viruses were being discussed with great fluency. There was a good variety — A, B, C, D and even several varieties of A, one of them Asian. In 1960, URTI was all the rage, and this was particularly favoured by Brisbane doctors. URTI was short for upper respiratory tract infection. The name, one felt, had a touch of class for a winter disease, and it's no wonder we didn't keep it. In 1961 Virus X, a strange, unisolated virus, was abroad, and obviously an unisolated virus was far more awesome than anything that had been isolated.

There was the American flu in 1963, and the U.S. was the first country to use winter disease as a serious political weapon. It was isolated as A/2 North Carolina 1/63 strain and it was a great help to be able to call the office and announce that one had A/2 North Carolina 1/63 strain. Naturally, the Russians struck back, so in 1965 we had the Russian flu. It began in Leningrad on January 9, 1965. An Altona doctor announced the discovery of our first case here on February 19, only a month after Russia, so this was a good effort.

After this one notices a change. The Russian flu was quickly forgotten. It seemed to have more impact to accuse your enemy of having a flu rather than to invent one yourself. It seems no one wants to own the Hong Kong flu. There are stories that it began in China. In Britain they call it the Mao flu. In Russia they call it the Chinese pestilence. In Hong Kong there is resentment over the name. Very likely in Peking it is known as the Harold Wilson Pestilence.

However, the most interesting discovery I made from reading 15 years of newspapers was the constant reiteration by research men of the inability to find a cure for the winter disease.

So, to add balance to this White Paper, it was important to consult the records of the Melbourne State Library. Mrs. Beeton's 'Book of Household Management' has an entire chapter entitled 'How to Cure a Cold.' No lack of confidence here. For a cold on the chest she commands: 'A flannel dipped in boiling water and sprinkled with turpentine, laid on the chest as quickly as possible, will relieve the most severe cold or hoarseness.' Apparently if there was any chest left the cold was gone in two to three days.

On the other hand, the 'Magazine of Domestic Economy,' 1836, had a series on the common cold written by an anonymous but distinguished London doctor. The doctor pointed out that two types of people were most afflicted with the common cold, the poorer classes and 'the gay victims of dissipation.' The abuse of stimulating liquors, the overindulgence in undue quantities of ale, porter, wine, or spirits was a fearful cause of this indisposition, he said.

So here is his cure: 'First put the feet and legs into very hot water, as hot as the feelings will permit, for 15 to 20 minutes. After this get into a warm bed and take one or two grains of ipecacuanha. In half an hour the patient must have a large quantity of very thin gruel. He should be awakened every two or three hours for more gruel and then in the morning he should take an active aperient like epsom salt.' The doctor added that the patient should have no food whatever, just his routine of emetics, gruel, and aperients until he, undoubtedly, felt better.

Not good enough? Well what about Home Hint No. 560 to be found in 'The Family Save All' edition of 1861. This remedy was highly recommended in France, they said. 'Collect two dozen garden snails and add to these hind-quarters of two dozen stream frogs, skinned. Bruise them together in a mortar, after which put them in a stew pan with a couple of turnips chopped small, a little salt, a quarter ounce of hay saffron and three pints of spring water. Stir well on the fire, skim, simmer and strain through a cloth.' According to 'Family Save All' the soothing effects of this broth will conquer any cough.

These remedies have been utterly ignored by the Rockefeller Foundation, the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute and others. However, if they wish to receive the complete details of the Batman White Paper your correspondent is available at his desk from 9 a.m. to 6 p.m. daily.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: If You Can't Beat Them in the Search, Join Me in Research, The Bulletin, 26 July 1969. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
