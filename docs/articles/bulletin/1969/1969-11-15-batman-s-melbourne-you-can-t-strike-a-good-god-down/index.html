You Can't Strike a Good God Down

6 min read.

First published 15 Nov 1969

The 1969 Melbourne Cup is nearly wrecked when the Railways Union and Tramways Union threaten to stop the special trains and trams to Flemington, the bank clerks call a Monday strike, and the Trades Hall Council places a black ban on all TAB sporting fixtures, until Chief Secretary Arthur Rylah cuts short an oil-rig inspection tour to negotiate a settlement, Sir Paul Hasluck arrives in a Rolls-Royce in top hat and tails, and 85,000 faithful attend the religious festival undisturbed.

JOHN FRANCIS HOGAN once wrote that animal worship was a common practice in Eastern religions, therefore it was not unusual for Australians to revere the horse as a god. All religions have a deeply holy day, and our most sacred festival is the first Tuesday in November. Admittedly, these times it is hard to get the young to show proper respect. For example, around 1926 we used to get 118,000 in the congregation at Flemington. Now we do well if 82,000 to 85,000 make an appearance.

Quite properly, there is a holiday in Melbourne for our religious festival. According to the official list the banks were closed, so too were drapers, butchers, delicatessens, grocers, plumbers, dentists, State schools. Post offices opened at 9 a.m. but closed at 10 a.m. in time for the most sacred period of the day. The Stock Exchange was closed, but one regrets to say that this year many heretical stockbrokers flew to Sydney so that they could continue operations.

Strange and terrible forces were at work this year to disrupt our festival. First the Australian Railways Union and the Tramways Union threatened to stop the special trains and trams to the Flemington shrine. The unions pointed out that there were three sacred days not received by the unions like other State workers and this was one of them. Mr. O'Shea, the Tramways' leader, had a bout of conscience on interfering with the workers' transport on the way to church, but what else could he do? Fortunately, this fear cleared at the last moment.

But there was much worse to come. On the Friday before the holy Tuesday the Trades Hall disputes committee placed a black ban on all sporting fixtures covered by the TAB. The reasons were complex. But the main reason was that the State Government decided to set up a separate wages board for TAB employees. This, according to the Trades Hall, could have deprived them of an increase coming from the Commercial Clerks' Award.

And that wasn't all. The bank clerks called a sudden strike for the Monday — another grave and terrible affair. The timing of the strike was kept secret. Nobody knew until too late. The banks were closed on Saturday, Sunday and Monday, which meant that members of the congregation were unable to take out their collection money before the service on Tuesday.

Grave as this was, it wasn't as grave as the threat imposed by the Trades Hall Council. This would have stopped the TAB, all course workers, mobile-barrier men, trains, trams, bars, catering, ticket collectors, electricians, ambulances. The idea that the festival should be abandoned, however, did not even occur to us. We thought that the services should continue, even without staff, if only the right priests could go to work. We could have Mr. Creswick at one gate, Hilton Nicholas at another. Sir Chester Manifold could drive the mobile barrier. Rupert Steele could sell the chicken sandwiches.

But many of us wondered at the bravado of the Trades Hall Council. We have had some terrible strikes in recent times, strikes that have closed all the power houses, strikes that have closed all the factories, strikes that have grounded the aeroplanes, stopped all public transport, gas strikes that have left us with cold houses and cold stoves, strikes that have left our schools without teachers, strikes that have even left us without a city fire brigade. But this interfering with the religious festival on the first Tuesday in November was altogether going too far. The people could easily riot.

It is good to be able to report that the Government saw the enormity of the situation. The Chief Secretary, Sir Arthur Rylah, when the crisis blew up, was on an inspection tour of oil rigs in Bass Strait, and dashed back to Melbourne for emergency talks at the Treasury buildings, called by the head of the Chief Secretary's Department, Mr. Dillon. Sensibly, there was an immediate settlement and the chairman of the Victoria Racing Club, Hilton Nicholas, said they would set up a separate wages board for TAB employees. The secretary of the Trades Hall said 'the TAB workers will receive 100 percent of its potential.' And Sir Arthur announced to reporters that the festival would go on. There was general relief over this.

But one thought occurred to us. What would Sir Paul do? It was generally felt that he was a non-believer. Right back to the beginning State governors had attended the race and it was absolutely de rigueur for the Governor-General to present the cup. One remembered Cup Day 1950 when Mr. Paul Hasluck, then a member for Curtin, protested. Your correspondent turned back to Hansard. At 11.8 p.m. on November 7, 1950, Mr. Hasluck complained to the House that both the Senate and the House of Representatives had delayed their normal starting times. He said: 'When we reflect on what happened we must surely realise that we, the members of the National Parliament, formally demonstrated and declared to the people that anything that we might have done between 2.30 and 3.15 p.m. was far less important than finding out whether one horse could get its nose in front of another horse in a certain race.' The Leader of the Opposition, Mr. Chifley, said he had not prepared the notice and even the highest courts in the land stopped for The Cup. The Prime Minister, Mr. Menzies, also had strong feelings of the people. He said: 'As Prime Minister notorious throughout the House, I accept complete responsibility. I do not think the four million people who listened to the broadcast will have any complaints.'

So it was with relief one noted that Sir Paul Hasluck found the festival on November 4 this year the most vital thing happening on November 4. He arrived in an open Rolls-Royce and drove down the straight accompanied by the Southern Command band and a full contingent of the Victorian mounted police. Sir Paul also was in proper vestments, top hat and tails.

You may have noticed also that a serious political crisis has been taking place in the country. But the figures involved in that crisis knew their priorities. They still found time to attend matins and evensong at the shrine that Tuesday. Present there were Messrs. McMahon, McEwen, Fairbairn, Barnes, Nixon and, of course, Sir Henry Bolte, who was absolutely beautiful with a solid mile of roses blooming there before the altar.

It was a lovely day, with a congregation of 85,000.
