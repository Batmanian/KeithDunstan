A vineyard to end all vineyards

4 min read.

First published 23 Mar 1974

The grand opening of the Mitchelton winery on the Goulburn River near Nagambie reveals a $3.5 million operation with nine radio-equipped hostesses, three licensed restaurants, a 200-foot tower, an aviary, a swimming pool, a chapel and a supermarket-style tasting room designed to process 1000 visitors a day.

BACK IN THE 1880s we were crazily optimistic about our wine production in Victoria. South Australia and New South Wales were junior wine colonies and we looked upon ourselves as the new Bordeaux. What with the small population it must have been a deep labor of love to consume all the wine we were producing. Then phylloxera struck and nigh on 90 percent of the vines perished.

Well, there was a grand opening this week of the Mitchelton vineyard which could recall the grand old days. The managing director of Mitchelton is Ross Shelmerdine, a restless Victorian businessman with a flair for showmanship. Your correspondent has labored through a few vineyards around the world, but never anything like this. Just to put it briefly, Mitchelton is on the banks of the Goulburn River near Nagambie, on the other side of the water from Chateau Tahbilk. Apart from 250 acres under vine, there are three licensed restaurants, a market, a wild life reserve, a display farm, a swimming pool, a waterfall, a 200ft tower, an aviary, a chapel and other things such as an art display and a resident artist.

The cost has been over $3.5 million. Shelmerdine has geared the whole operation for a constant large-scale flow and he is expecting 1000 a day. There is a bitumen area for 500 cars at the gatehouse, where you pay your dollar to get in. You are not allowed to take your car any further — there is a fleet of electric carts to take you around to the tour.

You are met at the door by a hostess. There are nine, all radio equipped, so that the staff will know where each party is at any time. So you go on the tour. First you walk through beautiful white Aptos cloisters softened by flowers and vines to the winery. This has a dramatic sloping roof and is as lofty as a cathedral. None of the dear old earth floor, galvanised roof, leaky vat atmosphere of the old days. This is as modern as a pie factory. There are tiles on the floor, green tiles on the walls, fluorescent lights shaped like barrels. Next the guide takes you down to the cellars. These are startling indeed, not so much for their extraordinary size — they're built of local bluestone — as for the route, the labelling, the design and all.

Next you come to the chapel. This is pure white with heavy brown beams. At most chapels I have visited you get baptismal fonts or Stations of the Cross. Here you get a bowl of earth, a crystal of water, a bunch of real grapes and a fresh loaf of bread, symbols of the good life. Your guide who has a little communications radio on her belt, stops before two great doors and checks whether they are ready for you inside. Then she throws open the doors.

The effect is meant to make you say 'Wow!' and it does. Here is the tasting room. It reminds one a little of the fancier bars in the Hotel Wentworth, only it's more spectacular. There is a long bar and there are stands everywhere with all the products of Mitchelton. Then there's the supermarket technique. You can pick up a 'handypack' which can take anything from two bottles to a dozen and it is decorated with the history of the area. Sweet music is playing. If you don't buy now you never will. Naturally everything is air conditioned. Next door is the Major's Crossing cellar bistro. Brick arches, bright red carpet, prices about the average of quality restaurants.

But you don't have to eat there. You can take the lift which is lined with pure Australian wool and go up an incredible 200ft tower with a witch's hat on top. Up there you can see for miles. You can walk around and look at the underground changing rooms because they're underground. You can look at the big walk-in aviary packed with cockatoos. You can make up your mind whether you want to visit the Travellers' Rest.

You look down on the market. This is filled with shops where you can buy many products of the area — fresh fruit from Shepparton, peaches, pears, apricots, meat killed locally, fresh bread, moleskin trousers, plus all the country gear from baling wire to sheep dip.

Shelmerdine is all confidence: 'We want to show tourists what Australia is really, in comfort, without their having to eat a bag of chips.'
