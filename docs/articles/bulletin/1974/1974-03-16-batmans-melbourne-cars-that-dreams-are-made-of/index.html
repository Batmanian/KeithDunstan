Cars that dreams are made of

5 min read.

First published 16 Mar 1974

The Melbourne Motor Show at the Exhibition Building showcases Maserati, Ferrari, Lamborghini, Aston Martin and De Tomaso alongside a Range Rover, a Leyland floating in dry ice and an experimental safety Toyota, all priced from $19,000 to $55,000, and not one person mentions the energy crisis.

THOSE OF US who live permanently in cloudland are always deeply moved by the Motor Show. We have just had ours in the grand old Exhibition Building. The organisers always do what they can to nurture this cloudland image. As I entered the building and nomdrali those lovely curved bonnets which my nervous hands wanted to stroke, the theme music from Love Story was coming over the amplifying system.

At head office they had a number of models available for photographers. One, in a tiny little black playsuit, had half her breasts showing like headlamps. You could put her at the wheel of a Maserati, Porsche or Jaguar and she was very popular indeed.

Display also enhanced one's cloudland dreams. Honda had a marvellous tubular steel tower stacked with indoor plants that went almost to the ceiling. Leyland, however, was the best. They had built a real pond, right out there on the exhibition floor. Around it they put rocks and ferns. They had water lillies, a fair dinkum little fountain, and lo, out there in the middle of the water sat an eight cylinder Leyland. Every so often they heaped some dry ice into the water and up came gorgeous mist. It gave the Leyland overtones of pure magic, like that beautiful moment in Cinderella when the pumpkin is turned into a golden coach.

There were some very tempting cars. I was taken with the latest Range Rover. Here is the Land Rover which has moved into pure luxury — lovely upholstery, carpet, air conditioning. The sheep you are carrying in the back can now move in comfort, but I understand it is very non-U for a real outback cattleman to buy the Range Rover. They are terrified at being mistaken for a Collins Street or Pitt Street farmer.

Also there was an experimental safety Toyota. This had a long snout so that it could absorb crashes at 70 mph or more. When one stepped into the car, lights flashed on and off like Emergency Ward 10, warning that the door wasn't closed properly and the seat belt not tightened. But the seat belt was ingenious. After you had taken your position a sensory device under the seat noted your weight and tightened the belt automatically. What's more, there was an air bag, designed to blow out of the dash board and fill the cabin on impact, presumably before your nose crunched on the steering wheel.

What else? Well, as you know the speed in Victoria is now a severely administered 60 mph. I would like to bestow an annual award each year for the car which, in the fastest time, can reach 60 mph in first gear. We would have quite a number of candidates — Maserati, Ferrari, Aston Martin, Jaguar, De Tomaso, Lamborghini...

On a fashion note it was interesting that all these were of a pattern. Your interiors should be black. Every instrument, dial should be individually angled towards the driver. Your gear shift, mounted fairly high on the left-hand side, should be no more than 3in long. Your steering wheel should be surprisingly small and leather-covered. The configuration 2 plus 2 is also very popular. Two plus 2 means that there are two seats in the back but it is only possible to sit in them if you have been born without legs.

A typical 2 plus 2 was the De Tomaso Pantera. This was an Italian car with a Ford V8 engine. It could reach 154 mph in 24 seconds or five-eighths of a mile. This could be a candidate for our 60 mph award. Price: $20,500. But if that was too small you could have the large De Tomaso Deauville for $26,000.

The Aston Martin I liked. Bright red, beautiful English leather upholstery, 5.4 litre V8, 160 mph, 'suggested price,' thank-you, $29,750.

Ferrari was well-represented. The Dino was there, a small car with the engine in the middle. You tended to stand back a pace or two just in case it might bite. This was the car Enzo Ferrari named in honor of his son, who was killed. The salesman said that in Italy it never bore any other name but Dino, but out here they had 'Ferrari' names added, so that people would not be confused.

They also had a nice family-sized, four-seater Ferrari, as roomy as a Kingswood. This could do 155 mph and you could have it for $32,000.

Lamborghini is another of my cloudland cars. My first contact with a Lamborghini was in a film called The Italian Job. The evil ones in this movie seized a beautiful new Lamborghini and after disposing of its owner, tipped the car over a cliff. As it plunged down, crashing from rock to rock, there was an anguished gasp from the audience. Theatre audiences can watch humans being tortured and cut into slices without even pausing over their ice-creams, but the massacre of a Lamborghini is too much.

Just lately there have been advertisements in the newspapers for 'the new Lamborghini Urraco — only $19,441.' Only? Why, a four-seater Lamborghini Espada is $36,000 and if you really want to get into the luxury class, a two-seater V12 will cost you around $55,000.

But I had a look at a Urraco. It was a very nice vivid yellow. Press a button and the headlights would pop up like the eyes of a snail. It was a great problem finding the engine. You lift up the bonnet. Not there. That's where they stow the spare wheel. So you go round and open the back compartment. Good heavens, it's not there either. That's the boot. Finally you lift up the long, sloping back window and there's the engine struck crossways down the middle. A 150 mph car, and the most advanced car of its type in the world, according to the local representatives.

Yes, a fascinating show, and not one person mentioned the possibility of an energy crisis.
