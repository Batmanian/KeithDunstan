Riding the ritzy rattler

4 min read.

First published 20 Jul 1974

Taking the overnight train to Sydney — where nobody frisks you, the dining car serves mushroom soup and entrecote while Melbourne's lights recede, and the conductor folds your bed and asks about your morning tea — Keith Dunstan commends a journey that ends at Central Station at 9 am, fresh to begin the day, though showering in a twinette roomette calls for particular skills.

IN CASE you haven't heard it is becoming the in thing to travel by train. It is particularly in to be able to say that you really prefer to go by jumbo rail overnight to Sydney.

I have done this several times recently and frankly it is a most remarkable experience. The best part about it is the thought that no-one is likely to hijack the diesel and divert you to the Sinai Desert. So, as you go through the barrier, nobody goes through your luggage, nobody frisks you for knives or guns and you don't have to step through one of those whistling gadgets.

Nor do they deem it necessary to play soppy music to ease your terror on take-off.

The best thing to do is to get to the train about 7.15 or 7.30 and have drinks in the lounge car before moving off at 8 pm. Then you can go straight in to the dining car, so that you can watch the receding lights of Melbourne as you have dinner.

We had aboard a Melbourne City Councillor, one of our top playwrights and several distinguished businessmen. One of them was a bit stunned by it all. He wanted to know if it was possible to walk right through the train from carriage to carriage. I suspected that the last time he observed a train was on a Wild West movie. So he was terrified that upon leaving the dining car he would have to walk back along the roof.

As we passed Broadmeadows another gentleman said patronisingly, 'This sure is a long runway. When do you think we'll get off the ground?'

The food generally is excellent. On the two occasions I found the same menu. They had a very crafty unspillable mushroom soup, which you could eat with a fork and a choice of Entrecote with Béarnaise sauce, followed by pudding with brandy sauce and the inevitable fruit salad and ice cream. The choice of wines was dreadful. Here the railways suffer.

Getting a twinette or roomette berth is quite an experience. They have two types of carriages, those with roomettes (one bed) and those with twinettes (two beds). Getting through the twinettes is not so bad — they have a straight passage. But the roomettes have a crazy zig-zag passage and, as the train sways, you tend to zig when you should zag.

The conductor folds down your bed when you are ready and explains all the beautiful forgotten services. There's the box where you put your shoes to be polished. What time would you like your morning tea? One lump or two? Sliced bread and butter or biscuits?

Your twinette has extraordinary little recesses for spectacles, books and such and little tables that flip down. You have your personal twinette-shower-lavatory attached. When you have a twinette you can dress, undress, shave and have a shower all at the one time.

Showering, though, calls for particular skills. For one thing if you scald yourself there is no escape. Second, you have to extricate the towels from a waterproof cupboard from behind your right buttock.

There certainly is not room to shower with a friend. Even showering on your own is a sexual experience.

Breakfast starts at 7 am but the best time to have it is around 8 am, and this is the best meal of all. The eggs and toast are excellent, good white cloth, noble, heavy, ancient railways silver, Moss Vale is galloping past and, as you drink your coffee, you can inspect suburban Sydney. Then at 9 am, lo, you are there. Right at Sydney Central station, not miles away at the airport and you are fresh to start your day.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Riding the ritzy rattler, The Bulletin, 20 July 1974. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
