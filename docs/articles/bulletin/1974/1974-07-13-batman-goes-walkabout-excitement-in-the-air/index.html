Excitement in the air

4 min read.

First published 13 Jul 1974

Bush Pilots Airways of Cairns — started in 1951 with a single Tiger Moth and now operating five DC3s on a weekly New Guinea tour for $275 all-inclusive — takes Keith Dunstan and retired passengers through Lae, Rabaul, Madang and Port Moresby, where snorkelling off a New Yorker-cartoon island leads one woman to reflect on the Allied campaign in the Pacific.

THE GREAT NORTHERN hotel in Cairns now has a duty free shop. It's as good as Nandi airport. It is stuffed with watches, Minoltas, Asahi Pentaxes, Sonys, Nationals, Dimple Haig, Black Label Johnny Walker, the lot. Since I last visited Cairns the Bush Pilots Airways have gone international.

Of course Qantas also started in north Queensland, and one wouldn't like to suggest that the Bush Pilots are looming as a dangerous rival just yet, but they're doing very nicely with a weekly tour of New Guinea. Hence the duty free shop. The passengers buy their tape recorders and such before they set out.

As a 20-year observer of the Bush Pilots I am fascinated by this progression. They started in 1951 with a single Tiger Moth and slowly they built up a network all over north Queensland, to the mission stations, to the outback properties, carting passengers black and white, mail, groceries, even the occasional stud ram. Now their circuit covers 13,000 miles with 121 ports of call.

But with the government progressively abolishing subsidies they have had to expand or perish. So they have acquired five DC3s, to add to their light aircraft fleet, and this is a move that has kept them out of the red.

Ah yes, nice reliable 30-year-old vintage DC3s that move along at a gentle 140 knots or even slower if there's a bit of wind about. The managing director of the Bush Pilots said: 'We're not the only people who have discovered that the DC3 is still a money maker. Air Niugini has bought 12, and Connellan Airways have bought some. Actually DC3s are in desperate short supply.'

The Bush Pilots studied their market. They found that 80 percent of all visitors to Cairns came by road. Of course, at least half of them sought accommodation in caravan parks.

Why not offer them something a little more exciting? Most of them were retired people and not all of them short of money. The idea has proved a winner — a four-day tour of New Guinea, everything provided — all meals, hotels and sight-seeing — for $275. I went on their tour — Lae, Rabaul, Madang and Port Moresby with a day in each. Our tour leader, Ray Oldman, wore a brilliant tropical shirt. Our hostess, Sue, wore a mumu and the cabin had pretty New Guinea motifs.

There was the friendly atmosphere of a Pioneer bus. 'If we ever get in trouble,' announced Ray Oldman, 'you'll know by looking out the window. You'll see a white mushroom going down. That'll be me, out first with my parachute.'

As for the tour, maybe the highlight was Madang. Here the tour stayed at the Smugglers' Inn which recently has been extended and now has an open-air dining room which juts right out over the water. The boys who wait on the tables wear hibiscus-decorated lap laps and flowers in their hair.

During the afternoon they organised a boat ride across the beautiful Madang harbour. There was snorkelling, and swimming off a white sandy beach on a little tropical island. This island, complete with palm trees, was like a cartoon out of the New Yorker. One lady turned on her husband and said: 'No wonder it took you five and a half bloody years to win the war. And all the time you were writing to me that you were up in the Green Hell.'

That night, in the foyer of the Smugglers' Inn, every New Guinean from 20 miles around was showing his wares, New Guinea masks, New Guinea pots, baskets of all shapes and sizes and heaven knows how many strings of beads. Our party bought enough masks, pots and beads to create another record payload for the DC3. But isn't it interesting how Captain Cook and all those chaps brought out beads for the natives. Now the white people are buying them all back again.
