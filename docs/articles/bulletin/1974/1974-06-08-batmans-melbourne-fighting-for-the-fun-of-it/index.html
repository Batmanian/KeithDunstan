Fighting for the fun of it

5 min read.

First published 08 Jun 1974

Melbourne's new War Games House — claimed to be the only building in the world entirely devoted to war games — features piped martial music, 300 members re-fighting battles from Waterloo to the hypothetical Sino-Soviet war, while its associated Viking Society stages full-scale axe-and-shield battles for $4 a minute a head, going through three shields in a single six-minute duel.

IT IS DOWNRIGHT interesting now that we have a reduced Army, a scattered Air Force and a Navy that you could put in the Yarra, that we have a marvellous new interest in the martial arts. We have just opened in Melbourne a War Games House.

Its owners claim that it is the only building in the world entirely devoted to war games. In the window they have battle flags — Old Glory, the swastika, the flag of the Chinese Red Army. Immediately you set foot through the door you hear piped music. But it is piped martial music with heavy brass drums. Automatically a man has to straighten his spine, pull in his stomach.

On shelf after shelf there are models of military equipment used during the past 500 years. Apart from Waterloo cannon there are World War I Fokkers, Spads, SE5s and then Phantoms, Mirages and F111s.

You never saw so many tanks — British Centurions, German Tigers and Panthers. If only they were real you could take all Australia in half an hour.

The officer commanding all of this is Max Hatrick, a former RAF man and president of the War Games Association. As you may know, war games are booming the world over. Enthusiasts use miniature military equipment, miniature soldiers, and making moves with all the intensity of a vast game of chess they re-fight the world's battles.

You can make up your own gear for these battles for you can buy special kits. For example, one kit is titled The African Campaign and the message on the box says: 'Match your skill against Rommel, or command the Afrika Korps as you relive history.'

But you can also be Hitler commanding the German forces as they invade Russia. You can take over the US Pacific fleet engaged in the battle of Midway. Or, if you don't like that, there is another kit called the Battle of Britain and you can have a nice old time deploying squadrons of Hurricanes and Spitfires against the invading Heinkels and Messerschmidts.

It is fascinating to note how up to date the kits are. There is a kit, complete with the most incredibly detailed information of the three Arab-Israeli wars, 1956, 1967 and 1973, called Sinai. The kit has just about everything but a black eye patch.

Then there is another game called The East Is Red — the Sino-Soviet war. This is for the war that has yet to happen, the ultimate holocaust between Russia and China. They give you a beautiful run-down of the comparative military and manpower strengths of both countries, and presumably it's nice to have this war already in your living-room. This time the blurb on the cover says: 'The armies of two great nations have been glaring at each other across their common border with ill-concealed hostility. A great mechanised army is moving tanks and men towards the border, preparing to launch a war against its foe . . . the June day could be 1974, '75 or '76, when the denouncing stopped in Peking and Moscow and the shooting started.'

There are 300 senior members in the association. Mostly they are fairly kindly men with such innocent occupations as computer programming, teaching, lecturing in photography, selling insurance.

One asked Hatrick if it was good for his ego to be, say, Napoleon Bonaparte at Waterloo. He said: 'Certainly not. During the weekend we re-fought the 1808 battle of Vimeiro in the Peninsula War. We reversed history, actually. I was General Junot and the French won. But in the original battle the French incredibly were overcome by a single British brigade.'

'Good heavens, then who were the mighty General Junot?' He replied: 'General Junot was an absolute idiot. I play the game because I enjoy the tactics.'

Also associated with War Games House is the Viking Society of Carlton. The Society is dedicated to the idea that the Vikings were among the bravest, noblest, most adventurous people who ever lived. They don't have any office bearers, because the free-living Vikings didn't work that way. But they do have an administrator, Brian Poke, a 31-year-old fireman with the North Melbourne fire brigade.

Brian Poke says they are divided into two divisions — the cultural side and the martial arts. The martial-arts division is only two months old but he reports they are making remarkable progress.

Twenty of them have created their own uniforms — dress helmet with horns, nose caps of shells. They make their own chain mail just as it was done 1000 years ago and some Carlton Vikings have become highly skilled.

So now for various functions they stage full-scale Viking battles with battle axes, spears and shields. They charge $4 a man a minute for each battle.

Your correspondent thought this charge was rather steep, but Poke said: 'You wouldn't think so if you were involved. We go through equipment at such a rate. Take a beserk duel, that's a battle with axe, sword and shield. A duel lasts six minutes and we have been known to go through three shields in that time. Things just disintegrate.'

One wondered why axes did not cleave through heads and that sort of thing, but Poke says they train together five hours a week until they know every predictable movement.

They are not skilled enough yet to take on jousting and tilting, but he says this is done by Vikings in England, where they are full-time professionals and earn $300 a week.

At the opening of War Games House the Vikings turned on a ferocious battle with axes and spears. Even the members of the War Games Association, who every weekend leave anything up to 50,000 dead on the battlefield, were impressed.
