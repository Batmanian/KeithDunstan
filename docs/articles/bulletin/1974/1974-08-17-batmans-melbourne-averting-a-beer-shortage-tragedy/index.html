Averting a beer shortage tragedy

5 min read.

First published 17 Aug 1974

A transport strike leaves Melbourne without beer, prompting Keith Dunstan to relate his experiences with home brewing — keeping the fermenter warm through Melbourne's coldest winter in a decade by dismantling his bedside lamp, trying the hot water service, then pressing an electric blanket into service outdoors — while Sydney beer crosses the border by night in closed trucks at twice the normal barrel price.

THE TRANSPORT strike has left us without beer now for several weeks. An official of the Australian Hotels Association has declared there is not a drop of beer in the entire State. Now I always thought that beer was as essential to the Australian character as mortar to bricks and if ever it was withdrawn for any length of time, awesome and terrible things would happen.

Indeed Cyril Pearl in that noble work Beer, Glorious Beer, warned of this. He said beer was the essential religion and apart from the uprising at Eureka the only true Australian revolution had been over beer. It took place in Darwin towards the end of 1918. There was a rise in the price of bottled beer from 1/6 to 1/9 a bottle. More than a 1,000 citizens marched on Government House. They kept the administrator, Dr Gilruth, for several months in a state of siege until he was forced to flee by steamer.

Curiously, we beer drinkers are remaining strangely calm here in Melbourne. I was in Young and Jackson's temple the other afternoon, as I often am, and the citizens were dolefully drinking brandy and dry gingers with no thought of marching on Parliament House.

Some pubs in Richmond, Caulfield, Chadstone and Brighton have kept going with Sydney beer. One licensee told me that the black market in Sydney beer has now become tremendous. 'I pay almost twice for a barrel what I used to pay before the Carlton brewery closed its doors. It is coming across the border in closed trucks by night. It is just like prohibition in the great days of the U.S.'

However I think there is more behind the saving of our character than this. Many of us have returned to home brewing, knowing that no longer can we rely on continuity of the famous product. I would like to tell you a little of the problems.

Here in Melbourne we have endured the coldest winter in a decade. For example a few mornings ago the temperature reached freezing point. Now this is terribly difficult for brewing. You do your brewing in a 5 or 10 gallon plastic fermenter with a little plastic gas escape valve on top. This bubbles. Your yeast won't work unless you have your brew at 70 to 75 degrees Fahrenheit. What a problem in August.

First I did what the books recommend. You dismantle your bedside lamp. Put the fermenter on bricks, then place the lighted bulb under the fermenter. That's supposed to provide enough heat to get everything working. No go.

Next I put the fermenter in with the hot water service. Which is actually right beside our living room. Soon the fermenter was bubbling. Maybe you have heard of that famous line 'Happiness is a bubbling beer fermenter.' No? Well, very soon that glorious smell of beer and hops, identical to that which pervades the better brewery areas of Carlton and Abbotsford, began to pervade our living room and house.

It always puzzles me how women don't appreciate that this smell has a lovely quality far superior to anything ever created by Chanel, Dior or whoever makes those French smells.

But the order was, more or less, to get that beer thing out of the living room. How else to provide the heat? Suddenly there was an inspiration. The electric blanket had to come off the bed. So the fermenter went outside. The electric blanket on a long lead was wrapped around the fermenter, turned up to 'two' and then to 'three' and lo, at last the dear thing started to bubble again.

You must appreciate that you need an extraordinarily understanding wife if you keep doing that sort of thing. The fermentation process can take four to seven days and after bottling you drink the beer in four weeks. In times of awful crisis you can drink your beer after a fortnight, but it doesn't taste so good.

Supplies for brewery, malt, yeast, hops, raw sugar — all these can be had at your local food shop. The health food shop will even give you a recipe. Don't ever buy the prepared do-it-yourself kits. I always get a feeling of great satisfaction when I go into the health food shop, surrounded by yoghurt, wheat germ, dried apricot, herbal tea and such. I mean, if the health shops sell all the beer-making ingredients, then surely it must have marvellous medicinal qualities.

We do our bottling out in the garden in a disused aviary. We call this our bottling hall and we usually bottle 40 to 80 bottles with every brew. We are very honest about it. We never, as a point of honour, leave the old Courage or Carlton labels on the bottles.

I had one great idea when I finally dispensed with Churchill. The children had found its noble proportions too heavy to push. But I thought that if I bought a GTK Superchief sports model they would want to push this thing eagerly around the garden every Sunday.

Actually I should say that crisis during the summer was equally terrible. In Melbourne it can also get very hot and right at beer-making time the temperature hits 103 degrees. Once again, you must understand, if the brew gets much above 75 degrees, then the yeast expires.

There was only one thing to do, take everything out of the refrigerator, plus all the shelves and put the fermenter in there. Temperature came down to the right level and bless its heart, the dear thing started to bubble again.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Averting a beer shortage tragedy, The Bulletin, 17 August 1974. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
