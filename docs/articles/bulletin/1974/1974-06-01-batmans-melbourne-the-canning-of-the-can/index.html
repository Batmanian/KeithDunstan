The canning of the can

4 min read.

First published 01 Jun 1974

A rash of can-throwing incidents at VFL matches, including a full can hitting a Richmond player and a brawl involving players, spectators and officials at Essendon, has the Premier, the Minister for Sport and the Chief Commissioner of Police all calling for a ban on beer cans at football and cricket grounds, but Keith Dunstan predicts the entirely Australian phenomenon of canspiracy, cantraband and canfusion.

A DEEP CRISIS has hit Melbourne sport. It now seems certain that the beer can will be banned from all football and cricket grounds. You must appreciate that the can is an emotional and spiritual thing, almost an extension of the right hand. In Melbourne as soon as a baby gives up his dummy he learns to take a rip-top can instead.

Regrettably the Melbourne Cricket Club does not publish figures for the number of cans it collects after matches. It has become a little embarrassed about this. However we do know that after the great cricket one during the fifth Test against the West Indies on February 11, 1961, the MCG collected the same number of cans — 48,000 cans for each man, woman and child. In those days all beer cans were still tinned steel and old hands used to use them as mini grandstands. Here they show great dexterity and balance. They put four cans on the ground, then another four on top, and eureka, here is a nice little platform from which to watch a game. Furthermore the drinking of one's grandstand usually lasts at least until half time.

But I said the can was an emotional and spiritual thing. We have always had a magnificent tradition of can throwing. Enthusiasts use them not so much against each other, but to express their artistic appreciation of events on the field. Can throwing is an art form. Full cans are thrown at the goalposts, into the river, at umpires and sometimes at police. When the passions are really stirred aluminium cans aid the aerodynamics.

But there have been occasions lately the rage has been so extreme spectators have thrown full cans of beer. This in Melbourne is the ultimate in sporting violence.

At a Carlton-Richmond game earlier this year a player, Vin Waite, was hit on the head. Then at the Richmond-Essendon match at Essendon there was an all-in altercation which involved both players, spectators and officials. The Richmond player Brian Roberts had his nose broken by Essendon physical fitness Jim Bradley, received a broken jaw. But as Roberts was being helped from the ground he was hit by a falling can. By the time he was taken to hospital his eye was so swollen he could hardly see out of it and he was very lucky not to be half blinded.

As I write this the Premier, Mr Hamer, the Minister for Sport, Mr Dixon, and Chief Commissioner of Police Mr Jackson have all called for the banning of the can.

The Victorian Football League is meeting to decide what to do next. This has come up many times and they have always drawn back before making the awful step of the full can ban. For one thing the can selling concessions are immensely profitable and are on long term contracts. For another they realise the mystic importance of the can, that the Australian bereft of this article on a Saturday might be lost.

My belief is that a total ban will be impossible. Australians have always been notoriously ingenious when it comes to unpopular laws. This will lead to canspiracy, cantraband, canfusion, and it will take the entire efforts of the canstabulary to check it.

At the MCG they will have to set up can checks at every gate, similar to the checks they have at Tel Aviv airport and elsewhere. They might even have to instal those special machines that go 'beep beep' when they detect hidden metal.

Australian industry is rising to the crisis. ICI already is trying to talk the breweries into marketing the 'soft pack' the polythene division has come up with, a quarter litre polyester sachet, which looks like a large tobacco pouch. You just pull a tab on the top and you can get the beer into yourself with all the speed of the rip-top can.

Mr David Ingram of ICI told your correspondent that Watneys have used them in England and also they are used by many soft drink firms. Regrettably they are no good for standing on, but apparently they have these qualities, they don't rust, they don't break, they burn easily and they are utterly lousy aerodynamically. A footballer hit by a sachet either full or empty is unlikely to be hurt.

There is one sad aspect to this. Say they do succeed in banning the can, this will be the end of one of the most evocative of Australian sounds — the equivalent of God Save the Queen being played on a gum leaf — that of an empty beer can gently rolling down the steps of the MCG. The thought of that CLANK, CLANK has reduced expatriate Australians to gushing tears of nostalgia in Kangaroo Valley.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The canning of the can, The Bulletin, 1 June 1974. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
