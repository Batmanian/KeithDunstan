They stayed away in droves

4 min read.

First published 05 Oct 1974

The Anti-Football League sells 75,000 non-tickets at 20 cents each — including seats in the all-male members' stand and Bay Nine — raises record profits, and stages a coffin burial at sea off Williamstown with Cyril Pearl and Royce Hart's football, though the football escapes the coffin in a gale and skids toward Port Phillip Heads; the Wilkie Medal for taking football off the air goes to Channel Nine's Leon Hill.

IT IS INTERESTING to be able to report that while many other organisations are failing the Anti-Football League in Victoria has just finished a bumper season.

More people were keen to stay away from its non-match than before and the league declared record profits. The idea of an anti-football, non-grand final was first organised in 1972.

The league obtained the ground plan of the Melbourne Cricket Ground and printed its own grand-final tickets each with its correct seat number. Some people chose to stay away from seats behind the goal posts, some from the notorious, high-drinking Bay Nine, but it was remarkable the number of women who specified non-seats in the all-male preserve of the members' stand.

The non-seats were 20c each and were good value, really, for they entitled the purchaser to stay away from any VFL match merely by presentation at the gate. There were lucky seat numbers with prizes attached. One of the prizes was that the winner was Cyril Pearl, of course, in the best and fairest manner.

Mr Pearl agreed to fly to Melbourne for the ceremony on condition that, in the middle of historic Melbourne Cricket Ground, he be allowed to burn a football that had been kicked by the legendary Roy Cazaly.

Such a sacred relic was impossible to find, but the AFL did seek out for Mr Pearl a ball that had been kicked by the captain of Richmond, Royce Hart. Your correspondent was present on the vital day. The ball was very reluctant to burn and it was eerie in the vast MCG with 60,000 people who weren't there. Indeed, there was a sense of sacrilege about burning the football. It was like exposing oneself before the altar of St Paul's.

The rush for non-tickets during the 1974 season was nigh unbelievable. Berry Street Babies' Home, which was handling the sale, could hardly cope, and at one stage the Sun News Pictorial printed a photograph of a non-queue outside the MCG which didn't extend right across Richmond Park.

By the season's end 75,000 people had bought non-seats and spent $16,000. The ceremony, set for September 26, was the burial of football at sea. Batman sought K. Dunstan, and asked him what was conducting this macabre, tasteless exercise.

Dunstan said the Victorian Football League was in a state of serious decline. Attendances were down throughout the season and history had been made when they couldn't even fill their seats for the finals.

Waving a strange AFL banner, he said: 'Do you know that Collingwood, of all teams, even returned 3500 seats it could not sell for the elimination final? Well, that settled it for us — we needed a ceremony that would symbolise the appalling state of affairs.'

Regular funeral directors, terrified of reprisals, were not keen to be associated with the ceremony. Eventually the AFL hired a 1940 two-ton Dodge hearse from Mr Ian Vinnicombe, of Hawthorn. He had bought it for $150 and been using it to transport a pop group. Inside this very stately dignified machine the AFL placed a coffin.

The theory was that the coffin would be committed to the deep at 11.30 am by an ocean-going vessel off Williamstown, but the Gods of football would not allow it. Terrible gales were howling across the water and no captain would put to sea.

Not to be thwarted, members of the AFL dressed in black-tailed mourning outfits complete with top hats and black crepe, bore their coffin to the end of the pier. It was all done most carefully: holes, both top and below to ensure a speedy descent under water. After suitable funeral oratory the pallbearers heaved the coffin waist-high into the sea. It tilted on its side in the water and started to subside. Then a very curious thing happened. The lid blew off in the gale and out popped the football. It skidded across the water and at incredible speed made for Port Phillip Heads as if these were the great goal posts in the sky.

The awesome symbolism was not lost on the ghastly pallbearers and they doffed their black hats.

After this ceremony the 1974 Wilkie Medal winner, Mr Leon Hill, drew the raffle. Mr Hill, general manager of Melbourne's Channel Nine, won the award for taking off football replays.

He was very kind. He made an offer to the AFL in perpetuity . . . the use of the GTV9 box at VFL Park, Waverley — in February.
