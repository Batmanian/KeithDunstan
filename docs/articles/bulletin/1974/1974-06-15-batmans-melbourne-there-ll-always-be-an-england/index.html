There'll always be an England...

4 min read.

First published 15 Jun 1974

Russell Braddon's television declaration that Anglo-Australian friendship is as dead as the Anzacs prompts Keith Dunstan to survey the many ways England remains inescapable in Melbourne — from the new State governor's Gilbert-and-Sullivan ceremony to Rolls-Royces in Toorak, Oxon-accredited teachers and an English-born headmaster at Geelong Grammar — concluding that the love-hate relationship has simply continued in the same style for 150 years.

HELLO, HERE we are again. I remember a piece by John Pilger which appeared in London's Daily Mirror on November 9, 1967. The headline read 'So long, Poms, Go Jump In The Channel.' The article contained such lines as these: 'No one speaks of Britain, the Old Dart, any more, except to pity her money problems and lament her new immigration laws, which make a working holiday over there not the simple joy it once was.

'Indeed, if questioned at all about her, Mr and Mrs Australia, all petrified with comfort in their suburban edifice, will simply say, "She's had it, mate," and open another can of beer.'

To illustrate this article Mr Pilger had an ocker bloke thumbing his nose. He was using his other hand to hold a jug of beer and he had a Stars and Stripes flag on his Jackie Howe T-shirt.

Now Russell Braddon is declaring the doom of our mateship with the Poms. Some nights ago I watched him on the television in a show called One Pair of Eyes. There was lots of moody stuff — Braddon wandering through the gum trees, Braddon going through customs, Braddon walking through graveyards, and the message was always the same: 'Anglo-Australian friendship is as dead as the Anzacs in their graves.'

He also gave us the agonised line: 'Today it is inconceivable that any Australian would consider making any sacrifice for Britain.'

Your correspondent, with another pair of eyes, would suggest that he is shedding his tears about 30 years too soon. If the proper crisis came along you couldn't stop the mothers of Moonee Ponds soldering up the Fat-for-Britain tins all over again.

From where I sit I find myself still stifled with the heritage of the Old Dart. We have just sworn in a State governor, not an Englishman admittedly. But the ceremony was so utterly traditional the show could have been produced by Gilbert and Sullivan.

Football pools on Australian Rules football incredibly were a failure in Melbourne, but the Victorian Premier, Mr Hamer, has just announced a grand plan to go in with Vernon football pools of London. So using soccer teams in England and soccer teams in Melbourne will have a beautiful hands-across-the-sea arrangement with betting on soccer all the year round. Local citizens will participate and expect half a million dollars.

In Toorak and South Yarra Rolls-Royces are still as common as FJ Holdens. They still give one that extra touch of class.

No, you can't get away from the Poms. It's still nice for the ABC news reader to have a Pom accent. It's still nice for teachers at our public schools to have Oxon or Camb after their names, and noble establishments like Geelong Grammar insist even in the 1970s in having an English-born headmaster. They have never had any other.

Oh, I can give you countless examples. You just can't shake off England so easily. The feeling is based on a curious love-hate relationship, an inter-colonial generation gap, the hate rejection the 17-year-old gets for his parents. Except that this one has gone on in the same style for 150 years.

Punch several years back suggested that an exchange of insults was actually the best basis for a good relationship. 'Who wants friends?' it said. 'We want someone to fight with. Friendship is a poor substitute for the acrimony that makes up a good parent-child relationship.'

So the Poms are always sending out their star feature writers and their brilliant television crews specially to insult us.

For example, one remembers with affection a recent visit to Melbourne by Mr Clement Freud. 'No wonder Biggs used this place as a hideout,' said he. 'Melbourne is an ideal city for an escaped convict. There is a general air of incompetence.'

Yes, and every couple of years we play Test cricket matches against the English. These are played with a diabolical intensity. There is always a special satisfaction in thrashing the Poms.

Then there's the joke about the board that is supposed to be outside every Australian lion park: 'Cars $1; cars with senior citizens, 50 cents; Poms on bicycles, free.' Then the story goes on. A Pom did ride his bike into one of the lion parks. The lions rejected the Pom but ate the bicycle.

I mean, when you get this sort of counter-play between parent and child, obviously they are very fond of each other.
