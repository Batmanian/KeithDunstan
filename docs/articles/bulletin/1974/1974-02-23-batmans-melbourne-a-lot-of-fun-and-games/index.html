A lot of fun and games

6 min read.

First published 23 Feb 1974

A new Monopoly set for the holidays prompts Keith Dunstan to survey the booming market for topical board games, from Squatter and Watergate to imagined future games about the petrol crisis, corrupt doctors, Black Power and Australian bank robbers on the run in Rio.

WE BOUGHT a new Monopoly set during the holidays. As a keep-em-quiet line it was superb. There were times when the family did not ask for real money for stretches as long as five hours.

I first began to play Monopoly around 1938. At that time you could buy Whitechapel for 140 pounds, the electricity company for 150 pounds, Kings Cross station for a mere 200 pounds and your salary was 200 pounds every time you passed the corner. It cost you 50 pounds a time to put up a house and a hotel was 750 pounds. The most expensive piece of real estate on the whole block was Mayfair. We used to think it an outrageous price. You really had to be a millionaire to buy Mayfair and put up a few houses.

Monopoly was a product of the depression of the thirties, a dreams-of-glory game of greed, designed for people without money. Note that the pounds are now dollars, so that property is actually twice as cheap. It is the one secure thing in the whole inflationary scene. But according to a journal titled Games and Puzzles the big story of the moment is a new passion for topical games, and actually Monopoly, although still a top seller, is old hat.

Our local toy shop is now selling an art auctions game, called Masterpieces. You take on the role of a big-time art dealer and you pretend you are buying Blue Poles for the gallery in Canberra for $2 million or something like that. The blurb on the box reads: 'Your favorite Rembrandt is on the block and you are bidding for it against a dazzling array of eccentric art speculators. Baron von Oberlitzer, the German industrialist, signals a bid of $500,000. Elton Whitehall instantly ups it to $750,000. Should you go higher? What if it's a worthless forgery? You must decide quickly, but you'll never know for sure unless you outbid the competition.'

There's another game called Life. You start off with $2000 and a motor car. The entire game depends on your grabbing as much materialistic wealth as you can lay your hands on — stocks and shares, houses, country property. There is $7 million worth to be had altogether and the object is to reduce your opponent to dole-seeking poverty. Presumably the man who ends up owning everything is the winner. There is even a business game called Ulcers. Players have to run a business, hire and fire staff, make raids on other firms, indulge in take-overs.

There is a game called Squatter. You buy an unimproved sheep station and this you must irrigate, sow with seed, stock with sheep, and so on. You have beaut fun coping with drought, fires, floods and fluctuating wool prices.

Also on the market is the game Mining. You go hunting for minerals in Western Australia and elsewhere. I'm not sure what happens when your business fails. Presumably you end up in a luxury hotel in Rio.

There are all sorts of war games and here is the real chance to let loose one's egomanic tendencies. You can buy the Battle of Waterloo. The cover of the box says: 'The engagement that spelled doom to an empire. Here you are, Wellington or Napoleon; squaring away on the most famous battlefield in all history to decide the destiny of France.'

There's an Australian game out called The African Campaign and this time you are invited 'to match your skill with Rommel or command the Afrika Korps as you relive history.' But there are lots of others. Of course, Watergate is on sale. The blurb says: 'Nobody in this game wins. There are just losers. If you are caught covering up or trying to deceive you are given penalty points. Cover-up and deception not only add zest to the game but is the only sure way to come out on top.'

In the U.S. there is also a sex game. You play for dream prizes such as three nights with Raquel Welch or two nights with Marilyn Monroe.

According to Games and Puzzles, pollution games are all the rage in England and the U.S. There is Dirty Water. Each player has a lake which he must keep ecologically sound, fighting off the fearful polluting forces of industry. Smog is another and the players take the roles of air quality managers.

There's Dealers' Choice. You start off with 20,000 dollars and four used cars. These are just a few of the games. Think what others are sure to come on the market. A natural would be Petrol. The Arabs have cornered all the petrol. You have managed to hoard 500 gallons in your own garage. Can you sell it off at a black market $5 a gallon before the police get to you, before it all blows up?

A sure winner would be Doctors ... 'You are Mr Hayden in a tense battle with the AMA. The doctors have given $5 million to a public relations firm and a mighty campaign has been launched against your treasured medical scheme.'

Or here's a beauty, Backlash ... 'You have been given $10 million to assist an Aboriginal population in a far northern State. These people have been oppressed and broken down for 200 years. It is your job to distribute this money, put them into viable industries and occupations. The local population is bitterly resentful, calling them wasters and bludgers. All sorts of traps are on the board, etc.'

Or we could have To Let... 'You are a multi-million dollar speculator. You specialise in building 30-storey towers in Sydney and Melbourne that nobody needs. They are empty but you are speculating against inflation, playing a futures game. Will your capital hold out against possible government legislation, against possible financial collapse, or will the inflationary boom help you to make a million?'

The best game, I think, might be... 'You are a bank robber who has skipped to Rio de Janeiro. You are living with three girlfriends. The London police are battering at one door, your wife is battering at the other. Will you accept the $100,000 from the Daily Express, the $150,000 from the Daily Mail or the fortune from the News of the World?' I'd like to play that one.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: A lot of fun and games, The Bulletin, 23 February 1974. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
