Hooked on the blurbs

5 min read.

First published 29 Jun 1974

A projectionists' strike closes Melbourne's cinemas for weeks, sending Keith Dunstan — a self-confessed 'advertiolic' — back through Thursday-night newspaper archives from February to May, tracing the evolution of movie advertising from Theda Bara in 1916 through covered-wagon spectaculars of the 1920s to the R-rated massage parlour and nymphomaniac blurbs of 1974, concluding the ads are more exciting than the films themselves.

IT HAS BEEN a very hard time for us in Melbourne. The cinemas have been closed because of a projectionists' strike. They kindly allowed the film festival to continue, but that was only for the lucky movie buffs who happened to have tickets. Many people suffered cruel withdrawal symptoms. Imagine weeks without a good exorcism, or vomit scene. Imagine weeks without having seen one decent copulation, or even a decent act of violence.

Even worse, we had weeks without any movie ads. To be perfectly honest, rarely do I see a movie. I am instead an advertiolic. That is, I have an incurable passion for movie advertisements. I can't stop reading them, poring over them. You see a movie hardly ever lives up to the promise of its ads. So who gets disappointed? The ads have a marvellous quality. They are infinitely more satisfying, exciting, passion-arousing. My complaint has become worse since the advent of R certificate movies.

So what have I done during the strike? I went down to the newspaper backdates department and bought all the issues for the Thursday nights from February through to May. The true advertiolic always goes for Thursday night's paper. It is here that you get the biggest, the most luscious, the most sensuous spreads.

For example I am looking now at Thursday night, April 11. There are about three square feet of newsprint devoted to a movie called Massage Parlor, and the message says: 'Exposed — now you can see what really happens in some massage parlors. Your ultimate sensual dream.' Along with this there is a large picture of a nude, fine healthy black, and a nude fine healthy white girl rubbing down a white chap in a massage parlour. You get racism in movie ads.

A few weeks earlier we had I Am a Nymphomaniac and the blurb said: 'What I am about to tell you is the truth, the whole truth, the naked truth as they say. You're going to witness my confession as certain priests get to hear . . . Nymphomania is a disturbance of the sexual instinct leading to psychic disabilities. It results in abnormal sexual behaviour and an unbridled appetite for enjoyment which is never satisfied and drives you to make love again and again and more and more. So you find yourself in a kind of sexual escalation which is a genuine inferno of pleasure. That's the story of my life. Now today I want to remember and tell all.'

Then we had Swedish Wildcats . . . 'Pampered pussies of pleasure. Are you man enough to take on the Swedish wildcats.' That was showing with Snow White and the Seven Perverts. We had Venus in Furs . . . 'The year's most scorching piece of erotica' with a superb picture of big-chested Laura Antonelli, almost nude, wielding a whip. That's the sort of thing to make you feel good when you're going home in the 5.30 pm express. Oh, I could give you many more like the Language of Love . . . 'Many people including a cabinet minister walked out of this film. If you're happy to allow others to make important decisions for you don't bother to see Language of Love. Just because this film is definitely an explicit sex movie about real sex, it's interesting to discover that it's also highly entertaining and informative.'

See what I mean? You can get all the excitement you need from the ads alone.

As an ardent advertiolic I have made a serious study of movie ads and you mustn't think that the excitement only came with R movies.

Take 1916. In those days the newspapers did not use fancy blocks, but in January there was the release of Theda Bara in Carmen. She was appearing at 'the temple of motion picture art.' The advertisement filled an entire column in the Argus and they had 'Theda Bara — the most beautiful woman in the world' repeated 400 times down the column in block capitals . . . the reviews said: 'Theda Bara is a superb, gorgeous, coquettish woman, who is dangerously alluring and absolutely reckless as to how she works her will.'

By the 1920s we had moved in to the era of the big spreads and the Thursday night Herald already was getting some coups. I liked the ad for The Covered Wagon, October 1923. There were covered wagons, arrows, tomahawks, whooping Indians, as far as the eye could see. Here we had the blurb: 'The picture that has been cheered and acclaimed by over 100,000 . . . the picture that has been discussed on every hand for the past four weeks. Nothing in your wildest imagination can thrill you one jot as much as The Covered Wagon. Its love story is the most beautiful thing that has ever graced the silver screen. The buffalo hunt, the prairie fire, the plunging of maddened bullocks, terrified horses and the seething, shouting, swearing mass of humanity into the raging torrent is a sight that will lift you clean out of your seats with pulse-tingling excitement.'

In the 1930s they eased down on the mighty spectacles and the beautiful, warm-hearted love story took over. We preferred stories of sacrifice, superb self-denial. For example The Grand Canary was very big in January, 1935. Warner Baxter and Madge Evans were the stars and the blurb said: 'They were held apart by the very honor that the world accused them of violating.'

Another big one was Prestige with Ann Harding, Adolph Menjou and Melvyn Douglas . . . 'A lone white woman 600 miles from civilisation, surrounded by jungle terrors trying desperately to uphold the prestige of her race.'

But do you see what they mean? These ads are so good there's no need for the movies. Indeed, instead of running the old movies night after night on the telly I think they should run the ads. You'd be lifted clean out of your seat with pulse-tingling excitement.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Hooked on the blurbs, The Bulletin, 29 June 1974. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
