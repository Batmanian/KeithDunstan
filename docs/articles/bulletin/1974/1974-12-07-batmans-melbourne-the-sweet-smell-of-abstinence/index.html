The sweet smell of abstinence

5 min read.

First published 07 Dec 1974

The Quit Club's first public meeting draws 200 reformed nicotine addicts to Melbourne's State Film Centre, where members with purple-tongue novice badges share cold turkey strategies, group telephone networks and minute-by-minute watch-gazing techniques, while Dr Nigel Gray of the Anti Cancer Council screens banned anti-smoking commercials including Warren Mitchell's Alf Garnett coughing fit, Freddie Parslow smoking more ruggedly than John Wayne before a lung X-ray, and the now-banned 'Till Death Us Do Part' poster.

LAST WEEK we had our first public meeting of the Quit Club, an organisation designed to help people give up smoking. Over 200 reformed nicotine addicts gathered in Melbourne's State Film Centre and related to each other their experiences.

One man described what it was like to be liberated: 'After a while everything comes back to you. You smell your kids' BO once again. You smell your wife's perfume. You pick up cooking smells when you're walking down the street.'

Another gentleman said he began smoking 55 years ago at Carnegie State School. Using a slide rule he estimated that smoking 40 cigarettes a day he had now puffed his way through 64,240,000 millimetres of tobacco. He had burned the equivalent of the Cheops pyramid or two Leaning Towers of Pisa.

A character from Wye River said: 'I found giving up easy. As a matter of fact I don't even think about it. I gave up three weeks, two days, three hours, 45 minutes ago.'

An advertising man, of all people, said: 'I firmly believe the group system is the answer. Half a dozen of us at the office put a notice on the board that we were pledging to quit that night, giving names, conditions and so forth.

'After that we worked it like Alcoholics Anonymous. Whenever we felt the strain was becoming too much we would ring someone in the group and he would talk you out of lighting up. It worked, really. None of us have started smoking again.'

A man with a strong North Country accent said: 'I'd look at my watch every time I'd start to weaken. I'd watch the second hand until it had gone right round a minute. By that time the urge had usually passed.'

An ex-smoker admitted to a nicotine past of 31 years. He said he cured himself by a positive mental approach. Every time he thought of having a cigarette he muttered his loathing, his hatred, he said to himself how much he despised cigarettes.

The Quit Club was formed back in September under the auspices of the Anti Cancer Council with Sir Macfarlane Burnet as president and patron. Club members wear little gold badges in the shape of a Q. The tail of the Q is a tongue and the whole badge symbolises a suffering smoker in the last stages of addiction. The novice, the probationary battler gets a badge with a livid purple tongue. After giving up for one month he switches to a green badge, six months' liberation rates a yellow badge, 12 months brown, and after five years, red, symbolising that the member is free, but cured of the terrible pangs.

The idea of the badge is that other people will recognise it, and, particularly if he is a purple tongue novice, treat him with special sympathy. They will realise that here is a man trying to fight a dangerous, habit-forming narcotic and they will not offer him a cigarette.

At the meeting Dr Nigel Gray, director of the Anti Cancer Council, told of methods of quitting. He said you could do it progressively; switch from a high tar content cigarette to a low tar. This helped reduce the urge. Continue this way, say for a month, reducing your consumption to 10 a day or less, then stop. Alternatively you could try the Cold Turkey method. Stop, literally altogether, then battle it out. Although he did admit some people, particularly those in high tension jobs, found it much harder than others.

Every Q Club member gets a do-it-yourself-give-up-smoking-kit and at the meeting they distributed a fascinating tip sheet for quitting smokers. For example they had the tip: 'Before you quit smoking try wrapping your cigarettes with a sheet of paper like a Christmas present. Every time you want a cigarette unwrap the pack and write down what you are doing, how you feel and how important this cigarette is to you.' Other helpful hints:

'Put away your ash trays or fill them with objects so they cannot be used for ashes. Plant flowers in them or fill them with walnuts. The latter will give you something to do with your hands.'

'When you feel irritable or tense, shut your eyes and count backward from 10 to zero as you imagine yourself descending a flight of stairs or imagine you are looking at the horizon as the sun sets.'

At the meeting we saw that ex-smokers were a persecuted minority, illustrated by a series of anti-smoking commercials, which for various reasons had been banned from television.

The most spectacular showed Freddie Parslow, the actor, looking as rugged as all hell, big orchestra, famous theme. He smokes a cigarette even better than John Wayne. Then they flash to a lung X-Ray with the message: COME TO CANCER COUNTRY.

Warren Mitchell, alias Alf Garnett, was pretty good. He turns on the most brilliant display of TV coughing in history, turning purple in the face. Then he reaches for a cigarette: 'Ahhhhh, I needed that.'

We also had Miriam Karlin demonstrating the advantages of various cigarette packs. Finally you get the flip top box, which is a coffin.

The bonus for the evening was something special. Everyone received a free copy of the now prized hard-to-get anti smoking poster. You will remember this poster was banned by the Health Minister in Tasmania, Mr Farquhar, who described it as a Playboy centrefold.

It depicted a naked man and woman in each other's arms. They are embracing in the body of a burning cigarette with the title Till Death Us Do Part.

The get-together was such a success the Quit Club is planning another public meeting in four weeks' time. Club headquarters is 412 Albert St, East Melbourne.
