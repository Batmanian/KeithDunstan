Breaking the grass barrier

5 min read.

First published 23 Nov 1974

After 25 years of pushing Churchill, a solid steel-wheeled 1949 hand mower resembling a 28-ton tank, through record Melbourne spring grass, Keith Dunstan surrenders and buys a bright orange two-stroke motor mower, entering a world of prestige trade-ins, auto-drive luxury, ride-on models with twin headlights, and motor-mower mileage tests, before forming the Veteran Lawn Mower Club of Australia to rescue Churchill before he reaches $1000 at Christie's.

THE TRUTH is, I feel humiliated. After 20 years we have succumbed. We have bought our first motor mower, a vile thing in bright orange that sounds like 15 trail bikes rolled into one.

Yes, you are forced to conform. I was left out of the conversation with other motor-mower drivers. I couldn't rush next-door to borrow cups of four-stroke or two-stroke fuel. I knew nothing about automatic height adjusters, recoil starters, vacuum actions and such.

Now our mower was solid with a dignified Victorian air about it. We called it Churchill, not out of respect for the late Sir Winston, but because it resembled the famous Churchill tank, which weighed nigh on 28 tons. It was not one of those absurd machines with rubber tyres. The wheels were of solid steel and covered with lovely tracery designs like the old-time balconies.

Churchill was 25 years old, one owner, one driver, immac. condit. and it had always been driven at cautious speeds. This spring was a record grass season in Melbourne and Churchill couldn't quite make it through the nature strip. The humiliating thing was that our neighbours continually took pity on us and offered to lend us their new season motor mowers.

It was then that we succumbed.

You must appreciate that motor mowers are now a prestige item. People judge you by the size, quality and year of the motor mower they see you pushing along your nature strip.

You get offered trade-ins on your old motor mower and we have almost reached the stage that there are used-motor-mower lots along the Princes Highway all inhabited by beaming salesmen urging you to UPDATE.

It is not good enough to have one motor mower any more. Many have stepped up to become two-mower families.

You will have noticed that in the past month newspaper and gardening magazines have been filled with advertisements for the new models. Some journals even have motor-mower motoring writers and you get accounts of exciting grass tests.

The mowers have inspiring names. One notices models called the Impala, the Corvette, the Mustang, the Supa-Swift, the Galaxy and the GTK. The GTK comes in glowing multi-colors and one can only presume that the true enthusiast would paint two broad black stripes down the middle of his grass catcher. They offer you 'super easy start' . . . 'wide high-swept chute' . . . 'uncloggable jet power with exclusive zip starter.' They suggest too 'single knob height control' . . . 'silent nylon gears' . . . 'extra large wheels for easy turning' and you see yourself handling the GTK, making all sorts of marvellous sliding turns at the bottom of the garden.

This year you must have the zip starter . . . 'relief valve with no hard pulling against the compression' and also, if you are a rotary man, you must have 'auto-drive.'

Auto-drive means that no longer do you have to push the darned thing. It moves along under its own power. There will be great loss of prestige in 1974-75 if you are seen actually pushing a mower.

But also you can step up into the Rolls-Royce super luxury class. The ultimate is to have the motor mower which you can ride and steer. They are like a small tractor with lovely comfy seats, twin head lights so that you can mow in the dark, 12 months' guarantee or a 1000 lawn miles, all that sort of thing.

About 77 percent of motor mowers sold are two-stroke. These are most brilliantly eloquent. The two-stroke that we bought last week can be heard by anyone of sound hearing four blocks away.

The Victorian Government has threatened to introduce legislation this session to force manufacturers to make quieter mowers.

The acres of newspaper advertising given to motor mowers this spring have barely mentioned noise. I called at one motor-mower centre in the midst of a 'motor-mower outdoorium' and inquired about their silent-mower range. There was no response. You would think that there would be one mower with the slogan that the only noise you hear at five knots is the deep breathing of the blonde pushing it, but no. Apparently noise levels are a poor factor in the selling of motor-mowers.

I had one great idea when I finally dispensed with Churchill. The children had found its noble proportions too heavy to push. But I thought that if I bought a GTK Superchief sports model they would want to push this thing eagerly around the garden every Sunday.

And they did too, the very first day I bought it. They thought it marvellous fun. However they have not touched it again.

Now I have a new idea. I am in the process of forming the Veteran Lawn Mower Club of Australia. Our object will be to find ancient lawn-mowers like Churchill. We will spend countless hours on restoring them to their original pristine beauty.

Churchill eventually will attain a reserve price of at least $1000 at Christie's. When the children discover the old thing is a nostalgic, priceless work of art, they will want to take him out of the garage to mow the lawn, just to show off to the neighbours.
