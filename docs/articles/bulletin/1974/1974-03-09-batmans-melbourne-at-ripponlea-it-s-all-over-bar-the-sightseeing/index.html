At Ripponlea it's all over bar the sightseeing

5 min read.

First published 09 Mar 1974

After 11 years of legal battles between the National Trust and the Commonwealth over Ripponlea estate in Elsternwick, the mansion and its 13 acres of Guilfoyle-designed gardens are finally open to the public, drawing crowds that produced a traffic jam half a mile long on the first weekend.

THE BATTLE of Ripponlea is over. At last it has been opened as the richest jewel of the Victorian National Trust, but the awful battle has taken 11 years to finish. Now, Ripponlea consists of 13½ astounding acres at Hotham Street, Elsternwick. The mansion has 33 rooms, built by Sir Frederick Sargood, between the late eighteen-sixties and the early eighteen-eighties at a time when Melbourne was stinking rich. Sargood was the son of an English draper and his wife, Emma, the daughter of Thomas Rippon, chief cashier of the Bank of England. Sargood made a big fortune out of the softgoods business.

The house is vast and solidly constructed. You have to admire its elegance in that there is even a servant's bell-push in the ballroom lavatory, but dare one say, that as a work of art it is a shocker. Some have suggested that the architecture is Florentine, some Spanish mission, some Georgian, but more accurately I think you would have to call it Gee Whiz, Victorian revival gone gorgeous.

The glory of the place is the garden. It is laid out in the grand classic William Guilfoyle tradition of the Botanic Gardens and it is very beautiful indeed. It is unlikely that anyone of this age will ever leave something equally beautiful to a future generation.

But let us return to the battle. In 1963 Ripponlea and its garden was owned by Mrs Louisa Jones, widow of Timothy Jones, a former barrister. She had begun negotiations with the National Trust. However ABC's Channel 2 had blossomed on a corner of the old estate, and it had the belief that as it expanded more chunks of the estate would come its way.

In April, 1963, the Postmaster-General, Mr Davidson, moved. He compulsorily acquired 4.1 acres on behalf of the Commonwealth. Most of us have forgotten the splendid sense of outrage that we had at the time. The letters to the newspapers were remarkable. I always treasured this one: 'The Commonwealth seems to have greater powers than the ancient kings of Israel, for even Ahab took Naboth's refusal to sell his vineyard as final until his wife found a means of getting rid of Naboth first.'

Well, Mrs Jones offered Ripponlea and all its work as a straight gift to the National Trust and for a time the ABC seemed to be foiled. Mr Rodney Davidson, chairman of the trust, then moved quickly. He opened Ripponlea to the public on a Sunday afternoon and they called a good old-fashioned indignation meeting. The ABC in its 7 o'clock news primly put the crowd at 3000 people and gave it no TV coverage whatever. The figure should have been more like 30,000 and at times the invasion through the front gate was reminiscent of the retreat toward Dunkirk. There was a traffic jam all along Hotham Street and for half a mile around. Ambulance men treated 12 people and one gentleman had a heart attack. The trust did things very well. The proposed 4.1 acres of the acquisition was marked off by a line of stakes and red signs — the trust's red band of courage.

The Commonwealth persisted, the gates were closed and we didn't get in there again. Mrs Jones served a writ seeking $711,506 compensation, but the battle went on; at times she and the trust were dealing with nine different government departments.

Mrs Jones died 18 months ago and left the house and the 6.8 acres on which it stands to the trust and the Federal Government has decided it no longer needs the 4.1 acres for the expansion of ABV. So the trust gets the lot, the entire 13 acres. Heaven knows what it is worth, but as a nice subdivision job it would have to fetch nigh on $2 million.

So once more it is open again. It cost 50 cents for an adult to enter the garden plus another 30 cents to go inside the house. The first week-end wasn't quite as fruitful as the old indignation meeting but 12,000 people did go through.

Back in the nineteen-thirties when Benjamin Nathan, the furniture merchant, owned Ripponlea there were two head gardeners and 36 outside staff, who attended, apart from the lawns and ponds, 32 glass houses. There are only two of the old gardeners left but the estate looks as lovable as ever. There are four peacocks, there's a waterfall, there's a great lawn, a grotto and glorious curving paths through great oaks, elms, willows, mahogany gums and Moreton Bay figs.

But I did ask whether the modern generation could possibly leave anything to compare. Well... right now two gentlemen, Carl Hanis, 40, and Keith Ryall, 35, are building a bluestone medieval castle on the Western Highway just near Ballarat. It is on 5½ acres, it will cost half a million dollars and it will open in six months.

It is vast and fair dinkum. It looks like a cross between a Norman castle and Ray Méilland's old Fort Zindeneuf. Mr Ryall has been all over Europe studying castles and he says it will be utterly authentic complete with a dungeon, torture chamber and medieval torturing equipment.

The idea is that every week-end they will have chariot races and jousting competitions. Knights will dress up in armor, ladies will wear medieval costumes, and they are also building a replica of the Trojan horse. How that fits in is not entirely clear.

However it looks interesting and it could be the greatest attraction since the days of Ahab and Naboth.
