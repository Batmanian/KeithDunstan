On the outer in underwear

5 min read.

First published 02 Mar 1974

A birthday gift of expensive French black underpants sends Keith Dunstan into a Collins Street men's wear shop to discover that underwear has become a fashion statement, with designs from French girls in outline to spider motifs, zodiac signs and padded novelty items.

MY OLD grandmother was a great stickler for protocol. She used to say it was impossible for a gentleman to go out at night unless he was wearing a blue suit and a white shirt. Do you know, I went into one of our most respectable stores this week and I couldn't even get a white shirt!

The young man behind the counter looked back with terrible contempt: 'I'm sorry sir, they're just not being worn any more and we have no call for them.'

Perhaps I can bear wearing the awful colored shirts that are on the market. What is worrying me even more is the drift in underclothing. My grandmother had some very serious advice there also. She always insisted that one should wear immaculate underclothing. If they looked even slightly frayed or tired, wearing them was out of the question.

One would plead: 'What does it matter grandma, nobody is going to see them.'

'That's just where you're wrong,' she said. 'What if you're knocked down by a car in Collins Street?' This was the big worry in our house, getting knocked down by a car in Collins Street.

Grandmother always had the belief that if you were knocked down somehow you would be bereft of your trousers, and those awful tattered underpants would be exposed to public view. Even worse you might be taken to hospital, undressed by nurses, and the second-rate underclothing would betray you as a coarse person of miserable means.

In the winter one wore pure wool neck-to-knees, a single remarkable garment. In the summer there was the white singlet and the white boxer shorts. The design, shape and color never varied, and why should it?

Well, for my birthday last week I received a pair of underpants. They were French and they came in a box about twice the size of a matchbox. It is embarrassing even to talk about them. Clearly they were designed to show off masculinity.

I consulted my friend Stan Hughes, a former director of the Men's Wear Fashion Council. 'You've got to realise,' he said, 'underwear is now as important as outerwear. It's made to be seen. You think as much about the color of your underpants and singlet as you do about your shirt.'

'In case you're knocked down in Collins Street?' I said, remembering grandmother's sound dictum.

'Good Lord, where have you been?' he said incredulously. 'You're living in a different era. Males have to look good getting dressed. Have you seen any advertisements lately?'

He pulled out a series of glossy ideas and they were filled with pictures of chaps in startling bikini-like underpants. In every picture there was a female lurking in the background. Usually the female was half nude or sitting on a bed. You might say they were shots inspired by the latest TV serials. In no instance did I get the impression that the couple involved were respectably married.

So we went into a men's wear shop and there was a brilliant display of undergear. I stress that pure white was simply not to be had. There was underwear from France, Switzerland and Italy. The item I received for my birthday was called 'Hom,' and it cost an astonishing $5, and was black. Black was the IN color, said Stan. I remember my grandmother telling me that any young lady who wore undergarments of black was up to no good. It was a sign of unpardonable sin.

Apparently it is also important to be associated with the poverty look. There are underpants which look like faded denim and undies with dyes that have gone into an hysterical mess. If you believe in the stars one firm has brought out Aquarius, Sagittarius, Virgo and Libra underpants and all the rest.

So black underpants and singlets are the rage. They had one pair of black underpants and etched out in white all over them were figures of unclad French girls. At this very respectable men's wear store, they also had a pair in flesh color for wearing under see-through trousers. The old gentleman behind the counter was as surprised as I was. 'You wouldn't believe the nice people who buy them,' he said.

Some underwear dealers now have the Underpants of the Month. Apparently it is a sort of variation of the Book of the Month Club. Each month you get a dashing, new, novel design. Some of the designs are symbols of extreme masculinity. They sport knights in armor, Tarzan leopard skins, Jack and the beanstalk, vikings or cowboys carrying six guns.

Australians are said to be terrified of any threat or suggestion of lack of virility so no doubt the Underpants of the Month help them against such fears.

Other designs carry a slight air of menace. There is a line out now which features African tribesmen, pictures of Al Capone-type hoods, convicts, or, perhaps worst of all, a pair of shoulders covered in spiders. The next time a villain strips in a bedroom scene from The Box, I have no doubt that he will be wearing spider underpants.

Although, of course, you can go the other way. There are bikini undies covered in beautiful marigolds, petunias, roses and sun flowers. One firm has a line of almost unspeakable vulgarity, and naturally they are selling marvellously. Each pair carries a single motif where it can most obviously be seen. The motif is not just a straight print. It is about three inches long and raised and padded so that it looks spectacular. You can get quite a variety: a dragon, a cowboy, an elephant, a drunken-looking Flintstones character, a clown, or, incredibly, a large polka-dotted bow tie.

Modern art underpants are also coming in. I didn't see a Turner, but I did note a pair that looked suspiciously like Jackson Pollock's Blue Poles.

Stretched out in Collins Street in a pair of these you would look lovely.
