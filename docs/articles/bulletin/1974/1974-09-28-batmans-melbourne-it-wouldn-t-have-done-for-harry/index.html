It wouldn't have done for Harry!

4 min read.

First published 28 Sep 1974

The triumphs of loudmouth self-promoters — Evel Knievel conning $6 million for a canyon motorcycle stunt, Muhammad Ali's $100 million world television deal, Jimmy Connors declaring himself number one at 22 — lead Keith Dunstan to mourn the useful skills never taught at his boarding school, where achievement without visible effort was admired and Harry Wharton of the Magnet was the ideal, and to note that only Alan Bond has the appropriate lip for today's world.

WHEN I WAS at boarding school one's ideal was Harry Wharton out of the Magnet . . . strong, heroic, terribly decent and honourable and slightly stupid in a proud public school sort of way. Modesty was an absolutely essential character trait. It was simply not done to put yourself ahead of your fellow man in any way that would be unkind and embarrassing for him.

Achievement was admired, but no unfair methods were to be used. Say, a young gentleman scored four As in his final examinations. If he did this without any evidence of work then he was praised as a 'brain.' Ah, but if he swotted round the clock around. Then he was what the Magnet would have called 'a filthy swot.' He would have been an idiot if he hadn't got those four As.

The same thing applied to the sporting field, indeed more so. We had one character who became very good at long-distance running. His triumphs were not put down to natural ability — 'No wonder the silly bastard won. Do you know what he does? He gets up at 5.30 am every day and runs four miles before breakfast . . . we could all do that if we wanted to.'

Do you see what I'm getting at? We've gone hideously wrong. Our dear old school taught all the wrong personality traits and I am just beginning to realise it.

Just look at the scene around us. In the past weeks:

We have had a loud-mouthed, totally ineffective stunt man, who by screaming how good he was, conned over $6 million out of his fellow Americans. All he did was drop a jet-powered motor bike into a canyon.

We have Muhammad Ali or Cassius Clay, whatever they call him. That's another thing about our crazy school training. It was an Anglican establishment. Why didn't they tell us there was good mileage to be had in turning Muslim, Buddhist or whatever? Mr Muhammad, or does one call him Mr Ali, has pointed out that his fight is the greatest event since the parting of the Red Sea. No — better than that — the most super colossal event in the history of the world. 'A miracle is going to be performed,' he said. 'Ain't no washerwoman like George Foreman gonna whip me.' This gentlemanly, self-effacing attitude enabled the promoters to make a world television deal of $100 million.

How about Jimmy Connors, the new tennis star. He has learnt superbly. Every Press conference is insult-laden. Mr Connors is only 22 yet already I find him irresistibly loathsome. After mercilessly beating Ken Rosewall at Forest Hills he sent champagne up to the Press box and said 'Get Laver for me. Jimmy is now number one. And if you're number one then you've got to beat everyone.' Apparently our Rod had said of Jimmy: 'Connors thinks he's the greatest thing since Seven-Up.' So now it appears that they are to have around the world a series of five gladiatorial contests worth a million dollars. Presumably they will each have their script writers equipped with copies of Roget's Thesaurus and rhyming dictionary working out filthy things to say to each other.

Billie Jean King has just produced an autobiography. They used to call her 'Little Miss Moffitt' until she learned, as they say, 'to put her act together.' Now she is known as 'The Lip.'

Do you see that we lack entirely the appropriate skills for this sort of thing? Only one Australian has them, and he has been absurdly criticised for having launched the most disastrous America's Cup entry. We have missed the point entirely. The important thing is to keep talking.

Unquestionably Mr Bond is our most brilliant performer in the 'Lip' stakes. I think his best line, apart from abusing rival helmsmen, was this: 'I haven't brought 50 men and two boats half way around the world to pussyfoot gently about Newport playing boatsypooh.'

But for quotes like that one the America's Cup would hardly have rated a line in the U.S. newspapers.

Neale Fraser came back to the U.S. last week, a little startled by all these events and he noted that the world now belonged to the man with a gimmick. If you could devise a good stunt you could get away with anything in America. What they wanted was excitement.

Now Neale Fraser was the one true amateur we produced, probably the nicest bloke ever to pick up a tennis racket. I asked him what he would do if he were starting out all over again as a budding player. He looked a little wistful: 'I would get myself a good PR man and manager.'
