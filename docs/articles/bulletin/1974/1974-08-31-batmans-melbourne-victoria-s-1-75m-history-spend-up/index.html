Victoria's $1.75m history spend-up

5 min read.

First published 31 Aug 1974

The Victorian Government's $500,000 combined with a Federal Tourism grant of $1.25 million funds a wave of folk museums and historical recreations — Echuca's riverboat waterfront, Bendigo's Chinese village Dai Gum San, Castlemaine's goldfields museum, Warrnambool's maritime village — as midnight raids by shire councillors scour the countryside for log cabins and steam tractors, though a worrying uniformity of antique cash registers and pioneer pharmacies is creeping in.

THERE WAS a great advertisement in our newspapers last week which began 'Warm to the Wimmera where the covered wagons rolled to pioneer the plains of the Golden West. They came in creaking covered wagons, over the ranges from the silent deserted goldfields of Victoria, the 19th century pioneers of the golden granary we now call the Wimmera, legendary land of the emu . . . Victoria, all it needs is you.'

You wouldn't believe how hard we're flogging history at the moment. During the past financial year the Victorian Government has put $500,000 into projects recommended by the Historical and Tourist Projects Committee. The Federal Ministry of Tourism has put up $1,250,000 mostly for historical ideas.

Among the new ones, Echuca is restoring its waterfront to the dear-old riverboat town it was in the eighties. Bendigo is busy building a great walled Chinese village with pagoda, museum ornamental gate and all. It is called Dai Gum San (big gold mountain) and one minister already has described it as potentially Australia's greatest tourist attraction.

Castlemaine is getting a goldfields museum and Warrnambool is going for a maritime village, 15 acres including an old lighthouse, restored barquentine and according to one brochure 'the village atmosphere will be that of a bustling busy port. The smell of tar, rope and wood will be in the air and skilled craftsmen will be seen at work — shipwrights, sailmakers, coopers, shipsmiths and model makers.'

Decentralisation hasn't exactly been easy or a bonny success, but if you have an ailing country town or provincial city, history can be a sure-fire money spinner. It fascinates me, because until eight years ago nobody really thought of it. History was something the English or the French had. Australia was the country without a past. Now drop into a country town and say 'take me to your folk museum.' If they haven't got one, you can bet they're standing in a deputation queue at the Treasury buildings waiting for their grant.

So the competition in the history trade has been ruthless. There is not always enough history to go around. In the old days city fathers, councillors, and experts used to make lightning midnight raids through the country searching for muscular young footballers. Now it is quite different.

Now aldermen, shire councillors, town clerks, shire engineers, sometimes disguised like CIA men, are searching the countryside for material. Particularly sought after are log cabins, steam tractors, genuine old mechanics institutes, mining machinery, lovable old handpainted signs, mirrors and advertising posters, coaches, timber jinkers, old lamps, sailing ships, and the whole bit of Victoriana from grandmother's clothing through to bedroom crockery. If you are hiding a bark hut at the bottom of your garden you can practically name your own price.

Swan Hill, back in 1966, was really first into this business on a large scale. They opened their folk museum with the Gem paddle steamer and a complete little pioneer town. It was such a money spinner everybody followed. Ballarat fought back with its pioneer gold mining centre, Sovereign Hill. Bendigo counter-attacked with its Central Deborah gold mine and old trams. There are so many tramway museums, so many people utterly devoted to old trams, that when the State Electricity Commission stopped their Bendigo tram operations, there were applications from all over the country. There was even a raiding party from South Australia and for several days Bendigo, under the leadership of its mayor, had to take siege precautions to prevent a hijack.

Jeparit and Warracknabeal in the Mallee have similar folk museums both specialising in farm machinery. I tell you, that rival winnower and harvester scouts have been right through that country.

And what started in Victoria is spreading across the nation. At Somersby, 45 miles out of Sydney, the citizens are re-creating Sydney Cove exactly as it was in 1810. Hill End in NSW has yet another gold rush site, Kiandra another. Forbes is going in for a vintage village. In South Australia they have restored the Pichi Richi railway and at Port Arthur in Tasmania they have a fine Federal Government grant to restore the old lunatic asylum as a museum and reception centre.

There is just one saddening part. Many of the folk museums are taking on a deadly similarity . . . the same vintage grocer shop with the same antique National cash register, bulk flour and boiled lollies, the same pioneer pharmacy with the big bottles, the same pioneer cottage with hessian covered walls.

However I have a great idea for a new folk museum. We could build it as a re-creation of the late thirties. The staff would be clean shaven, short back and sides and they would even wear ties. The folk museum post office would deliver letters twice daily and be open Saturday mornings. The bank too would open on Saturdays.

The folk museum pub would have brown lino on the floor and on top of the bar. It wouldn't attempt to be a phony English pub and beer would sell at sixpence a glass. Folk museum matches current would have 60-a-box and a striking surface right down either side and there would be department stores and shops that not only delivered but didn't charge for it.

There would be lots more like that. As a history item it would be an absolute winner.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Victoria's $1.75m history spend-up, The Bulletin, 31 August 1974. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
