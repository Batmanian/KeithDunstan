Driven close to a breakdown

5 min read.

First published 21 Sep 1974

With three family members simultaneously pursuing driving licences under Victoria's new Learner Driver Permits Act, costing $140 and months of agony for a neighbour's daughter, Keith Dunstan surveys Melbourne's 780 driving schools, the ruthless battle to be first in the phone book producing the A.A.A.A.AAABAAABAAA Driving School, and the psychological impossibility of a parent teaching their own child when a gear grind is a shaft through the heart.

DO YOU KNOW what is happening in our house? Three members of the family are all straining after a driving licence at the one time. Ye gods, the strain, the nervous tension and the cost! You wouldn't believe.

My friend Bill, the lawyer who lives around the corner, reckons that it has cost him three months of pure agony and $140 to get a driving licence for his 18-year-old daughter. That would be about routine and you might even say he was lucky.

September 20, 1974, is a significant date. That's when the new provisions of the Victorian Motor Car (Learner Driver Permits) Act, 1972, comes into operation. From what I can gather this is the rule: from now on you must have a permit to learn. That costs $2. You go along to the prescribed police station where you get an eyesight test. Then you get a written test on road law. That costs $5. There are 240 written questions and you are asked some.

If you pass that, your learner's permit is valid for 12 months and you must be under instruction for three months before you can go for your actual driving test, which costs $10. And if you pass that your driving licence is worth another $12.

I am not quarrelling with any of this. Heavens above, you don't quarrel with income tax assessors or people who hand out driving licences. Correct instruction is very important.

It's the worry of the thing that is hard to live with. All three members of the family have so far failed once. That means they have to go through the whole process again. It can take six weeks even to get an appointment. So getting a driving licence is an event of immense cultural importance, and sometimes as you move in jerking grinding movements from Camp One to Camp Two to Camp Three it seems a task as inhuman as climbing Mt Everest.

Now take the daughter who failed. She worked for months on that road law. What she didn't know about millimetres from fire hydrants, passing stationary trams, and curious road signs parked outside country towns wasn't worth knowing.

Well, she had failed. Did we have to keep paying all that money to the driving school? I gave a little exhibition of how the car worked. The blast she gave me was ear-splitting: 'You didn't give a light when you diverged from the kerb. You drove with one hand on the wheel. You changed gears going round a corner. You rode the clutch. You took the corner into Burke Road dangerously wide. You are utterly incompetent. You have so many ingrained bad habits your only hope of getting a driving licence now would be to fork in Outer Mongolia.'

So I thought back to the time when I had gone for my driving licence. It was during the war years and was it only 10 bob for the licence? I don't remember. But I do remember we went down to Russell Street. The policeman said: 'Pull up at that kerb.' He went into a shop and bought his lunch-time sandwiches. 'Take the car around that corner.' He telephoned his wife from a public phone box. We went back to the police headquarters and he told me to back around a kerb. I failed that three times. 'Well, I dunno,' he said, scratching his head.

My father who was in the back seat said: 'You realise he is licensed to fly an aeroplane and can even loop the loop.' The policeman then said: 'Jeez, if he can do that he can drive a car.' The whole process took seven minutes.

In those days fathers usually gave the driving instruction. Now it is considered psychologically impossible to train one's young. This is because the motor car has become such a status symbol, such a treasured expensive part of one's establishment . . . You can't stand watching it being tortured by your young. A gear grind is a shaft through your heart. And besides, as mentioned earlier, you are incompetent to teach anyway.

So you are forced to go to a driving school. Under 'Motor Schools' in the pink pages I counted 780 names. It is not generally realised, but the trauma of getting a driving licence has created one of the biggest industries the country has ever seen. When two or three people are gathered together in one place you can be sure that one of them is learning to drive.

Even at the beginning of the ordinary telephone directory there are 14 driving schools. Indeed the battle to be first in the telephone book is one of the most ruthless of our time. I understand that the PMG is now laying down rules that you can only have four As with full stops followed by three As without full stops, with the result that the winner in Melbourne, until someone else comes up with a smart angle, is the A.A.A.A.AAABAAABAAA Driving School followed by the A.A.A.AAABAAA Driving School.

Even writing a cheque to one of these driving schools calls for extraordinary hits in concentration.

But just take those phone book entries. Some have interesting names like the Pink Panther, or the Jet Cat School or the Romulus and Remus.

There is also a Camelot School, which I doubt would be particularly expert in training you to joust with your car.

Lessons mostly cost $5 an hour. On final examination day we have paid $6.70 for a special hour lesson beforehand. Then $10 for the driving instructor to go with the candidate for the test. Parents should never go — they make the child nervous.
