It isn't cricket but it's an art

4 min read.

First published 09 Nov 1974

As the MCC tour opens on a miserably wet day — confirming another season of meteorological writing — Keith Dunstan surveys the craft of wet-weather cricket reportage from Neville Cardus's Fifth Test meditations to the Port Lincoln press box watching the Ali-Foreman fight on television, while English writers note that Australia perversely stages Tests only in high-rainfall coastal cities when the dry centre might solve everything.

IT IS IMPORTANT to believe in omens. Claudius, for example, had no doubt that he would become Caesar after he had been shat upon by a bird. For your help, I don't remember what type of bird cack makes you Caesar, but I suspect it is an eagle. Today's omen is that it was miserably wet on the first official day of the MCC cricket tour. This leaves little doubt that we will have an appalling number of wet days throughout the length of the tour.

Now, for the sake of young cricket writers, I feel one should pass on the benefits of one's lengthy experience at wet-weather writing.

Whether there is play or not, editors always expect something. Neville Cardus was always a marvellous wet-cricket writer. A classic example was during the Fifth Test in Melbourne, in 1937. He wrote that after seeing so much rain in Australia and in Manchester he felt he should publish this advertisement: 'Cricket writer in reduced circumstances seeks position in meteorological office either in Australia or England. Not afraid of hard work. Graduated at Manchester Weather Bureau.'

Then he went on to describe the dogged nature of the crowd, how cricket crowds were so superbly trained to display patience, how the police protected the pitch as if it enshrined the crown jewels and so on. Then he went on to say that apparently the captains could not agree, for when they left the scene the umpires had a look and walked around the pitch warily as though half expecting it to get up and shake itself and wet everybody.

The problem of establishing objective wetness or dryness of a cricket pitch is as deep and mysterious as all your metaphysics, from Descartes to Professor Whitehead. It is a serious business.

My personal apprenticeship has been good. I covered a Test match at Manchester when not a ball was bowled. Some of my stuff on the hand-shovelled style and beautiful approach to the wicket of the Manchester roller was considered by everyone to be deeply moving.

I also covered a wet Test at Birmingham. The cricket writers stayed in a very curious hotel built over the Birmingham Railway Station. Undoubtedly the finest story of that Test was written by the fellow who recounted the nightmare he had while sleeping there. He kept hearing announcements all night — porters telling what time the hotel would arrive at Victoria Station.

I thought the standard of reporting wet-weather cricket was high at Port Lincoln in this match MCC versus Country XI. There was virtually no play. However, the teams plus cricket writers had a magnificent meal of scallops and prawns at the Great Northern Hotel. During lunch everyone watched the Muhammad Ali-George Foreman fight on television. Now in our rain-soaked Press box we had telephones. Later in the day we all listened while a BBC chap shouted his story on a direct line right through to London at heaven knows what expense.

Brilliantly, he didn't mention the cricket. He described the scene at lunch around the television set. He was intrigued that the MCC team to a man were all for Ali. They thought him a great character, they loved him for his inventive vocal style. The Australians, on the other hand, despised his 'I am the greatest' attitude and were all for George Foreman. There was a Tarzan cry of triumph from England's Freddy Titmus when Ali sent Foreman to the canvas for the big count. You can put this down as England's first win for the tour.

Other writers turned in very good meteorological stuff. Australia is remarkable for its high-speed, fruit salad weather. Ten-minute bursts of sunshine alternated with torrential downpours.

However, many of the cricket writers are concerned that they have had to display their aquatic talents so early in the tour. One English writer said to me it always rains on cricket in Australia and it's obvious why. They play only on the coast in the high-rainfall areas — Brisbane, Sydney, Melbourne, here at Port Lincoln. Why don't they play somewhere in the dry centre, he asked, and he has a point.
