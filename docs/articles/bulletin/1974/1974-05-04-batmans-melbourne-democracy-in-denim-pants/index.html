Democracy in denim pants

5 min read.

First published 04 May 1974

A worldwide shortage of denim — the fabric that the magazine 'Clothes' says has united capitalist and communist, Arab and Jew — brings Keith Dunstan to Yakka's sales manager and Melbourne's swing-door jeaneries, where 15 million pairs were sold in Australia in 1973.

THIS WEEK it seemed important to give you all the intelligence one has accumulated on denim. I had not fully realised that apart from the energy crisis, the newsprint crisis, and the shipping crisis, there is also a terrible denim crisis. This news comes from an American magazine called Clothes which states: 'The United States, the most technically sophisticated, most economically expansionist, most growth-orientated society the world has ever known, cannot produce enough of this cloth to meet the need — even though it has a virtual monopoly on the techniques and machinery which produce it.'

Richly eloquent, Clothes pointed out that denim was drawing the world together like no ideology had ever done. Denim jeans were stretched across the behinds of male and female, capitalist and communist, rich and poor, Arab and Jew, African and South African... they were even being sold illegally in Russia for prices up to $50. Abbie Hoffman, Joan Baez, Bernadine Dohrn, Bob Dylan, Mick Jagger, Brigitte Bardot, Lana Turner and Henry Kissinger all wore jeans.

Regrettably Clothes did not tell us whether Richard Nixon wore jeans, but its fine eloquence continued in this style: 'Class distinctions do not matter. Generation gaps do not matter. Ethnic hatreds do not matter. Three-piece suits are being made of denim, bikinis, boxer shorts and trench coats are being made out of denim. The fabric of the working class is being lined with mink and studded with diamonds. Cars are upholstered with it, and Bibles are covered with it.'

The Australian magazine Tailor and Men's Wear in a recent issue reported: 'As far as our information goes, world production of denim has reached 400 million yards. Today, there is a backlog in deliveries of 100 million yards. It costs one million dollars to build a factory with a capacity of 10 million yards of denim. But what will happen to denim in two years from now?'

Well, one wonders. Tailor and Men's Wear warned that to meet the shortage all kinds of cottons were being sold under fancy names like 'indi denim' which were not real denim.

Your correspondent called on Les Bidey, sales manager of Yakka, which claims to make more trousers than anyone else in Australia. Bidey produced these figures for the production of jeans in Australia for the 12 months to December 1973. Men's, 6,400,000; Boys', 2,600,000; Women's, 4,000,000. Imported jeans, 2,000,000. Grand total, 15,000,000.

'In one year,' said Bidey, 'a pair of jeans is sold for every man, woman and child in the country. The average person must possess at least two pairs. But I think we are past the hysterical boom. That is, we are beginning to flatten out now. But production will never go down. The fabric will be with us like this for ever, we'll just get variations in fashion. Oh yes, there is a shortage and there is a lot of rubbishy denim to be had. But the shortage isn't as bad as it was. We're getting ours from Bradmill, Australian made.'

But what about the fashions? You've got to have the washed-look. This came in last September. Before that, said Bidey, you mercilessly maltreated your jeans yourself. Some people bleached their jeans by strapping them down on the roofs of their cars. Some went swimming in them, waded up and down in the sea on the theory that this not only bleached the jeans beautifully but shrunk them correctly to the shapes of their bodies. Others ran them endlessly through the washing machine, using ferocious bleaching agents. This often destroyed the fabric in the process.

Yakka are using a special kind of bleach. First they are washed, then they are bleached, then they are rinsed at least another three times after that. 'This has been a sensational hit. Unwashed denim looks funny to me now.'

The era of jeans studded with brass and jewels is also over. Their line is called Keyman, and like most jeans they're mostly unisex. Their latest design is in the style of sailors' pants. That is, they have a square flap in the front held down by press studs and they lace up in the back in the style of old footballers' pants. The sexier you want to be, the more press studs you leave undone. The fanciest look as if they were designed for streakers. One swift pull and you could be out of them in a 50th of a second. Denim skirts, short and long, also have the press studs.

Once the jeans were sold mostly in department stores, but now, says Bidey, the biggest sales are in the specialised jeaneries that don't sell anything else. And it can be very exciting buying a pair of jeans.

He says there's one shop in Melbourne. The changing rooms have swing doors like in the old pubs. There's a horseshoe circuit of them. The basic idea is that you go into one of these swing door changing rooms and you're likely to burst through the swing doors and you're likely to see a naked bird inside.

But that's life in denim. And the social consequences of it all are far from decided. You may have noticed the recent arrest of Txipora Cohen, 25, Israel's first female bank robber. The cable reports cryptically stated that they traced her from her trousers which she lost while fleeing from the hold-up. So you see jeans are really a more deadly piece of identification than even fingerprints. No two pairs are the same.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Democracy in denim pants, The Bulletin, 4 May 1974. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
