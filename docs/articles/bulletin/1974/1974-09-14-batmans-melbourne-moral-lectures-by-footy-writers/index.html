Moral lectures by footy writers

4 min read.

First published 14 Sep 1974

North Melbourne's Keith Greig wins his second consecutive Brownlow Medal — receiving 58 times more coverage than Patrick White's Nobel Prize — but Richmond Football Club secretary Alan Schwab calls it a joke and says the system must be changed, triggering pointed responses from the Herald's sports editor and letters to The Age, all of which demonstrate that in 1974 Victorian football is where moral problems are most superbly debated.

IT IS VERY interesting here in Melbourne. If you want learned discussion on social questions go to the religious columns, but if you really want high-toned debate on morals, then you do better by reading the football writers.

You must appreciate that we have just moved through a very significant period — the annual announcement of the winner of the Brownlow Medal, that is the best and fairest Australian Rules league footballer for the year.

I have just done a quick estimate on column inches and I reckon that the winner of the 1974 Brownlow received 58 times more space than Patrick White won for taking off the Nobel Prize. Even the build up was pretty good. On Brownlow eve, the Herald, a broadsheet, gave an entire page listing every footballer from Phar Lap who was even faintly likely to win.

After that you proceeded to television, where you could fill in each vote as it was counted. The Brownlow ceremony took place at a swanky dinner in the grand ballroom at the Southern Cross Hotel. As a spectacle it was superior to any telecast of the Federal election or even the crowning of Miss Australia.

At each match during the year the field umpire gives his vote for the best three players — three points for the best, two for the second best, and one for the third. These are put into an envelope. On Brownlow night they open the envelopes. They announce the single votes first, the two votes second and the three votes third. As the vote tally builds you fill in your Herald Brownlow guide. You can imagine the tension.

The winner was north Melbourne's Keith Greig, the same fellow who won it last year. The Age had a story on the front page and almost the entire back page. The Sun had front page, all the back page, turn over from back page and the entire two-page centre spread of pictures, showing the excited winner, tense loser, cheering, back slappers, enthusiasts hugging each other.

But here's the rub. None of the commentators had listed Keith Greig as a likely candidate for the Brownlow. They hadn't even put him down as an outsider.

Officials of the Richmond Football Club were upset. The Age quoted the secretary of the Richmond Club, Alan Schwab, as saying: 'It's a joke. The Brownlow Medal doesn't mean anything any more. The whole system of awarding votes must be changed. I don't think Greig has deserved to win a Brownlow Medal let alone two.'

The Age football writer was stunned that his team mate, Doug Wade, who had kicked 91 goals during the season, hadn't gained a single vote.

There was some sharp reaction to this. Terry Vines, the Herald sports editor commented: 'There is a sickening sensation in the gut over Richmond's reaction to Keith Greig winning a second Brownlow Medal. An outburst of sour grapes and wretched sportsmanship comes not from the players but from responsible officials. Look at little Kevin Bartlett, the man everyone was willing to win the trophy. Right from the start he said he didn't stand a chance and he accepted defeat as philosophically as he accepts victory.

'The shattering reaction came in the form of the spurious attack from Richmond secretary Alan Schwab, the sort of hurt, resentful reaction of a man who says and acts before he really thinks about it.'

There was a reaction, too. In a letter to The Age, Brian Morley of Nunawading said: 'To say the very least, Richmond are bad sports. Is this the sort of attitude Richmond wishes to cultivate in its club and junior footballers? If it is, my advice to the VFL, as a whole is that the next time Richmond talks about leaving the league — let them.'

It's been a tough year for Richmond in many ways. The Richmond follower, Mal Brown, back on August 19, was suspended for four matches on a charge of throwing the ball at the umpire.

Bob Davis, ex-champion, ex-coach of Geelong, wrote in the Herald: 'When you are a small boy you are taught that whatever you do, there are certain rules you must follow. You don't pick up a duster and throw it at the teacher. You don't pick up an orange and throw it at a policeman. If you do these things then you know you will be disciplined. In the same way you don't pick up a football and throw it at an umpire whether you are a little kid or a big VFL player. If you can't control yourself and break a rule you are punished. It is as simple as that.'

See what I mean. Victorian football is a fascinating study. Nowhere else in 1974 are moral problems so superbly painted.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Moral lectures by footy writers, The Bulletin, 14 September 1974. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
