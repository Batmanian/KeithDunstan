Footballers get worker power

4 min read.

First published 05 Jan 1974

The formation of the Victorian Football League Players' Association at a secret meeting at Melbourne University's Union House prompts Keith Dunstan to imagine the consequences of a footballers' union capable of black banning finals and stopping the game.

AM SURPRISED that there has not been more discussion, even alarm, over the grave new mood of our sportsmen. I trust that it did not escape your notice that there was a strike at Wimbledon this year. Practically all the tennis players of true note downed their rackets. If discipline had been just a little tighter, if the shop stewards had been more efficient they would have been able to stop the tournament altogether.

A short time ago we had the jockeys' strike in New South Wales. It was interesting to discover that there was actually a jockeys' union. They were fighting for such things as sensible payments for losing rides and their power was sufficient to stop the horses. The loneliness of the racetracks had all the classic atmosphere of the loneliness of the railway stations and bus depots during the best of our strikes by the transport unions.

I am surprised it has taken so long to come about, but last week saw the formation of the Victorian Football League Players' Association. About 60 players met, very appropriately, at Union House, Melbourne University. It was all done in secret. No Press man was allowed in to report the proceedings. They refused to reveal any of the names on the attendance book. The spokesman, presumably the Jack Mundey of football, was Geoff Pryor of Essendon. Pryor is a defender which is a little disappointing. One would have thought they might have chosen an attacking forward, or an aggressive ruck rover for the job. Eleven clubs were represented at the meeting and the theory is that they will go back and form their own associations that Footscray, Fitzroy, St Kilda and elsewhere with members right through, down to the seconds and thirds.

The Victorian Football League itself is opposed to the new association because it fears players demands may lead to strikes with an entire disruption of the football season.

That situation is not without precedent. Just before the start of the season in 1970 there was a footballers' strike at Essendon. Five of the top players, including the captain, Don Lund, refused to play in a match against South Adelaide at Essendon. The Herald at the time reported that 'Essendon officials were stunned.' The strike continued all the week and they were still on strike for the opening match of the season against Carlton. Then after three days of haggling they agreed to some arrangement.

There have been many other cases of top players refusing to play unless their rewards were increased. But now we have a very different situation — a union, which is able to observe the rewards won and the way footballers are esteemed in other parts of the world.

What will they be after? Well, I can see them seeking better working conditions, shorter playing hours, only one training session a week, no running on Sundays, indeed no Sunday play at all and double time for Saturdays.

The thought of stopping league matches in Melbourne, the thought of, say, stopping a grand final leaves one spellbound with the enormity of such a thing. It would be like stopping a Coronation in London or a presidential election in New York. But I would suspect that the players' association would use more sophisticated modern methods. Instead of calling a total football black-out, it would call out key-men, say all umpires, all ruckmen, all forwards. They might stop matches just for 10 minutes, I mean, they would have stop work meetings of the VFLPA at the centre bounce at, say, 3 pm.

All these are as inevitable as the dawn. Also inevitable I think is the morning when a footballer is locked out from training. Can you see the picket lines outside the MCG? Or the great forward who has been declared a scab because he insisted on playing? Can you see the headlines: 'VFLPA Calls Out Rover In Pay Dispute'?

The power of such an organisation would be awe-inspiring. Can you imagine a national sporting strike on Saturday — nobody across the nation running, jumping or kicking. The TAB silent and empty, the radio stations playing soothing music, the ABC sporting service crossing over to 'nowhere', no learned commentaries or post mortems. The effect would be so awful I think the ACTU would wreck the government within a week.

But before things go any further I would like to give a small piece of advice to the VFL Players' Association. The name is not good enough. I would suggest the Amalgamated Football Workers' Union, or the Federated Footballers' and Dropkickers' Association of Australia, or even the International Football Workers of the World.

A union must have an air of menace.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Footballers get worker power, The Bulletin, 5 January 1974. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
