The boss is busy in room 350

6 min read.

First published 25 May 1974

A three-week hotel workers' strike — rejected a $14-a-week rise in favour of $20 — forces hotel owners and managers across Melbourne to make beds, wash dishes and work the bars themselves, including the Windsor Hotel's manager found on the third floor making beds and Jack Lacantro presiding over an empty Young and Jackson's.

IT IS NOT generally realised how much we have suffered in Melbourne these past three weeks. For three weeks we have been unable to buy our counter lunches — no chicken and mash, no curried lamb and peas, no fried flake and chips.

The trouble has been the hotel workers' strike. They were offered a rise of $14 a week but they wanted $20. So, in the sour, dry battle that followed they allowed the hotels to carry on only with owners, managers, licensees and their immediate families. Any breaking of these rules then the unions cut off their beer.

So there have been heartrending scenes. Take the hotels. Mr Jack Kortum owns six enormous wells in the suburbs like the South Side Six, and the Waltzing Matilda. Mr Kortum would be a millionaire. Mr Kortum has been washing dishes, making beds, waiting on tables, clearing away after weddings. So has Mrs Kortum. So have the three Kortum children who are old enough to serve in the hotel.

In Collins Street the lady working the vacuum cleaner in the foyer would probably be Mrs Judy Matear, the wife of the managing director. The most heartrending case I think is the dear old Windsor Hotel in Spring Street. Built in the 1880s it is the last of the very distinguished grand hotels.

We call it the Blue Rinse Hotel because it is inhabited by all the rich old dears with blue rinses. You see them in the lounge taking tea from the good silver and eating very thin tomato or chicken sandwiches. In the foyer there are so many Buckmasters in big gilt frames you would think they were supplied by the Forestry Department.

When I called there mid-morning there wasn't a blue rinse in sight and every bar was locked. I asked to see the manager, Mr Alan Carruthers, and a very formal gentleman at the registration said, 'You will find him up on the third floor making beds. I am sorry, sir, he is too busy to come down to you.'

I found him in room 350. He had all the classic gear, a mobile trolley with all the bits, little cakes of soap, writing paper, linen, etc, carpet sweeper. He hardly looked up as I entered. 'Can't stop now,' he said. 'Fire your questions while I work.'

But I was fascinated by his style. Superb economy of movement. His beds were smooth, unwrinkled and I was very happy to see that he always mitred the corners.

As he dashed on to wield the carpet sweeper in 352 he reported that his day started at 6 am. Room service was out, so that he served breakfast in the dining room to house guests only. Just a continental breakfast. Washing the dishes kept him going until 9.30 am. Then he worked on the rooms all morning, making about 60 beds.

He served lunch, again only to house guests — a straight menu, soup, oysters, fish or meat and dessert. By the time he had washed the dishes it was three o'clock. 'Then I've just got to sit down for an hour,' he groaned. But after that it's drinks in the lounge, dinner, dish washing, dusting, carpet cleaning, and he finishes up around midnight. He wouldn't say how much money the Windsor was losing. He just shuddered. He sped on to room 359 and found that the beds were already made. The lovely people there had cleaned up themselves.

Some of the little hotels in the city have carried on almost as before. They have been able to dispense with counter luncheon, dispense with accommodation and provide bar service through the family. For them it has been like the dreamy days of six o'clock closing with the parched hordes pushing to get to the bar. They have made a fortune.

But many of the larger establishments have closed altogether. For example, Young and Jackson's. I could hardly have been more shocked had they permanently closed the doors of St Paul's cathedral over on the other corner. Y and J's is a religious institution, some say of equal importance. On the cultural tourist rating it has always been close to No 1 in Melbourne slightly in front of the stuffed effigy of Phar Lap.

I could hardly believe that Y and J's had closed. I went from door to door beating with my fists. All locked. Eventually Jack Lacantro, the famous licensee, came to my rescue and let me in. It was dark, gloomy and eerie inside like the Melbourne general cemetery at midnight. The untouched bottles of liquor on the shelves could have been the tombstones.

'Normally at this time,' said Jack, 'we would have 400 or 500 people in here.' To which one replied, 'But you're not the number one well in Melbourne. The Matthew Flinders out at Chadstone sells the most beer.'

Jack tossed that off with contempt. The Matthew Flinders was big on bottled beer, but Y and J's still led them all with across the counter draught beer sales. Normally he employed 40 barmen but they were all on strike. He looked round sadly at the emptiness, the loneliness of the bars and said, 'It is the first time I can remember that Young and Jackson's has been closed like this, and I have been here for 35 years.'

He pushed a button, turned on a light, and there was Chloe illuminated. You know how she stands haughtily in that grotto with her head turned to one side. I had the impression that she was even colder than usual. Her head seemed to be turned around even further in total disgust at the situation.

One recalled the American serviceman who had thrown a glass of beer at Chloe during the war. But that was just another rumour according to Jack. It wasn't an American at all who threw that glass. Actually it was a disgruntled employee.

The lesson for hotel keepers is clear. Obviously they must get busy and populate or perish. I would suggest that they pick up, say, four or five wives in order to generate enough legitimate children to serve behind the bar.

STOP PRESS: The hotel strike is over. Employees have settled for an offer of $18 a week. The counter lunches are on again and the drought has broken in more ways than one.
