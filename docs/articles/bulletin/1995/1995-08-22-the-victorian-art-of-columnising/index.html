The Victorian art of columnising

5 min read.

First published 22 Aug 1995

Keith Dunstan tours the mansions of Toorak and finds the suburb's latest fashion is neo-classical columns, tracing its architectural fads from mock Tudor through modernism and "Fortress Toorak" to the Greco-Roman revival of the 1990s.

One thing remains strange about Melbourne. If your shares are paying superb dividends, if you are extremely rich, the one place you have to live is Toorak. It is a very small portion of the vast new council of Stonnington, extending barely 10 square kilometres from Williams Road to Glenferrie Road, from Malvern Road to the Yarra.

But for the best people it is even smaller than that. There are grades of Toorak. If you really want to be superior, if you want to live in the absolutely well-stuffed area, then I suggest you should be east of Grange Road, up around St Georges Road, Lansell Road, Albany, Heyington Place and Hopetoun Road. Ah yes, that's where the Rolls-Royces, the Porsches, the big Mercedes lurk. That's the place to be.

Vagaries: As a dedicated Toorak watcher, I have observed it through all its vagaries. I can remember the subdivision of the grand estates in the 1940s and '50s. I can recall when the first tower blocks went up in Toorak Road. Luxury apartments, they were (shudder). We never had 'flats' in Toorak. I can remember the ghastly phase when mock Tudor was all the go. This was splendid Elizabethan stuff, all along St Georges Road, and Toorak Village — laughably, still called a village — had some of the touches of a hamlet in Dorset.

Come the 1960s it was essential to be modern, to shed all the old stuff. Your lines had to be clean and pure — floor-to-ceiling glass, clean edges, no nonsense with decoration. As Tom Wolfe said, 'Bauhaus was our house.'

In the 1980s, glass went out of favour. Protection was the thing. It became 'Fortress Toorak'. Over in East Malvern and Glen Iris there was a return to Federation architecture, the gazebo, the gatehouse. Everything over there had to be romantic, and all the fence builders had special dies so they could turn out fences with gorgeous twiddly bits.

But Fortress Toorak went the way of Manila and Beverly Hills; one had to protect one's wealth, particularly when one was on tour at Portsea or Antibes. We saw some of the most magnificent walls since the construction of Pentridge. Better than Pentridge, though, they had the most sophisticated security phones at the entrance, power-operated gates that lifted like a drawbridge and wonderful sensory lights that illuminated the whole place should an intruder poke his nose anywhere near.

Now, Toorakers, please note — we have entered an entirely new era. Modernism, even postmodernism, is out. Neo-classical is all the go. You must have columns; not little columns, but great columns — Doric, Ionic or Corinthian. It is hard to say precisely what has prompted this Greco-Roman phase. Some think it is part of a worldwide return to conservatism. It dates back to Thatcherism, Reaganism and was brought into full flood by Jeff Kennettism. Could be, but I think it is part of the security thing. If you have a place that looks like the headquarters of the National Bank, it just seems harder to rob. A small tour of 'nicer' Toorak will reveal some examples ...

Now, Hopetoun Rd is becoming a columnists' paradise. There's No 60. They were building it for most of 1994. The locals call it Tara, straight out of Gone With the Wind. It has lovely grey columns that rise two storeys. Behind the columns there is an upstairs balcony with windows. Sometimes on an autumn evening, when the sun is at the right angle, I swear you can almost see Clark Gable and Vivien Leigh on that balcony. There are sets of massive iron gates, which presumably open at the touch of a button. The facade is not sullied by anything vulgar; the garage is underground.

Further up the hill, there is No 51. Known as the 'State Library', it has the same grey look, the noble Doric columns out front and the tiered steps, but I don't think the students would be allowed to sit there at lunchtime and eat their sandwiches. Even further up the hill on the other side, heading towards Malvern Rd, there are two very large buildings under construction, covered with workmen. The columns on these mansions are of Samson and Delilah proportions. Indeed, the buildings remind one of the Cecil B. de Mille set for The Ten Commandments.

Mournful: But seriously, you don't have to travel more than 200 metres anywhere in this area to find good columns. Take 182 Kooyong Rd — this is really neo-Georgian, very severe, even a trifle mournful. My friends call this 'Funeral Home Classic', and there are a lot of them in Toorak. As you walk past, talk respectfully in whispers.

Mmmm, No 2 Iona Avenue. That was built columnless in 1940, but mercifully the current owners have added columns to the entrance to keep up with the trend. St Georges Road is also highly columnworthy. No 59 is all white, with a portico exactly like the Melbourne Town Hall's.

At the corner of Duffryn Place, there is a big one under construction. The board outside tells us this will be Torresdale House, 'a luxurious classic-style residence built to an extremely high standard leaving nothing to be desired'.

Quite so. I notice particularly from the drawing that the columns will be splendid — all the way up to the best Toorak standards.
