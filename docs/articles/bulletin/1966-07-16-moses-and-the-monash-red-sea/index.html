Moses and the Monash Red Sea

5 min read.

First published 16 Jul 1966

Charlton Heston visits Melbourne on a U.S. State Department goodwill tour, plays exhibition tennis against Frank Sedgman at Grace Park as a Bethesda Hospital fund-raiser, and gives poetry readings at Monash University and other venues, finishing with Robert Frost's 'Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening', which he had read on a TV memorial program two days after President Kennedy's assassination.

There was a sort of beautiful Harold Holt in the U.S. While Charlton Heston conducted a role, it will be noted, the State Department chose Australia as marvellous. Why was Heston chosen for this role, as he was for all those other imposing films? In his Gothic features there is a feeling of nobility, rectitude, and perhaps honesty. In Melbourne he was met by the mayor. Dame Pattie Menzies said that 'Chuck' took her out to dinner in London several years ago. At that time Heston's contract stipulated that he had to travel with a Rolls-Royce and chauffeur. But he became very fed up with this over-imposing chariot, so he bought himself an E-type Jaguar.

One day your correspondent rang and asked in his best obsequious voice: 'Would you have Mr Heston's Jaguar brought here to the front door?'

And that was the last he saw of his Jaguar. Eventually it turned up at Brighton. Thus he told the story, and one gathers immediately that Heston sets up exhibition tennis matches for himself wherever he goes. Your correspondent was even offered a go at him at the Albert Ground — but Heston turned up in a Davis Cup jacket with matching Davis Cup shorts. He said that if ever I learned to hit the ball properly, there was a U.S. shield to go on the pocket. He then asked with amusement about Australian players: 'Yes, in the past fortnight I have played with Laver, Rosewall, Segura, Buchholz, Santana ... I guess for a bad player I've played with more good players than anyone alive. You know, the other day I was playing doubles with Kenny Rosewall and Bob Howe. As we were grouping, I said to Kenny — and I consider this a great thing — "The last time I was here, it was as the winner of a fixed chariot race."'

When he reads the Declaration of Independence he describes Thomas Jefferson as the only true genius ever to occupy the White House. Then he tells the famous story of the night President Kennedy entertained a very distinguished body of scientists and the President said: 'This is the most brilliant assembly gathered in this room since Thomas Jefferson dined alone.'

The State Department's tour he has given his audiences here has been straight down the line, very American, very cultured, very readable. He has read early anonymous Australian convict poems. He has read 'The Man from Snowy River,' a poem about Sydney Harbour by Charles Higham, Mark Twain's Journal on his visit to Australia, some of Thomas Wolfe and the last chapter of Moby Dick, when Captain Ahab meets the whale and tells how he first comes from timber country in the mountains of Missouri, where as a boy his father used to read to him. That's how he gained his love for acting and for reading aloud.

He said that when performing on stage he was acting for an audience, but this way in a reading he felt there was more direct communication.

He is no Charles Laughton, no Sir Cedric Hardwicke, and certainly no Sir Laurence Olivier, but he has a powerful Ben-Hurish command of language.

His best reading was 'Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening,' by Robert Frost, and here he was at his absolute best. He said that when President Kennedy was assassinated he read it two days later on a TV memorial program. This always wound up the reading:

The woods are lovely, dark and deep, But I have promises to keep, And miles to go before I sleep, And miles to go before I sleep.

On the court, Heston was easily as imposing as El Cid, 6ft 3in., narrow hips, far more impressive than those of Mr Nicklaus; and he was very destructive with his overhead volleys. His serve was excellent, plenty of spin and bounce. However, he was rather slow around the backline. He would make about B grade Pennant. He played an exhibition match at the Grace Park courts with Frank Sedgman as a fund-raiser for the Bethesda Hospital, $2 a head. They had a marvellous gallery, mostly of middle-aged matrons. Sedgman won 6-1, 6-4. Heston, however, was very gallant. 'I made a good effort to make him look good, but my opponent doesn't return it.'

The ladies presented him with a tennis racquet in a kangaroo fur cover and he gave a poetry reading. These have been very much the pattern all round Australia. He puts his audience at ease with the line: 'This is quite a change for me ... the last time I was here, it was as the winner of a fixed chariot race.'

Someone at the Melbourne Club said, 'The club has never seen so much culture. What there is is usually in the bar.' At Monash, 1000 students went wild and several girls fainted. At one stage he found it impossible to force his way through the crowd. As someone suggested, if he was able to part the Red Sea with such ease, why was he having a problem here?

Source: Around Melbourne: Moses and the Monash Red Sea, The Bulletin, 16 July 1966. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
