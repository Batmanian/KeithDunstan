Will Howling Ruffians and Unsexed Women Threaten Melbourne Again?

5 min read.

First published 11 Dec 1971

The Victorian Government's moves to extend late-night shopping hours provoke a debate about the character of Melbourne's city centre after dark, drawing on Victorian-era warnings about howling ruffians and unsexed women to argue that fears about deregulated shopping hours are as overblown now as they were a century ago.

THE MINISTER for Labor and Industry has the thoroughly incomparably charming name of Mr. Rafferty. And for the past five months now he has tried to apply Rafferty's Rules to Melbourne's shopping hours. The Government decision was almost beyond credibility. Shops could open virtually any time. The Italian Rules football did not apply to Saturday afternoon shopping. In order to keep some air of fact the most religious events of Melbourne were surfacing — in the Italian Rules football.

Concern came from almost everybody except the buying customers. The Retail Traders' Association had the awesome warning that late shopping would mean a 15 percent increase in oil prices. The shop assistants held their annual convention at the Sidney Myer Music Bowl. Many of us thought there was a certain appropriateness to this choice of venue.

The retail unions got together and tried to restrict late shopping to Friday nights, and all of that. Friday seemed altogether marvellous. In honor of the shop assistants, the retailers introduced a six-page supplement. It told of the appalling days in the 1930s when shops were open in Melbourne on Friday nights to 9.30 p.m. 'Just as the rest of other human beings the world over (Tokyo, London, Singapore, Hong Kong) Singapore were open until 9.30 or later — five nights a week. In Greenwich Village, New York, you could buy a six-page supplement at 9 a.m. and Bett a silk purse at midnight.'

'The city centre at night was beautiful: gay — there were pop groups, music, quite glamorous.' Mrs Gai Chen who gave children rides in an enormous paddleboat in the city said she solemnly commented that the crowd was equal to what it was when the all-nighters was the rule. Mr J. W. K. Pratt, the manager of the Myer complex, backed the system. He announced his shops, if possible, would open almost every night. If his shops stayed open Friday nights, they might not.

Just as he was ready to go two State Health Reports came out. They found that no employer could require or compel any employee to work after 9 p.m. from Monday to Thursday. Who can compel? The employee, of course. It seemed that Sir Henry Bolte and Mr. Rafferty had not, as Wages Boards actually are part of the Labor and Industry Department, given the unions and employer representatives to settle the question amongst themselves.

Mr Henry was furious and said: 'It seems that two men outside Parliament have more power than Parliament itself. That's an intolerable situation.' Mr Rafferty has now announced that Parliament may suspend the Wages Board decisions. If he did this it would almost certainly be a grand clash with the unions.

Yet perhaps Sir Henry should remember the campaign. The earliest movements was one of Melbourne's most famous battles. Back in the 1880s, when Melbourne really used to swing, shops were open every night except Sunday and until very late on Saturday nights. The campaign, and all of these young girls, had to work 15 hours a day and sleep eight in a room on the premises.

The Storemen and Assistants' Union started its campaign in 1891 and it was eventually in 1895 introduced a famous Bill to regulate factories and shops. There had been earlier attempts, in 1880, but there was a loophole. Local agents had the shops open for forty hours. Some shops, particularly those in Flinders Street, paid terrible salaries.

In late March and April there were famous scenes in some of the Melbourne works. Smith Street, Collingwood, had about 6,000 people rampaging the area. The shops that closed were cheered, those that didn't were given treatment — abuse and prolonged groaning. The police had a terrible time, as if they still remained open usually they tried to walk policemen around the shop the area with them closed. 'The howling ruffians and unsexed females,' as the 'Age' called them, also frequented river in Collingwood and Fitzroy until quite a late hour — 'they appear to have rendered the effects of the police. The state of the streets is entirely abortive.' Windows were smashed, mud was thrown, insults assaulted, and threats of personal violence made in Collingwood.

If this region of terror is to continue, Mr Rafferty says, Melburnians will not have to seek as far afield as the 1880s. Melbourne is still capable of anarchy and mob rule.'

And so, with shopping here closing, and over the next few years the night must be opened to some extent.

One wouldn't like to predict that Sir Henry Bolte is going to plan a savage onslaught of unsexed women and howling ruffians. Dr Francis Crowles wrote a letter to the 'Sun News Pictorial': 'And who will benefit from the extended longer shopping hours. And who wants it? Is it to give life to the city? Cannot of the weekend? Does Melbourne actually suffer in comparison to the rest of the world? Why should it want to change? More cars, more congestion, more traffic, more pollution. What is wanted is the utter perfection of the few peaceful, restful cities left in the world.'

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Will Howling Ruffians and Unsexed Women Threaten Melbourne Again?, The Bulletin, 11 December 1971. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
