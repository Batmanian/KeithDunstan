How to Be a Stylish Gold Digger Without Getting Your Feet Muddy

4 min read.

First published 10 Apr 1971

A tour of Victoria's competing goldfield tourist towns, from Wedderburn's fossicking pond to Ballarat's Sovereign Hill historical park, where the gold is purchased from a bank and the dunny wall is already growing honeysuckle.

ONE OF the most fascinating battles of tourism is taking place for the goldfield towns. The goldfield towns are fighting it out dish for dish, windlass for windlass. Beechworth has its annual gold fossicking competition. On March 28, Wedderburn had its annual gold dig. The whole town reverted to the 1852 vintage scene with females wandering around in costumes of the era. But right on the spot where gold was found a century ago, the Wedderburn team dished up 600,000 gallons of lore, a mining dish for 20 cents an hour, and dig your spade into a likely pile of 'fill' provided by the hive. The sight was unbelievable. At times there were nigh on 2,000 people around that pond, all panning like crazy. Although few dressed correctly for the part, if one really wanted to show a little upsmanship the proper gear was very, very old jeans, a Jackie Howe singlet and a rusted panning dish. Some lucky people panned pieces of gold that could be seen through a magnifying glass.

Wedderburn keeps up the tourist battle during the year with a coach filled with grocery items which would have been on sale circa 1910. Barry Humphries would go into tears of nostalgia over the display. They have supplies of Sloan's family liniment, Mother Seigel's digestive syrup, Woodward's celebrated gripe water, Zebra stove polish and, of course, quantities of Frank Miller's renowned black harness soap.

Bendigo on the other hand is well behind in the race, but the Council has bought the Central Deborah goldmine right in the main street, and this is being restored as a brilliant tourist trap. Bendigonians feel they will out-authenticate Ballarat, which, really, has only built a 'stage set.' However, your correspondent has just visited the Ballarat historical park, and it is becoming a success story beyond all Ballarat's sweetest dreams. When they opened the park on November 29, they budgeted on 175,000 visitors in the first year. They have had better than 140,000 in the first four months and they are getting around 5,000 every Sunday.

The historical park has a mine, mining machinery, a reconstruction of a street of the era, and a stream going through your panning dish, some earth salted with a little gold, and a lesson from an expert in the utterly correct panning style. The gold that goes into the earth is not found at Ballarat, it is purchased from a bank. The stream is a pretty one with firm, pebbled concreted edges so one can pan nicely without getting muddy.

Somehow the whole place looked a little too new, too clean, too well designed, not a hair out of place. One would have liked to have seen a bit of dirt, a bit of dung in the main street. The director, Mr. Brian Whykes, was a little put out by this criticism, but you must realise we're not critical of the overall idea. It is only ten years old — it is in its 1860s.

In the main street there are such authentic items as a store, Johnny Ah Loo's Chinese restaurant, the 'Ballarat Times,' Cobb and Co., the blacksmith's shop and the bank. Since they opened they have added a Chinese Joss house and a weatherboard miner's cottage. The cottage looks as oven fresh as a Doncaster display home. Mr. Whykes, however, says that soon it will be correctly furnished and out the back it will have a classically perfect country 'dunny.' Indeed, they have already struck the honeysuckle which will grow up the dunny wall.

Now under construction is a fire-brigade building which will house an 1853 vintage fire engine. After that will come the New York Bakery and Tea Rooms, plus the United States Hotel, which will be licensed, adjoining the Victoria Theatre, just as they were in old Ballarat. You will appreciate that Lola Montez performed at the Victoria Hotel, that she waited for Harry Seekamp, editor of the 'Ballarat Times.' Right there she thrashed him with a horsewhip and Harry Seekamp tried to horsewhip her back until he was stopped by the miners.

The plan is to, perhaps, re-enact this scene every weekend for the tourists. They could put it on, say 3 p.m. on Saturdays and 3 p.m. on Sundays. This should beat the hell out of all other gold towns. People will come for miles to see an editor being whipped. Flagellation is terribly in just now.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: How to Be a Stylish Gold Digger Without Getting Your Feet Muddy, The Bulletin, 10 April 1971. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
