Please Don't Rob the Poor Man of His Beer

5 min read.

First published 19 Jun 1971

A defence of Melbourne's drinkers ahead of a threatened Budget tax rise on beer, surveying the legal alternatives open to the conscientious objector, from home-brew kits and SOHBA lobbying to fruit wine, Zugspitze Birnenwein, and the million gallons of backyard wine made every year in Victoria.

I HAVE always felt it a fearful injustice that the weak should have to carry the entire nation on their backs. We pay for our weaknesses — the innocent, kindly drinkers, gamblers and cigarette smokers are now suffering an intolerable burden. Cyril Pearl in his book 'Beer, Glorious Beer,' said there was an outcry in 1890 when the Victorian Government imposed a tax of threepence on a gallon of beer. The 'Australian Brewers' Journal' described it as a 'crushing impost' and urged that it be reduced to a penny.

Now the tax on beer is $1.375 a gallon and every Canberra political Cassandra is warning that it will go up to $1.4575 a gallon in the coming Budget. Statements are now pouring out from Bouverie Street, Carlton, such as: 'The brewing industry is staggered...' and: 'We find it hard to credit that the Government could seriously plan such a savage penalty.'

Here in Victoria a bottle of beer costs 42 cents. If this new tax does go on it will cost us 47 cents. Out of this Mr. Snedden will get 24 cents. So the beer drinker, after drinking more than half the bottle, will be able to say to himself or herself: 'I have just drunk all that to help support a civil servant or one of our boys in Vietnam.'

You unselfish beer drinkers this financial year will contribute $385 million to the Federal Treasury. If the new tax goes on, and you wish to continue to support the Government at the same altruistic pace, it will mean an extra $108 million to Mr. Snedden. According to the Carlton brewery, the industry pays $40 million company tax on top of this, and the entire profit from the industry in a given year is only $40 million. But what interests me is how uncomplaining we are. Mr. Pearl gives the ancient quote: 'But damn their eyes if they ever tries to rob the poor man of his beer,' yet the drinkers appear to be the last of the protesters to march on Canberra.

What is the alternative for the conscientious objector, for the drinking card burner, for the alcohol lover who conscientiously believes that he should be able to take his stimulant without penalty?

First, there are the home brewers. According to law you are forbidden to make a home brew with an alcohol content of more than 2 percent. The home brewers actually have their own pressure group, the Society of Home Brewers of Australia, known better and more beautifully as SOHBA. They even have their own home brewers' journal. SOHBA has petitioned the Minister for Customs and Excise, Mr. Chipp, for a rise in alcohol content. Our friends at the brewery insist that home brewers are making more than 20 million gallons a year and their numbers are multiplying at an alarming rate. A friend who had been working on these figures said: 'If you think any home brewers are sticking to the 2 percent rule then you are out of your mind.'

The gear has become much easier than it used to be. There are now home-brew kits available at grocers and even health food stores. They come in packets, which look like 'ground-up breakfast food.' All you need is a large plastic rubbish bin for your brewing and an ability to add water at the proper stages. If you stick to the instructions, you get a very pleasant 2 percent beer; your pack costs about $3 to make 10 gallons or 60 bottles. That is, five cents a bottle. But it requires little brain power to discover that if you make only five gallons from that kit you get a four percent beer at 10 cents a bottle. You are still magnificently in front.

What else is available for conscientious objectors? The fruit wines are still untaxed. At the last Lilydale show there was a fine range of highly alcoholic wines made from cherries, oranges, apples, peaches, carrots, strawberries and even quinces. The quince wine was very tasty. Wynns are now doing a splendid flagon trade in what they call apple wine. They say it is twice the alcoholic content of beer. Untaxed, it sells for $1.50 a half gallon compared with $1.85 to $1.95 for the regular red or white taxed wines. There is also a country cider, 12 to 15 percent alcohol, which is $1.25 per half gallon. W. Salter and Son of Angaston, S.A., sell a riesling-like drink called, glory be, Zugspitze Birnenwein. It comes in a beautifully labelled hock bottle and sells for 70 cents.

What else? Huge numbers of Victorians buy wine grapes at the Victorian market during the vintage season and make their own. Under the present law you are permitted to make 400 gallons free of tax. You can make excellent wine this way for less than 20 cents a bottle. It is estimated that a third to a quarter of all the wine made in Victoria is produced in backyards — more than a million gallons.

Am I being of any help? Could I on behalf of all conscientious alcoholics make a plea to Mr. Snedden. We have done our bit. Why not try some fresh avenues? How about tea, instant coffee, all the highly litterable soft drinks that come in tins, or why not a special tax on pornography and sex books? It's a wonder you haven't thought of that.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Please Don't Rob the Poor Man of His Beer, The Bulletin, 19 June 1971. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
