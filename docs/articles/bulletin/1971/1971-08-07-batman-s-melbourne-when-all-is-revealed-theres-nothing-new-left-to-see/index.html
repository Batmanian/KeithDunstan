When All is Revealed There's Nothing New Left to See

5 min read.

First published 07 Aug 1971

The annual cat-and-mouse game between General Motors-Holden and Australia's press reaches its peak with the launch of the new HQ Holden, described through elaborate factory security, undercover photography at the Dandenong plant, and the spectacular stunt of dangling the new models from BHP-ESSO helicopters over Bass Strait for a TV commercial.

One of the most enjoyable games of monopoly that takes place every year is the peek-a-boo find-the-Holden game. It is conducted with all the drama and cloak-and-dagger secrecy with which the U.S. Government likes to lay bare its Pentagon Papers. In other years we have had photographers waiting at various corners for weeks for new models to pass by. There was the year when a motoring magazine got a job for one of their photographers at the assembly plant so that he could conduct undercover espionage.

Security at all the GM-H assembly plants in Sydney, Melbourne and South Australia is intense. GM-H men claim, for example, it is virtually impossible to crack the plant at Dandenong. There, all humans in and out are screened with all the care of Viet Cong guerillas going into a subterranean lair. Your best chance of getting a peep at a new model is from the golf course overlooking the Pagewood plant in Sydney. The ABC 'This Day Tonight' team even did a telecast from the golf course. The publicity derived from this sort of thing is splendid.

Pre-release photographs of 'Here's your new Holden' appeared in the Melbourne 'Herald,' the 'Daily Telegraph,' the Adelaide 'Advertiser,' the 'Advertiser,' the Sydney 'Sun' and the 'Sun News-Pictorial,' just to mention a few. The first photographs and story, syndicated across the nation, appeared on April 10, 1971.

How did they get the pictures? A workman took a camera into the plant at Dandenong. He had it hidden in a lunch box with a little flap over the lens and it was all operated by remote control. Came the photographs: all the engine power, the perimeter-frame chassis, the coil springs all round, the engine, the anti-dive geometry, the steering lock, etc. Everything was accurate except they said the Brougham would retain its old name. They didn't realise that GM-H would come up with an excitingly original Australian name like 'Statesman de Ville.'

Meanwhile, GM-H continued to maintain security. Tests were conducted with what they called 'Q' cars. These were new-model HQ Holdens inside old bodies. Five Q cars drove to Darwin via Cloncurry and back to Melbourne down the centre. Indeed, most of the year these Q cars drove around Melbourne, unknown to the public, like an unrevealed Vladimir Petrov on the move.

There were a few more clever feats of newspaper espionage until July 8, exactly a week before the new models were revealed at Press lunches. This time the photographs were so clear, of such good quality, one wondered if they were really 'Pentagon leaks' from GM-H. GM-H denies all, and a spokesman said they fought to maintain security to the end. But the pictures were acquired so easily. An anonymous donor said he was passing them out gratis because he had a grudge against GM-H.

Actually the most skilled operation was the making of the TV commercial. You must appreciate it is an agony finding new ideas. In the past GM-H has had Holdens thrashing across deserts, Holdens alone on the tops of mountain crags, Holdens charging through waterfalls — what on earth could they do this year? The gentlemen of George Patterson Pty. Ltd. found the idea — why not have each one of the new models hanging from helicopters out over Bass Strait?

Planning for all this began in February. It turned out that the only helicopters powerful enough to carry Holdens around the sky were the choppers of the BHP-ESSO oil rig. They were very reluctant and wanted $500 an hour for each of five choppers and furthermore could allocate only two hours between 10 a.m. and noon for one morning in March. Furthermore they made the proviso that should there be any emergency at the rig they would cut the cables immediately, drop the cars in the sea and return.

Came the March morning the weather, blessedly, was fine. The cars hidden under cover on semi-trailers went down to Lochsport, to a lonely spot on the Gippsland coast. There were 40 people in the GM-H George Patterson team, including engineers who had specially constructed cradles designed to reduce the load for lifting.

The helicopters swept in at the precise time and all five cars were aloft in three minutes, including a sixth helicopter which was there for the camera work.

At the first lift one Holden slipped its cradle and nosed down into the earth. They tried again and away they went, in formation at 600ft., out to sea. Then the Monaro began to spin round and round. The pilot became alarmed and at one stage he reported he was considering ditching it in the sea. Yet they got them all photographed and safely back, although the nylon cables had frayed another few minutes and it would have dropped.

Finally all this was put together into one brilliant 60-second TV commercial. The cost: $28,000. The message? Well, we're not sure about that. It could mean that if traffic jams become unbearable this coming summer the new HQ Holdens, for one, could be carted away by helicopter.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: When All is Revealed There's Nothing New Left to See, The Bulletin, 7 August 1971. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
