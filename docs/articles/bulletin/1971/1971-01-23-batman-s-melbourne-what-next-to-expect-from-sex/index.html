What Next to Expect from Sex

5 min read.

First published 23 Jan 1971

A survey of the sex creeping into Melbourne's comic strips, fairy tales, Collins Book Depot's bestseller lists, and even the rejuvenated Sexton Blake detective series, concluding that the day cannot be far off before someone publishes a sex book by Malcolm Muggeridge.

THE SEX MONSTER is creeping upon us from the most unlikely directions. You can get it kinky style from the comic strips. Here in Melbourne we have a daily comic strip called The Heart of Juliet Jones. Juliet Jones and her sister Eve have maybe three romantic affairs a year, but over 18 years, according to my reckoning, 54 of these attachments have not come to fruition. It is clear to all keen observers that the Jones sisters have a lesbian relationship.

The same thing happens in another comic strip concerning an extraordinarily weak-brained girl called Tiffany Jones. Miss Jones has five affairs a year which do not come to fruition and is known to have a lesbian relationship with her roommate, Jo.

The other day I decided to check on the sex-book department. According to Collins Book Depot the number-one seller is cook books, sex is second and do-it-yourself home handyman books come third. But I submit that this categorisation is unfair to sex. The Collins Book spokesman was referring to books on sex which dealt with it purely how-to-do-it, technical business. The thing is, it is becoming virtually impossible to buy a book of any kind that does not contain sex.

Just before Christmas I picked up a nice little paperback of fairy tales. My God, when I got home I found it was called 'Famous Erotic Fairy Tales,' edited by Paul Tabori.

The first story involved a young locksmith and it concerned, of course, a young princess. On page 12 I read: 'With a sudden gesture she clawed at the fastenings of her gown and as she stripped it off it fell to reveal to the young man's staring, hungry eyes a perfect, inviolate body. Its curves were ravishing, the skin as white as the foam of a mountain stream cascading down the slope. But it was spanned by a golden bridge. For the princess wore a chastity belt, enclosing in a cruel embrace her hips, her waist, her sex. "A confoundedly complex lock," said the locksmith when he had managed to tear his eyes away from the swelling breasts, the slightly quivering rosy nipples.'

Do you know what I mean? You don't normally get that sort of stuff in fairy tales.

Well, I decided that surely there was safety in the older, more classic mystery writers. Carefully I shunned the Spillanes, the James Bonds. I picked up a Perry Mason, 'The Case of the Glamorous Ghost' by Erle Stanley Gardner. There was a naked girl on the cover. The story opened: 'Last night was a night of witchery. The moon was full and fragrant wisps of breeze gently rustled the trees and the greenery. George Belmont and Diane Foley were sitting in their parked car. A Wraith, apparently in the nude save for a fluttering diaphanous covering, drifted out of the shadows toward the car.'

Clearly one couldn't go for Perry Mason, but then I noticed that there had been a whole new release of Sexton Blake. Now Sexton Blake dates from 1893 and I always looked upon him as the clean-thinking, decent chap's Sherlock Holmes. It must be agreed that there were some dark areas in Holmes' character — he was arrogant, bad-tempered and a well-known junkie. But Blake was the upright, clean type, the Harry Wharton of detectives.

More than five million words of Sexton Blake were produced, so it was with some interest that I came to the new series. The first Blake I read was called 'The Witches of Notting Hill' by W. A. Ballinger. It opened with a witches scene and a human sacrifice. Ballinger describes a witches coven: 'The boys and girls, naked, their bodies greased with a special elixir, are dancing around an altar. Breasts and buttocks twinkled in the moonlight.'

Now you'll remember Blake's assistant, Tinker, a clean-cut, very nice young chap. Later on, he is unashamedly and willingly seduced in a Jaguar by a lady called Betty Juniper.

The next Blake I picked up was called 'The Laird of Evil,' set in a lonely castle in Scotland in which the laird, Sir Dugald Chlynie, conducted fearful black-mass ceremonies. Even going to the most monstrous lengths, including the ancient custom of droit du seigneur, but inflicting appalling sexual tortures.

'Against a rising chant of expectant glee in the echoing hall down below, there was another sound — the sinister chord in the satanic overture. And the introductory chord of Ann Ramsay's night of hell.'

In my reading, admittedly, Mr. Blake himself has indulged in none of the cheap sexual tricks of Bond. But I fear it won't be long. He has a new assistant, Paula Dane, 27, tall, beautiful, with honey-blonde hair, and skin smooth, and what not.

So what's left, I ask you? Any day now I expect to see a new work titled 'The Strange Perversions of Billy Bunter' or 'Sex at Greyfriars' or even a sex book by Malcolm Muggeridge.
