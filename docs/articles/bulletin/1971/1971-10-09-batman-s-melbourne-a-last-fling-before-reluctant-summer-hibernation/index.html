A Last Fling Before Reluctant Summer Hibernation

3 min read.

First published 09 Oct 1971

A report on the fifth Ron Barassi Memorial Lecture at Monash University, in which Professor Ian Turner argued that Australian Rules Football is inseparable from the national character, a surrogate for warfare and a socially approved outlet for aggression.

BEFORE we pass into the terrible limbo of a football-less summer, it is important to detail for you perhaps the most thought-provoking of the annual lectures at Monash University. It took place on the eve of the grand final and was the fifth Ron Barassi Memorial Lecture, given this year by Professor Ian Turner, Associate Professor of History.

The Professor lives in Richmond and is known for his devotion to such folk heroes as Jack Dyer. He started his lecture by reading telegrams. The first:

AS HOLDER OF ALL WORLD RIGHTS RON BARASSI SUPERSTAR DEMAND YOU END ALL PROJECTED PERFORMANCES OR YOU MAY END UP WITH A NUN.

Signed Harry M. Miller. The second was from the late George Johnston: 'In Melbourne, football is a fever disease like recurrent malaria and evidently incurable. For six or seven months of the year a mad contagion runs through the Press, TV, radio and everyday life. Melbourne has no summer — only a brief idea of hibernation between football seasons.'

He quoted Joseph Strutt, the first historian of British sport, who said that in order to understand a people, it was necessary to study their sports. Dr Turner made it very clear that our character was intimately associated with football.

Even the clergy become deeply involved. He remembered the Reverend Alan Walker's words at the MCG: 'Ladies and gentlemen gathered here on this great occasion, whichever team we may support — the Saints or the Demons — we can all surely agree that we are joined together as brothers in Jesus Christ.' Whereupon there was a mighty roar from the southern stand: 'What about the bloody umpire?'

Donald Horne has said: 'Only in sport and soldiers did Australians see themselves as being in world class.' The Professor agreed with this and, of course, the two things went together. Prime Minister Alfred Deakin said back in 1908 that when Australians were called upon to defend themselves, the battles would already have been won on the playing fields of Melbourne.

The Professor argued that football, like war, provided Australians with one of their few genuine claims to world-class status. Sport and soldiering were, in his analysis, two sides of the same national coin.

He gave a fine example of the game's hold on Melbourne by noting that at the MCG even the clergy were drawn in. He delivered a series of psychiatrist and learned psychologist observations. Football, he said, reflected racial, political, social, sexual and male chauvinist prejudices.

He believed all football-lovers had an aggressive component in their personality. Football was one of the few socially and legally approved outlets. Therefore it needs social approval and sanity — he said he could not quite trust his knowledge that the move was a substitute for warfare, the one meaning of Enlightenment football and Marxist moral standards.

The Professor said that football players first ran on the football field in the nude. He had dug up all available information on this subject. He said: 'This is God's truth and it is something you have never been told by your football commentators.'

The closed for wild cheering.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: A Last Fling Before Reluctant Summer Hibernation, The Bulletin, 9 October 1971. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
