Marriage A La Mode — Spanish, Medieval or Oz

4 min read.

First published 14 Aug 1971

Melbourne's wedding reception industry is booming, with the city's new registry office installed in the Bullion Room of the old Treasury Building and Governor Rohan Delacombe opening Chaucer's, a $750,000 medieval-themed reception complex on Canterbury Road, complete with kosher kitchen, cantilever lavatories and parking for 160 cars in what the owners might reasonably call Arthur's Stables.

Last week The Bulletin ran a cover story about divorce Australia-style, but here in Melbourne we would like you to know that marriage is still terribly strong. Why, last week we even opened a new registry office. There had been a row over the old one. A correspondent to the newspapers had complained of its grimy walls and windows and its austere vintage public-service clock. 'But for the sickly plastic flowers it could be a police interrogation room somewhere in Russia,' she said.

The new registry office is an absolute ripper. It is in the Bullion Room of the stately old building and, with a high ceiling, velvet curtains and cedar furniture, it is utterly beautiful. Television teams were busy covering the first day of marriages in the office and one felt that cameras dwelt a little unkindly on the urgency of one of those marriages.

But the marriage business is booming as never before. Your correspondent has just gone through the pink pages and counted over 90 reception venues in Melbourne. You can take your pick on atmosphere. For example there is Sherbrooke Lodge, 'The romantic atmosphere of an old mountain lodge, marriage ceremonies solemnised in our candlelit garden.' You can go Middle Eastern at Kismet, 'The apex of magnificent entertainment in a setting of unrivalled splendour.' There is Spanish French, German Rhineland, or you can be superbly Dickensian at the Charles Dickens Banquet Room. Or how about going wonderfully Oz at the Sentimental Bloke, which opened last year. The Bloke has a special bridal changing-room. It is very tastefully done with washbasin, shower and bidet, which puts it clearly in advance of other bridal changing-suites in Melbourne.

But wait, this week the Governor, Sir Rohan Delacombe, opened the grandest of them all, Chaucer's, out in Canterbury Road, Canterbury. Actually medieval England is already reasonably covered. We did have a Tudor Court out in Caulfield and a Camelot in Clayton, but no doubt they could joust for the available trade.

You may well ask what Geoffrey Chaucer is doing in the city of Camberwell. Well, Chaucer's is situated between Canterbury Road and Chaucer Street and the idea is that the pilgrims will proceed this way from Camberwell Junction.

Chaucer's cost $750,000 and the theory is that it will be able to handle five or more weddings at the one time. There is one vast room called King Arthur's Grand Hall which will seat 600 for a banquet or take 1330 for a conference. But this will divide up into soundproofed areas. Alternatively you can have the Troubadour's Square, the Griselda Suite, Pilgrim's Court, the Merrie Makers' Square or the Knight's Castle.

Those who expect stone floors, deep roasting on the spit in open fires and dogs everywhere waiting to be tossed a thigh bone will notice there have been a few adjustments. There are many acres of magnificent heraldic carpet going virtually from battlement to battlement. There is 'multi-zoned air-conditioning with twin failure-proof plants,' piped music, multi-lingual translating equipment and built-in facilities for TV coverage.

There is another thing that might have surprised medieval England. Apart from the main kitchen there is also the kosher kitchen. This has its own stainless steel equipment, its own separate room and its own utterly separate cutlery. There is also a terrace the size of a tennis court called the Shalom Garden.

The medieval washrooms are interesting. The knights wash their hands in the Cavalier's Room and the maids go to the Fair Lady's Room. The lavatory bowls are the very latest cantilever style with a foot button to operate the flush. A foot flush gives freedom for those wearing full armour.

There is, of course, a changing-room for the bride, equipped with shower and bath basin, but, unlike the Sentimental Bloke, no bidet. Mr. Rudolph Unger, proprietor of Chaucer's, said it would also double as a private suite for the guest speaker at conventions. One noted that on such occasions it would undergo a sex change and become the Muse's Room.

What else? Well, there's Children's Paradise, complete with cots, rocking horses, mini cantilever lavatories, everything. Folk going to night receptions can leave their children in paradise with Chaucer's nurse. Waitresses wear genuine period-designed hip-length minis. And now there is parking space for 160 cars. I don't know what that's called — Arthur's Stables or Griselda's Saddling Paddock maybe.
