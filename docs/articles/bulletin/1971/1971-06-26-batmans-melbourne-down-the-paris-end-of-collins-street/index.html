Down the Paris End of Collins Street

4 min read.

First published 26 Jun 1971

A lament for the demolition of the Oriental Hotel at the Paris end of Collins Street, whose bar contents sold for almost nothing at auction, and for the announcement of twin 45-storey towers to replace it, designed by I. M. Pei of New York to the indignation of Melbourne's own architects.

YOU WILL appreciate that for many years now your correspondent has filled the high office of honorary anti-progress officer for Collins Street. Even the slightest examples of progress such as the changing of paving stones or the replacement of street lights or even the churlish treatment of a plane tree, he has condemned immediately.

These are very hard times. Next week — in fact the week after the wreckers moved in — comes the final auction of the contents of the old Oriental Hotel. The final auction of the contents was a very sad-making affair. They held it in the Persian Room. In the latter days of the battle to keep the place going the grand old dining-room was converted into a Persian discotheque. It might surprise you to know that Persia was famous for its discotheques, enabling us to receive a room like the sheik's electric guitar-swinging harem.

It was important to attend the auction just to see what would happen to the old Oriental saloon bar. This bar had a quiet, panelled, brown lino charm. Some of us have spent a considerable portion of our lives in that room. There was a very distinguished group always to be found there as soon as it opened at 10 a.m. This group was known as the Morning Tea Club.

First, the auctioneer put the electric clock on sale. That went for only $6. All the shelves, cupboards, panels and the entire back wall brought $15. All the veneer panelling in the main bar area brought a mere $10. Then came the big moment, the sale of the historic bar. The auctioneer said: 'I understand some of you have come especially for this.' The bids started. 'What is it made of?' someone crudely asked. 'I don't know,' replied the auctioneer. 'But it would be very solid.' There was a bid of $2, then $3 and by cajoling, pleading, almost crying he got it to $5.

So now that's all gone, and the Oriental is a silent, empty hulk, waiting for death. For nigh on two years the future of the three acres bounded by Exhibition Street, Collins Street and Flinders Lane has been a beautifully kept secret. One time we thought Qantas would build a hotel there, but Qantas is not as optimistic as it used to be.

Last week, we heard the news — ANZ, AMP and Mainline Corporation will build a whopping $87 million project with twin towers going up 45 storeys, 600ft. high, almost twice as high as their neighbour, the CRA building. The ANZ tower will have a three-storey banking hall and 42 storeys for bank administration, renting, etc. The other great tower, to be called Collins Tower, will have 30 floors of office space and 15 floors for a 400-room hotel.

The Lord Mayor, Mr. Ted Best, said: 'Magnificent. Absolutely magnificent. Just what the city needs.' There were also some headlines which said something like 'A Lift for the Paris End of Collins Street.'

Anything less like Paris would be hard to imagine. Paris has always kept tall buildings away from the centre of the city. The lofty creations are several miles out in areas which, one assumes, are known as the Melbourne end of Paris.

The Oriental used to feature umbrellas and tables on the pavement, and served only tea, milk and cream cakes to its customers — it's a sin here to drink in the open — and leafy Collins Street might have had a faint air of Paris. Since then a string of tall buildings have been erected on either side to be capped within a few years by these monstrous money-boxes.

The new project has received little praise from Melbourne's architects, because once again they have been spurned. The architects who will provide the plans and ideas are I. M. Pei and Partners of New York. Robin Boyd said: 'It seems to confirm a popular belief that major Australian buildings need American design. How can Australian cities develop identities when their biggest elements are handed out to overseas stars?'

Meanwhile the Lord Mayor, at a special Press conference, announced Melbourne's very own song. One of the so-meaningful verses ran:

A tram on a hill A train rattling under a bridge The swans on a lake, A ship sailing slowly up the bay All around with light, This is Melbourne This is Melbourne.

The song of course has the wrong approach. A city which has no city plan, a city which willy-nilly increases its congestion with 45-storey towers, yet still fails to build a subway, might do better to chant a song (sung to a well-known tune) which could begin this way:

Somewhere over the rainbow Subways ply. Sydney, London, big cities have subways, Why, oh why, can't we try?

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Down the Paris End of Collins Street, The Bulletin, 26 June 1971. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
