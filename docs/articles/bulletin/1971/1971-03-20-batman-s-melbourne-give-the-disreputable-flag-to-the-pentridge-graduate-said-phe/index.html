Give the Disreputable Flag to the Pentridge Graduate, Said Phelan

5 min read.

First published 20 Mar 1971

The history of Melbourne's St Patrick's Day processions, from the triumphs of Archbishop Mannix and his fourteen Victoria Cross winners in 1920 to the 1971 edition, which has been reduced to a Summer Happening at the Myer Music Bowl with proceeds to the Yooralla School.

PROCESSIONS have suffered greatly in Melbourne these past 20 years. We used to have a glorious expression of the eight-hour-day movement, the grand old trade union parade that stepped and strutted and dwindled and dwindled until Myers, Coles and Woolworths stepped in and launched their own march. That was a bitter piece of irony.

And now there is St. Patrick's Day. Did you realise this year it is no more? Last year, Archbishop Knox said, 'I wonder if these shows need more scripting.' Think of the past. Think of the glorious old Green and Orange duels. Think of the battles in the pubs.

Here is a body that understands the colour and dash and splendour and all the available passions. Also it is ready-made for Melbourne in that it has almost nothing to do with actual religion.

In those times of rising crime rates, in these times of soaring inflation, St. Patrick's Day marches used to happen in Melbourne. You could take the tram to town on a fine blue Saturday in mid-March and there they were — Hibernians in their regalia, step-dancers, bands, the Knights of the Southern Cross, and the flags of Ireland. In the 1920s these marches were extraordinary. There were sometimes 30,000 people.

The St. Patrick's procession, the great Melbourne event, started a little surprisingly. There were originally no marches. St. Patrick's Day was always the occasion for large picnics in the bush on the outskirts of Melbourne. That, at any rate, is what historical records say. Everything was festive and serene until March 17, 1843, the year Melbourne was less than ten years old, when a fight broke out between a band of Irishmen and two men who objected to the green. This introduced a sense of liveliness which has never fully departed.

In Rosina Hullah's history of the day, which traces the story from 1843 to 1920, the battle of the pubs and the march is vividly described. When was the first parade? There appears to be some dispute about this. But the tradition seems to have been firmly established by around 1850.

The Orange Institution always used to provide the best counterpoint. Their marchers would be proceeding one way, on the other side of the road the St. Patrick's marchers would be going in the opposite direction. The eyes of the two processions would swivel, menacing exchanges would be made. The Orangemen, who were not likely to attack unless they were in a majority of 50 to one, would chant: 'Thirty-three years in your eyes said the man who looked in.' Melbourne had only a small number of Orangemen. No wonder it works.

In 1880 there was a violent incident, serious enough for the authorities to ban all party processions and that was the end of the parade for 25 years.

Ah, but it came back stronger than ever. Because of an outbreak of violence in 1897 the Chief Secretary — much to the fury of the march committee — decided to provide the protection of the mounted police. As the committee put it, this was a rotten slur on Irishmen, who were well able to defend themselves, and flattering to the Orangemen, who were not likely to attack unless they were in a majority of 50 to one.

Unquestionably the greatest parade of them all was in 1920. There had been no march in 1919 because of the influenza epidemic, and the feeling around the city between Catholic and non-Catholic had a fine flavour because of the attitudes of Archbishop Mannix on the Irish and conscription during 1916-17. At one stage there was a move to ban the march, but the Lord Mayor said it could go on provided the Union Jack was 'emblazoned at the head of the march.'

Here the Archbishop, with the aid of John Wren, pulled off a coup that has never been forgotten. Archbishop Mannix led the parade, escorted by 14 Victoria Cross winners mounted on white horses. When they reached Federal Parliament House, the Archbishop and his VC winners took the salutes of 6000 troops, 140 horsemen, and 10,000 others.

The troops carried two Australian flags. As for the emblazoning of the Union Jack, the 'Argus' was caustic. Oh, it was there, all right, but not at the front of the procession. One didn't see the tiny flag until half an hour after the start, and then it was on a horse-drawn float at the corner of a St. Patrick banner.

The Government was terrified of trouble. They brought in 540 police from all over Victoria to stand by. Nothing happened.

The next year, Archbishop Mannix was away in Rome and the City Council insisted on the Union Jack 'out front.' Bishop Phelan, the Catholic administrator, observed the request, but he picked 'the most disreputable man in Melbourne' to carry 'the disreputable flag — and in tatters.'

This caused something that Phelan described as a 'so-called rebuff' to the city fathers and he intimated that if they wanted a similar observance next year, a recent convict from Pentridge would carry the flag.

Archbishop Mannix was back for the 1922 procession and he soothed matters with the council. It went on in strength, usually 20,000 taking part. Archbishop Mannix was always there. He led the parade in 1963 — the year he died at the age of 99.

So what happened this year? There was a St. Patrick's 'Summer Happening' at Melbourne Myer Music Bowl, with proceeds going to the Yooralla School for crippled children. I tell you, this city is going into a decline.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Give the Disreputable Flag to the Pentridge Graduate, Said Phelan, The Bulletin, 20 March 1971. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
