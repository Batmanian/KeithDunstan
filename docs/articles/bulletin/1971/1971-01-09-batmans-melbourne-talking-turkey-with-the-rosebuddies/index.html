Talking Turkey with the Rosebuddies

5 min read.

First published 09 Jan 1971

A Christmas visit to the Rosebud camping strip on Port Phillip Bay finds Melbourne's herd instinct on vivid display, with caravans equipped with double beds, air-conditioning and walkie-talkies, and the regulars boasting they haven't missed a season in 45 years.

THE HERD instinct is extremely well refined in the Australian human being. Psychologists have often noted how we have a terror of being away from our own group; left alone, we suffer from cultural shock.

If this isn't true, how do you explain Rosebud? Australia has more open space than anywhere else on earth. We have at least 10,000 miles of coastline, yet the favourite spot for Melburnians is Rosebud.

Rosebud is about 50 miles from Melbourne, a narrow strip on Port Phillip Bay; a flat, safe beach, no scenic grandeur, no dramatic bush for bush walks — main entertainment an aquarium, mini-golf, trampolines, hoop-la and lucky wheels.

In Myer's language I like to call it our biggest Outdoorium; it could be the biggest outdoorium in the world. The narrow strip between road and sea runs for 33 miles and in that area there are 994 camping sites and nigh on 5000 people. It has been suggested that it is the greatest gathering on sand since the filming of 'Beau Geste' or 'Forty Thousand Horsemen.'

Over Christmas the population on this area of the peninsula goes up to 50,000, so for those who fear loneliness it is just as if they were in Swanston or Bourke Streets.

The shops stay open day and night, Christmas Day, Boxing Day, every day. Local butchers and chemists give a shiver should even a cloud go across the sun. They want no one to go home. The chops and suntan lotion sold in these weeks pay for the holidays in August.

This time there were a good many clouds. Christmas Day and Boxing Day were fine, but for the next three days the rain poured down and the weather was so cold it seemed there was a possibility of a White Christmas.

You are probably saying that Rosebud, close to Melbourne, provides an ideal camping holiday for those on a low income. Now, this is the interesting point — at Rosebud, all varieties of income are mixed in together and a large proportion of them, if not well heeled, are splendidly well tyred.

On a run round through the teatree among the camps, I saw one of those highly sought-after 4.2 litre Jaguars, several large Mercedes, a Pontiac Parisienne, several new Ford Galaxies, and no end of GT Monaros and GT Falcons.

It is one of the great holiday sights in Australia to drive along the Nepean Highway. Theoretically, the campers leave Melbourne to get away from it all. But then, of course, there is the fear that this might happen, so they put out their name-plates. Please do not think that these name-plates are crudely scrawled on a plain piece of timber. It is de rigueur to have your name-plate professionally painted. Some are beautifully done with antique lettering and supported with wrought-iron stands — the Smithers, the Watsons, the Brady Bunch.

I went to the camp on a hunch that with the rain one would observe suffering among the campers equivalent to that of Pakistani refugees. But camping has come on with the years and is now highly competitive. For example, there was one camp owned by a man from Springvale. For this he had an enormous fly erected on a solid steel frame, which provided beautiful overhanging eaves. Under this he had a prefabricated house, each unit the same size for storing on a trailer. This was wire-screened and inside there was wall-to-wall carpet.

He had a double bed with laminex headboard. He had television, proper wardrobe, chest of drawers, kitchen dresser, refrigerator and an L-shaped bar to separate the living-room from the dream kitchen. He preferred not to use the camp showering facilities, so he had a private shower recess with slatted floor, a changing area and a tank overhead equipped with immersion heater.

Most have outdoor patios and proper stoves, either gas or electric. I asked one lady if she had a ham salad for Christmas dinner. 'Certainly not,' said she, 'we had hot turkey with all the trimmings. We don't lower standards just because we're camping.'

The most noble set-up was provided by the Hunter family from Thornbury. Mr and Mrs Hunter are Rosebud veterans. They came with their sons Robert and Brian, who are the current world go-kart champions. They had three caravans, three speed boats and a beach buggy. The main caravan was 34 feet 6 inches long. To this they attached an outside awning which gave it a veranda at least as large as that of the old Esplanade Hotel in Perth.

This caravan had a shower, lavatory, kitchen, several bedrooms, and air-conditioning units at either end. It was a great wonder to all Rosebuddies how this mighty thing was manoeuvred through the teatree and banksia to the sea. It was done with walkie-talkies, Hunters front and rear calling instructions. The progress, 50 yards from the road, took four hours.

Yes, the Rosebuddies are almost like a club. Some of them haven't missed coming for 45 years and they even come down with their neighbours from either side. Bookings for Christmas campsites open on September 15. Every campsite is taken on that day, by the time the first mail is opened.

The price is $9 a week without power and $13 a week with full power. Even if you do own a Mark X Jag, I suppose this isn't a bad deal. Some of the nice people a few miles farther up the road at Portsea are paying $220 a week for their shacks.
