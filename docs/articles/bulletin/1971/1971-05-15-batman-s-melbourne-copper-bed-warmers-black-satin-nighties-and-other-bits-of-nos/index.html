Copper Bed-Warmers, Black Satin Nighties and Other Bits of Nostalgia

5 min read.

First published 15 May 1971

Melbourne's competitive nostalgia industry, from a South Yarra gallery showing the history of bed-warming devices to the new Old Melbourne Motor Inn at Flemington Road fitted out with chandeliers from the demolished Regent Theatre, and the National Trust's campaign to raise the HMVS Cerebus from Black Rock.

COULD I give you some of the latest examples of our competitive nostalgia? Here in Melbourne we like to think that we can out-nostalge almost anyone on earth. Last week Gallery A, South Yarra, had an opening, not of the latest in painting but of the history of bed-warming devices. They ranged from the grand long-handled copper bed-warmers through to electric blankets and girls in exciting black satin nighties. They also had a marvellous range of antique electric radiators. Some were made of copper and looked rather like the face of Luna Park. Remember those radiators with the artificial coals made of chunks of red glass? There was nothing like looking into the faces of fires made of artificial coal.

They had one of these, circa 1925. They turned it on and splendidly managed to fuse every light in the place.

Another piece of nostalgia which will be ready in about three weeks is the $4 million Old Melbourne Motor Inn at Flemington Road, North Melbourne. This you must see. The idea is to have the ultimate up-to-the-minute hotel with swimming-pool and all, plus 9,300 square feet of air-conditioning, in a hotel that looks exactly like a lovable old piece of terraced Carlton from the 1880s.

Commodore Hotels have put up the money and this is how they have done it. It is built around a courtyard in the terrace style with balconies going up six storeys. They have enough vintage cast iron on display to restore all Paddo and Carlton. And where you can't see cast iron, there's bluestone. It's everywhere. They bought an old Methodist church in Fitzroy to get two tons of bluestone.

Then, as for the decor, much of it came from the late-departed Regent Theatre in Collins Street. All the elegant foyer furniture, the marble-topped tables and all the chandeliers, except one. The biggest of them all wouldn't fit. George Frew, managing director of Commodore, discovered he would have needed a drop of five feet more.

Old Melbourne, almost automatically you will start looking round for the front stalls. There are 248 bedrooms and so far Mr. Frew has gathered over 100 brass bedsteads. Very likely he has ruined the brass-bedstead trade for all time. Only ten years ago you couldn't even get the Brotherhood of St. Lawrence to cart them away; now they have the absolute pride of window space in those chic antique shops in Malvern Road and they sell like refectory tables. The hotel is on the site of Ma Dalley's junkyard. She will be commemorated with Ma's tea and coffee shop, so those who used to deal in scrap iron with our famous Ma won't be able nostalgically to drink tea in her memory.

Next in the nostalgia stakes we have a beauty. The National Trust has launched a campaign to raise and restore the old Cerebus, which is sunk as a breakwater off Black Rock.

The American Confederate raider, Shenandoah, bristling with guns, came to Port Phillip in 1865. This impressed on us the thought that we would need our own magnificent ship of the line sailing back and forth in front of the heads, daunting all who came near. In 1866 we sent off a man to London and ordered the most modern battle vessel of the day. A brilliant young naval architect designed for us a ship of utterly radical design — the first British battleship to dispense with sail power, the first with armour plate raised to protect the gun turrets, the first with a long low profile to make a difficult target. She was 225ft. in length, with a 45ft. beam, 9in. armourplate and 10in. muzzle-loading guns housed in two turrets and they fired 400lb. shells.

In April 1870, Lieutenant Parker, RN, went from Melbourne to London to take delivery. Now, Cerebus had 1,900 tons above the waterline and 1,800 tons of coal in the bunkers and a marvellous list. Most of the crew deserted en route from Chatham to Plymouth. Then, two days out of Plymouth, she ran into such a gale she was rolling 45 degrees. At Gibraltar further deserters said they would rather go to jail than continue on to Australia. Yet after 123 days the grand flagship of Her Majesty's Victorian Navy finally arrived at Port Phillip. One paper described her as looking like 'an elongated gasometer fitted with masts.'

What did she do? Well, she looked imposing at Williamstown. Every year she would go off with her torpedo boats for a mock battle against the forts at Queenscliff and Point Nepean. The only shot fired in anger, so to speak, was in 1878 when she killed an officer and three of her own men in a mine explosion. She was laid off in 1911.

How successful the Trust will be in its ambitious aim remains to be seen. But unquestionably Sir Henry Bolte will approve. It would be something for us to have our own personal Victorian battleship again. Sir Henry could send it off, listing impressively at 45 degrees, to conduct elegant acts of gunboat diplomacy off Sydney Heads, the Torrens, and even Hobart.

What a pity he couldn't float it on Lake Burley Griffin.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Copper Bed-Warmers, Black Satin Nighties and Other Bits of Nostalgia, The Bulletin, 15 May 1971. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
