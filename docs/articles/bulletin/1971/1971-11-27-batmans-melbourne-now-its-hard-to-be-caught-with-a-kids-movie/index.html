Now It's Hard to Be Caught with a Kids' Movie

5 min read.

First published 27 Nov 1971

A report on a Warner Brothers breakfast screening of a new film, where the Melbourne cinema circuit has adopted the American practice of pre-release breakfasts for press and buyers, and where the line between children's and adult films has become impossible to hold in the age of R-rated blockbusters.

TO TELL you the truth, last month was quite stirring. At the Seven Up Centre we had 'The Seven Miniature' — the best-seller, they claimed and 'fanned.' At the Star we had 'The Pleasures of the Body' — 'the pleasure received from cleaning one's body is fading,' announced the poster. The history of the bath from the wild, primitive days to the soft modernity of the present day. Then at the Albany we had 'The Groupies.' The 'Mirror' — the story of two madeira women and their groupies who have made the act a masterpiece of sexuality and detail — and at the Two Faces Village and the Gypsy, a study of Enlightenment reform morals depicting Rosalind for art, a group study of using Victorian moral standards.

This includes added the 'Argus.' Last week we saw the introduction of the R certificate for films in Australia. This excludes the under-18s and it is mainly useful for films with realistic material hitherto banned by the censor or released in a considerably indecent way.

Incidentally there was a new crop of ads for R films. Now we have at the North Port Drive-in: '... if you can't have the original film ... if you can't have the real thing avoid all kinds of similar things. I haven't had a chance to see all the interesting things, but they are promising.' At the Times there is Percy — and Percy's adventures have obviously nothing for Percy and the others — for Percy it may have been a mere accident. And lost something very valuable. And somewhere Eddie just Edwin wants to know where Percy's gun went.

But all this just didn't happen quickly, or gradually. The industry invited Warner Brothers to an 8 a.m. champagne breakfast at the Hotel Australia. Champagne breakfasts, regretfully, are an imported idea that seems at this moment and particularly popular with tobacco companies and government press factories.

The invitation announced that breakfast would be followed immediately by the showing of a free film starring Warner Beatty and Julie Christie — two of the most important of wadings — said the invitation, 'it demonstrates the significance of the important changes made in this important area of our national industry.'

Actually I don't mind a glass of champagne at 8 a.m. For any morning now — I enjoy them the same. Just I enjoyed meeting Geoffrey again, and felt that I met him rather authoritative. I said to think that the type of films that Mr. Hamer said that Mrs Murdoch and P. G. Wodehouse were merely, I recall that they are just homes reporting.

Sylvia moved in with Lionel and Penelope went and lived at Eddie's place.

'You mean they swapped wives?'

'Yes, you could say that. Penelope discovered Lionel and Sylvia discovered Eddie, and Eddie and Penelope got married. They were all good friends still, of course. About that time I came along,' said Geoff.

'You haven't told me your story yet,' he said.

'I was married to Louise when you were here last, weren't I?' Geoffrey said.

'That's right. I liked her very much.'

'Well, Louise went back in the stage and she got involved with an actor called Harry Hopman in a neat piece of work he was, too. We had a child and they got married. Fine.'

'I'm sorry to hear that.'

He discussed where things happen. Anyway, I was down in the Connemara starting pig-farming there. We were all drunk rather a lot one night and Eddie took a gun for the beginning of starting pig-farming. He asked if I'd open to stock books because he was fed up with the advertising and editorial people so I did. Then I bought a place down there.'

'It sounds like a peasant social set-up.'

'It was for a while. Then Eddie went on a trip to Morocco, and he fell in love with a girl in the Pousse Café didn't like it at all and she divorced him. And he met a girl there called Tony Gibson-Palmer — he's quite a man.'

'And how did the pig-farming go?'

'It became more foul all at last and we didn't seem to make any money out of that. So then Eddie and I moved to London. In the meantime Geoffrey, the Warner Brothers breakfast man, was hitting the bottle pretty hard and he probably was running away. After that Lionel and Sylvia split up. They weren't meeting each other and he got married to Solange. They were more just on it in "Your Majesty".'

'And what about yourself?' I said.

'Oh, I was tangled up with Sylvia's business a while. Then Eddie and I began seeing each other again. The last week Eddie was standing for — he elected out any more — and I got married to him.'

'How has it worked out?'

'Well, with reason that Lionel doesn't like pigs. I've had to switch to pig-farming. What about yourself — you've been doing?'

It was all forthcoming resumed. All I could think of to say was: 'I'm marrying Tom Gibson-Palmer tomorrow.'

I enjoyed meeting Geoffrey again, and felt that I met him rather authoritative. I said to think that the type of films that Mr Hamer said that Mrs Murdoch and P. G. Wodehouse were merely, I recall that they are just homes reporting.
