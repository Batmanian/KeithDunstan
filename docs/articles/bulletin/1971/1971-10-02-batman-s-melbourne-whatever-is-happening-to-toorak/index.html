Whatever Is Happening to Toorak

5 min read.

First published 02 Oct 1971

A lament for the decline of old Toorak, as wreckers demolish its mock Tudor streetscape to make way for a Safeway supermarket and office tower called the Village Centre.

THERE IS NO question that sensitivity and old-fashioned breeding are going out of Toorak. There was a time when I would look forward to the coming excursion there last week I was startled by the quality. Many of the wigs didn't seem quite out of the top drawer, and much of the leather was verging on plastic.

Nor are the shops what they were. There's a place called 'Nello's Restaurant' with a huge sign up aloft, 'B.Y.O.' Bring your own drinks, gentlemen, by all means, but does one make it quite so obvious in Toorak? One of the boutiques has a sign out: have your own personalised beer mug, antique even. And the word 'Trak' is horror enough, but doesn't it make your heart bleed for the days when a respectable establishment simply called itself the 'Toorak Pharmacy'?

But here's the real shock. The wreckers were in action and they were pulling down one of the mock Tudor buildings just down the hill from Grange Road on the north side. Now, you must appreciate that mock Tudor has been Toorak's glory for at least 35 years. I believe the only other place in the world that was in Forest Hills' shopping centre, somewhere in New York.

Mock Tudor first went up in Toorak in the mid-thirties. The style was repeated, enchantingly, by architects on both sides of the street. They have the leadlight windows, they have the white stucco with the wooden fascia board and the high gabled roofs to help shed the Toorak snow. Some of the fascia boards even have marvellous mock hand-hewn marks to show they were carved out with genuine mock Tudor tools.

And this is how Toorak won its name. Those Rolls-Royces, Bentleys, Jaguars, Packards and Daimlers wanted to give the impression that in truth it was a piece of olde English countryside.

There have been complaints about the wrecking to the Mayor, people mourning the passing of old Toorak. I was shocked that the National Trust had not done something. Why were there no demonstrators out on the footpath? Why hadn't Toorak matrons chained themselves to the telegraph post outside Doro's delicatessen?

The truth is that the Prahran Mayor, Cr. William Dane, ceremonially swung a sledge hammer to announce the start of the wrecking. On that day there was a 'press conference' to celebrate an 'architectural happening' for Toorak. Ironically the happening is to be called 'the Village Centre.' And it will cost $2.5 million. The centre will have two basement-level car parks, a Safeway supermarket, an 18-shop arcade on two levels and three upper floors of offices. The building will go right through to Jackson Street at the rear.

Your correspondent called on the architect Graeme Gunn and, naturally, he was very enthusiastic about the Village Centre. 'This whole place has been designed with the personality of the Toorak shopper in mind. This really is a different sort of place, a special community. It is a dense population area, with the people not as young as in South Yarra. But, look, you always meet someone you know in Toorak. It's a close community, many come on foot and they come not only to shop but to be seen. Even the traffic has to move slowly through Toorak and that alone gives you the feeling of a village.'

So the plan was to build a place where the shops out front are interesting, a place inside. The front of the building is not spectacular, it's fractured, so that it plugs into the street, then the arcade spreads out. Some places narrow, wide in others, the coffee shop is semi-open, a passageway goes under. Then you can go up to the next level, and there are all sorts of surprises.

Graeme Gunn says it will open about next year. The Leighton property company points out that according to their research there are 21,000 people living within three-quarters of a mile radius and only 15 percent of their custom is needed to pick up trade.

But the rest of the Trakkers are looking at the new development with gloom. One businessman, just a few doors away, said: 'Developers don't realise how tough business is in Toorak. We already have shops that are unoccupied and the whole place is filled with little boutiques that don't make any money. And people don't pay around here. In my business, 85 cents of every dollar is on the slate. Right now they are taking three times as long to pay as normal. You know, up the hill there, most of them are living on time, waiting on their dividend cheques.'

Even so it must be admitted that last week the butcher had Danish caviar on the counter and Doro's still has Baxter's tinned whole pheasant in the window. But just one thing disturbs me. Let us hope that some nostalgic buyer is snapping up all the genuine Toorak mock Tudor at the wrecker's yard.

What an opportunity to build a new hotel or motel, to be called Ye Olde Toorak and composed entirely of mock Tudor.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Whatever Is Happening to Toorak, The Bulletin, 2 October 1971. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
