Breaking Down Sexual Apartheid in a Most Endearing Way

4 min read.

First published 31 Jul 1971

Geelong Grammar School headmaster T. R. Garnett announces the admission of girls from first term 1972, prompting Keith Dunstan to recall the fierce gender segregation of his own public school days and speculate on the transformation the change will bring to Corio's bleak landscape.

Could one raise a pale blue cap in honor of Mr. T. R. Garnett, headmaster of Geelong Grammar School, for launching the attack to break down sexual apartheid at our great public schools. We have received his circular to parents for the introduction of girls to Geelong Grammar for first term 1972.

It all brought back memories. Apartheid at the old school was a fierce and painful thing. Once school started we were locked up for three months on end. Parents were allowed to visit for a Sunday afternoon between matins and evensong three times during that term. There would be a glimpse of a sister or a mother, but that is all.

One year I decided to learn dancing and for this instruction the famous dancing teacher Miss Lascelles would come from Melbourne weekly. You may well imagine that girls were provided. Certainly not. Boys had to dance with boys, and being rather small and blond inevitably I had to take the female role. I was one of the few males in the world who could do the extremely complicated quarter turn to the grand waltz backwards. Even to this day I dance rather better backwards than forwards.

We did have maids waiting on the tables in the dining halls and they added a little charm to proceedings, but something happened there and one day they were all banished. From then on boys waited on the tables and it was considered a good economy measure. But for headmasters' wives apartheid was perfect.

For years after leaving school it was difficult to overcome a shrinking, almost a feeling of fear — females were definitely different creatures, like meeting purple Venusians.

Next year there will be between 20 and 30 fully fledged Geelong Grammar girls. Mr. Garnett told us: 'Parents are a little cautious at the moment. Many are waiting to see how it will go. But we won't have any trouble filling the positions available for '72. There's been an application from Sydney, another from Canberra. Country people are interested, and particularly those who have all daughters and no sons in their family. You must remember they won't be too much an object of astonishment at school. For two years we have had 27 girls from the Hermitage in Geelong, coming for science and maths classes.'

The parents' circular informs us that there will be four girls attached to each senior school house. It says: 'Each boarding house has a married housemaster, two house tutors, at least one of whom is likely to be married. A girl (although she will sleep in the home of a married master) will be a full member of a boys' boarding house and will have all her meals in the dining hall. Her housemaster will be responsible for her well-being. The relationship with her host and hostess will be that of a friend of the family. If she has to be out after dark, e.g., to a society meeting after prep, she will be escorted home by a boy.'

No decision has yet been made about uniforms. It has always been a curious feature of Victorian schools that girls are provided with almost masculine uniforms, white shirts, ties, V-neck sweaters and blazers. Fortunately Mr. Garnett does not appear to be thinking this way. He says: 'If there is one at all, the requirements will be very simple and confined to skirt, blouse, jumper and dress ... The first girls will have quite a lot to cope with and not having to make decisions about clothes might be a relief.'

There is no doubt these girls will be a tremendous asset to the bleak Corio scene. We see a depth being added to sopranos in the choir, we see a possibility for girls at football matches. Mr. Garnett says there is no reason why new activities should not be added to the school list such as cooking and flower arrangement. 'We might even offer the choice to the boys,' he said.

As for sport, the circular says: 'Already there exist facilities for tennis, swimming, sailing, basketball, badminton, golf, judo, karate, fencing. We hope there will soon be a squash court. There should be no difficulty about baseball. Riding is also a possibility. Whether there will be a hockey team will depend on the number of girls who want to play.' What about cricket and rowing?

Rowing indeed. It may require a number of years before Women's Lib can provide a female stroke for the Geelong Grammar crew, but why not next year a gorgeously attractive 7st. female cox?

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Breaking Down Sexual Apartheid in a Most Endearing Way, The Bulletin, 31 July 1971. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
