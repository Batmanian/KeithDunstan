The Pharters on the Hill Know the Game's Lovely Ritual Qualities

4 min read.

First published 27 Feb 1971

A defence of Test cricket's solemn ritual against calls to brighten it up, arguing that its magnificent, unalterable dullness is its greatest charm, drawing on statistics from W. L. Murdoch in 1881 and a comparison with Chinese Opera as evidence that nothing has ever really happened and nothing ever will.

THE MOST disturbing feature of the Test series was the statement by both captains that they would like to 'scrap the Ashes.' Mr. Illingworth said: 'They are overrated.' 'I agree,' said Mr. Chappell.

Of course, getting rid of the Ashes would be like taking the joss out of a joss-house; it would be left an empty, aching thing.

Also this season it has been disturbing to observe the passionate enthusiasm of cricket writers for brightening the game. They want to reduce Test Matches to helter-skelter, one-day games; they want to limit the number of overs; they want to penalise players for non-scoring shots.

The great mistake is in assuming that cricket is a game. Of course, it is not. It is a carefully developed ritual — almost a religious thing, to be compared with the samurai code of honour or sumo wrestling. The observer should draw spiritual refreshment from it.

While it does not draw the same number of spectators as the rowdy codes of football, I believe the number of radio and television followers and worshippers is actually greater. Whenever I cannot get to a Test Match I always have the cricket commentary droning in the background. It has the soothing and uplifting effect of the chanting of a Mass.

The ABC chooses its commentators for the sepulchral qualities of their voices and they never say anything likely to disturb. The repetition of words, in other words the recitation, over 130 hours of a match has a beautifully calming impression.

The Chinese understand this all too well. They have their Chinese Opera, which also goes on for five days. It is amazingly like cricket. There are only boys in the cast. They are chosen and trained from an early age, giving up their entire lives to the ritual. Like cricketers, they are trained at once in very precise movements, handed down through many generations. The players have tea breaks, and much of the enchantment is gained through constant repetition. Yet so far as I know the Chinese are not trying to brighten opera. The Communist Government entirely approves.

So you see the only real difference between the two activities is that the Chinese never talk about brighter opera. It would be vulgar even to suggest such a thing. Yet it is part of our ritual to talk about brighter cricket, while knowing full well it will never happen. At their first get-together, Messrs. Illingworth and Lawry both promised lively, attractive cricket. Oh, it's always done. Hassett and Hutton did it. So did Ian Johnson and Peter May. I think even the legendary Mr. Stephenson promised a very bright tour when he arrived here with his eleven in 1861.

It is impossible. Inevitably one side has to start losing; one captain is put in the position of making a dour fight back. In the old days, this was never referred to as dull cricket. Commentators used to refer to 'an entrancing struggle between bat and ball.'

The truth is that cricket has always been dull. Its sheer unalterable dullness has been its greatest charm. Mr. Len Evans, our Cellarmaster, and Mr. Ron Saw, the columnist, have started an organisation called the Paddington Hill Ancient and Royal Tasting Club, known as OTT for short. Mr. Evans is the winemaster of this organisation. It is interesting that they have 20 cents in the kitty and the first person to actually see a wicket fall takes the kitty.

Now, those who say we want brighter cricket are absurd — one could quote statistics which go back for a long period. Back in 1881, at the Test Match in Melbourne, W. L. Murdoch took more than two and a half hours to make 62. And there was the 1878 tour of Lord Harris. His star bowler, A. N. Hornby, used to have a specialty called 'The Sydney Grubber.' He aimed it straight at the middle stump and at no stage did it leave the ground. Scoring off it was impossible, and the spectators were so disturbed that they shouted, 'Have a Go.' This is reported in the 'Melbourne Argus' and proves that the cry 'Have a Go' is also part of the ancient ritual.

Oh, Wisden has a page full of famous slow-scoring efforts. There are even Test Matches between India and Pakistan where for series after series they have failed to complete a match. The fact that they hardly ever finish a match only increases the tension and draws larger crowds. India and Pakistan play the most incomparably dull cricket and it is there that the game is most popular.

So let us cut out all that bright cricket nonsense and bring back Lawry at once. He really understands the game.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The Pharters on the Hill Know the Game's Lovely Ritual Qualities, The Bulletin, 27 February 1971. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
