Aeroplanes

5 min read.

First published 22 May 1971

A review of the illustrated history 'Aircraft of the RAAF 1921-71', moving through five decades of types from DH9 biplanes and Westland Wapitis to Catalina flying-boats and the CA-15, and concluding that the most beautiful aircraft the RAAF ever possessed was the de Havilland Mosquito.

MAYBE, just occasionally, it was the glycol fumes, maybe it was lack of oxygen at height, but almost invariably RAAF pilots would fall in love with their aircraft, and they would start to give them human characteristics.

I am told that one of the sweetest aeroplanes to fly was that little twin-boomed jet the De Havilland Vampire. It really had no blood-sucking tendencies at all.

One of the most spirited was that biplane the Bristol Bulldog. It was utterly marvellous for aerobatics and never really bit anyone. Some of the best turns ever seen here at air shows were provided in RAAF Bulldogs.

For those with a passion for aeroplanes, a book filled with pictures came out this week. It is: 'Aircraft of the RAAF — 1921-71,' by Geoffrey Pentland and Peter Malone, designed to honour the 50th anniversary of the RAAF.

For a little air force, it is almost unbelievable the variety of types that we acquired. There must have been times when bases like Point Cook and Richmond looked like a Kevin Dennis used-car lot.

We started off with 128 DH9's, DH9A's and SE5A's — a gift of the British Government to the Air Force. There is a photograph of a line of those SE5 World War I fighters at Point Cook. On the extreme end there is the two-seater trainer version. The pilots called it 'The Pig,' because of its vile manners. It was difficult to turn and manoeuvre and it had bad fuel tanks above the wings, an idea which considerably helped its instability.

As you turn through the pages there are many aircraft, now almost forgotten. There was the Fairey IIID, which had encumbrances not only of two wings but floats as well. If it was feeling terribly well it could actually attain a top speed of 106 mph.

Another float plane was the Seagull, a forerunner of the Walrus. When it hit the water it made a noise like a house being dropped from 40ft. It had marvellous four-bladed carved wooden propellers, now as prized for their antique value as Chippendale dining-room tables.

The Westland Wapiti was one of our front-line aircraft of the 'thirties, rather a vast two-seater biplane. It had a top speed of 135 mph and a range of 360 miles, which meant you would be lucky to assault an enemy as far off as Albury.

The late Air-Commodore Cobby used to swear that one of the most fantastic sights was to see a Wapiti going slowly backward across Point Cook in a high wind.

But in 50 years what was the loveliest aircraft in the possession of the RAAF? Among the biplanes there is no doubt, whatever, it was the Hawker Demon. We went into the war with those Demons; lovely they were, like a Spitfire — no, a Hurricane with two wings.

Then does anyone remember that once we had some German aircraft, some Dornier flying-boats, long, undernourished, extenuated-looking creatures with three engines up aloft. We received them from the Dutch East Indies and No. 41 Squadron used them as transports between Australia and New Guinea in 1943. They were in bad shape when they arrived and one disintegrated in mid-air when an electrical short circuit ignited the fuel in the sponson tank.

The greatest of the flying-boats was the Catalina. It was estimated after the war that in terms of actual tonnage of shipping sunk, one Catalina squadron was worth more than the combined efforts of several Liberator heavy-bomber squadrons.

Catalinas are now highly prized collectors' items. One American prospecting company at Darwin has an old Cat fitted out more beautifully than a boardroom. Remember those two big gun blisters down aft? These are fitted out now as gorgeous sunroom observation decks.

You keep turning the pages and you come across a flight of Avro Ansons. The undercarriage had to be hand wound up and hand wound down. Ah, save for the happy few.

Then the CA-15. I had forgotten about that. This was one of the great Australian tragedies. Designed by the Commonwealth Aircraft Corporation, it had a top speed of 448 miles and was to be a world-beater, but it was beaten by the jet age. For years it remained, lonely, unwanted, in a hangar at Laverton.

But we still haven't decided which was the most beautiful aircraft in 50 years. Of course, the Spitfire would have to be a candidate. But in the later models it became too cluttered. The Mustang always had cleaner lines.

Another candidate, and a very strong one, would be our present front-line fighter, the Mirage. But for my money I keep looking back to the De Havilland Mosquito. Clean and graceful, with no uncouth projections. Unlike modern jets with their ugly gadgetry, it was exquisitely shaped. There was a perfect line between nose and propeller boss.

Ah, yes, for my money the Mosquito was the most beautiful thing ever possessed by the RAAF. What a joy it must have been to fly her.

Source: Batman's Aeroplanes, The Bulletin, 22 May 1971. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
