Peaches and Cream in a Soulful Setting

4 min read.

First published 24 Apr 1971

A report on Cobram's inaugural Peaches and Cream Festival, a Murray River pop and folk event at Easter that combined an Apex-run stage on army pontoons, a cyclone-fence perimeter, five speedboats watching for drownees, and a Channel Nine crew egging on a brawl.

FRANKLY, I have slipped behind a little in modern music terminology. In my youth we used to have boogie-woogie nights. On occasions of extreme excitement we had jam sessions, but imagine my interest upon receiving an invitation to a 'Folk and Soul Happening.' Folk seemed understandable enough, but Soul? I checked with my dictionary. 'Soul: The spiritual part of a person; the moral, emotional, intellectual part of a person; the vital principle, and mental powers possessed by man; the essential or animating or inspiring force.'

No longer did I hesitate. I hopped in my motor-car immediately. The Folk and Soul Happening was the kick-off function for the Cobram Peaches and Cream Festival. Cobram is a dairying-fruit district on the Murray. It is de rigueur now for every town, no matter what size, to have a festival, but it is becoming hard to come up with new ideas.

This was the first for Cobram. Everyone who passed through received sliced peaches and whipped cream. They didn't have their peaches and cream festival in the peach season. It was too late for fresh peaches.

The town was decorated with white balls on fencing wire, borrowed from Moomba. During the Moomba Festival we called them mothballs, and thought they were like the globes used for holding up fishing nets. In Cobram, no doubt, they symbolised peaches.

The festival was being run by a local Apex club and the cost for the happening alone was $3,500. They took some Army pontoons and built a stage out in the middle of the Murray, opposite Thomson's Beach — a beach with more sand and considerably less pollution than Bondi. The theory was that under the full Easter moon and the pure, clean, autumn, starry night the soulfulness of this setting would be beyond imagining.

There are two classic situations with pop festivals — your groups don't turn up and your audience wants to get in without paying. At this festival, one group went to England at the last minute, another received a better offer and there were desperate efforts to find quality replacements.

The security arrangements were interesting. On one side they created an artificial lagoon by pumping water for three days through a 3in. irrigation pipe. On the other they erected a 9ft.-high cyclone fence. As soon as the sun went down the soulful ones crept forward like Viet Cong infiltrators at the siege of Saigon Airport. Apexians with spotlights patrolled the perimeter in cars. Some inched their way on their stomachs through the long grass by the riverbank, some burrowed prison-style under the wire. Long-haired, bearded, bare-footed creatures tried to heave the female of their species up over the top. Some of them, suspended half on one side, half on the other, looked very curious, like animated clothespegs.

The entry fee was $2 a head and as the evening moved on some of them became desperate. One character hit an Apex man over the head with an unloaded gun. Others, in a solid phalanx, assaulted the gate like the spearhead troops of Tiberius.

Over 2,000 paid to get in; at least 500 did not. The scene inside was perhaps a little reminiscent of the Israelites after they had come through the Red Sea. They were equally hairy. Many of them were wrapped in blankets. The cult uniform for both sexes was denim trousers with denim top. Campfires were everywhere. Many had been there since early morning and the scene was richly decorated with the great Australian symbol, the empty beer bottle. Some of these were floating down the river. Many couples just lay together under blankets or in sleeping-bags, taking no notice whatever of the music or those around, and one can only suspect from their activities that many little souls may have been created from the Soul Happening.

The entertainment was provided by Shirley Jacobs, folksinger, and two pop groups. There was a platform on the bank, maybe 20ft. high, for the spotlights. So the river was constantly in an alternate flood of red and green. Red globes on the stage flashed on and off. The Master's Apprentices had their power instruments and huge speakers on the stage and they must have been using sufficient electric power to illuminate three Cobrams. In the midst of one of their soul-disturbing numbers a brawl broke out on the bank. Everyone swarmed into the melee as at a football match. In the forefront was a Channel Nine crew with lights. This seemed to encourage assailants.

Yet Apex control was magnificent. They had a system. Five speedboats patrolled the river to pick up potential drownees, but also powerful young farmers grabbed all brawlers and threw them into boats. This way they didn't have to take them back through the crowds. They just quietly shanghaied them.
