An Old Lover Turns Faithless, and It's Not the Same Anymore

5 min read.

First published 12 Jun 1971

A return visit to Brisbane reveals a city that has bulldozed its beloved old 'High Noon' pubs and Rhine-castle hotels, replaced them with brick-and-plastic horrors, and inflicted on itself a paved city square that resembles a crematorium forecourt, though Sunday drinking in the old-fashioned pubs is still going strong.

MANY years ago, on this page, I wrote of my passionate love affair with Brisbane. The whole place looked as if it had been designed by Ronald Searle. I loved all the 'High Noon' pubs with their swing doors, the galvanised iron going off like ripples for ever into the distance, and there wasn't much in the way of sewerage. The nights were perfect, dead still. You would sit out on your front steps, barefoot, and things were so quiet you would swear you could hear the white ants chewing the stumps down below.

There used to be an old saying about Berlin and Vienna. In Berlin things are often serious, but nothing is hopeless. In Vienna everything is hopeless, but nothing is ever serious. Well, Brisbane was like Vienna — nothing was serious and the hunt for the almighty quid seemed more unimportant there than anywhere else.

Well, you ought to see how they have loused the place up. All the old 'High Noon' pubs have gone, to be converted into fancy brick and plastic horrors. Remember Chardon's — that incredibly old pub on Annerley Road? It looked like a cross between a Rhine castle and a Luna Park giggle palace. Gone. The lovely old Bellevue Hotel with its exquisite multiple verandas in George Street is still there. It was to be wrecked last January, but there was such a protest the Government has now turned it into a residence for parliamentary members. Even so it is like the public castration of a splendid old establishment. Then the Supreme Court building, another gem, has also gone.

Brisbane, as a tourist city, should always have concentrated on becoming New Orleans. It accidentally should have imported cast iron by the ton, instead of ripping it away. But the city fathers always had dreams of grandeur. The Lord Mayor, Clem Jones, has fought brilliantly and aggressively for his city square, and now it is complete. The level has been raised; there's the vast car park underneath and the only building he hasn't been able to move is the Ann Street Presbyterian Church.

But the design is a tragedy. It is paved, with rectangles of petunias, so that it all reminds one of the front garden for a crematorium. The whole area cries out for a large and beautiful sculpture, but instead there is a mechanical fountain that looks like a garden sprinkler. Oh, actually, there is sculpture in front of the Town Hall, that was in the same area before — King George V on a horse, looking appropriately imperious, but shockingly dressed for the climate. The square is surrounded by the predictable totems of the 1970s, towering insurance buildings and banks. That's another thing — the feature of the Brisbane skyline used to be the lofty campanile of the Brisbane City Hall — now you can hardly see it. The failure of the square in a sub-tropical climate is this — it's not made for living. There should be indoor-outdoor restaurants, shady places where you can sit with a Bulimba in your hand and watch the pigeons. But, no, the establishments all around are only for making claims and cashing cheques.

Also, there is a rather controversial plan in the making and this has caused a small furore. You know Anzac Square, opposite the railway station? This is to be raised 14ft., carried across Adelaide Street like a bridge and extended right through to Queen Street, thus doubling its size. Two companies have put up the money and in return they get a car park under the square. All the bottle trees, date palms, and such will be taken away and the promise is that everything will be returned exactly as before and not an Anzac Day will be interrupted. What's more, they will get a new fountain made of exposed aggregate which will symbolise the ruggedness of the Gallipoli campaign. The RSL has agreed to all this, but many of the old Diggers are livid.

Mr. W. G. Fox, of The Valley, wrote: 'It would be better to lose $1 million now and retain our respect for those who fought and died for Australia than to go ahead with the plan to tear down Anzac Memorial Square.'

Hazel Paterson, of Toowong, took to verse:

Please contemplate, my dear Lord Mayor, Your new and spurious Anzac Square, Where kinsfolk sad will mop their brow, Dipping their pennies in the fountain now. 'Lest we forget' you'll stand and ring.

But something is still right — Sunday drinking. Out on the fringe I found one of the biggest drive-in bottle departments I have ever seen, and at 4.45 p.m. there were cars moving through in a double column. Out the front in the public bar, just like the old days there were the fellers in shorts, singlets and thongs, tossing down the rum and the Bulimba.

Forget it, in the old days on a Sunday they'd have been away fishing at Jumpinpin.
