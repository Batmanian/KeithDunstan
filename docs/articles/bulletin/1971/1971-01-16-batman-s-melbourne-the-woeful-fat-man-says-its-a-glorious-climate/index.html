The Woeful Fat Man Says It's a Glorious Climate

5 min read.

First published 16 Jan 1971

Melbourne's notorious weather, which wrecked an entire Test Match and drew comment from Robert Menzies, inspires a dossier of celebrated complaints reaching back to 1863, from Ellen Kean to Marcus Clarke's 'Glorious Climate' monologue.

YOU MAY have noticed that in recent weeks Melbourne has picked up some remarkable world publicity with her weather. It rained for nigh on a week and ruined an entire Test Match. Even Sir Robert Menzies was moved to pass comment. Addressing a band of English cricket lovers he said: 'We belong to a conservative, calm, cautious city, but the weather you have experienced in the past week is beyond all human experience in Melbourne.'

Two pensioners in East Malvern believe the recent weather has been caused by Americans taking samples of earth from the moon. Mrs. Frances Clarke, of Windsor, was quoted at length in the 'Herald.' She said: 'I think the end of the world is coming. Man has upset the balance of nature. I see terrible things. The air is filled with electricity, the clouds are thick and ugly and the sun is getting weaker. Little people like myself are becoming too scared to venture out of doors. We can't sleep at night for the moaning of the wind. I can't even relax to play cards.'

There have even been damning editorials on the weather by our best leader writers. However, it just so happens that your correspondent has been compiling a dossier on the subject. I would like to call on Sir Robert Menzies and Mr. Gorton not to be alarmed. The truth is that our weather has always been like this.

Ellen Kean, wife of the distinguished Shakespearian actor Charles Kean, wrote on November 20, 1863: 'Since I last wrote to you this lovely climate has given us every variety of bad weather except snow, and they have that in some parts. When the rain ceased we have had a gull word come with dreadful sunshine, then the bay would be quickly lashed into wild disturbance, and then it would calm down coldly upon the water, the wind howling piteously and the sky threatening. The only really settled weather we had was when it continued raining.'

In 'Cobbers,' Dr. Thomas Wood wrote: 'The man who said that London had only one climate, which was called Most Unusual Weather, did not know Melbourne. If he had been there, only once, that barbed shaft would never have been wasted on London Town. Melbourne would have got it, deservedly, in the neck; and all Sydney would have thrown up its hat and cheered.'

Sheila Smith, visiting U.S. actress, June 29, 1965: 'Let's face it, dear, the weather here is a good story. One minute it's 58, the next it's 23. We even had a fog on stage on Saturday. I thought I was in Brigadoon. So I never know what to wear. I look out the window, see a woman shivering in furs, and I put on boots, scarf and trench coat. But by the time I get downstairs — you know what? — it's a lovely day.'

Miss Isabella Bird, travel writer, 1872: 'The number of heavy thunderstorms with torrents of rain averages 26 a year, while the Geelong folk on the same harbor escape with three. My own experience of the Melbourne climate was very unfavorable, though I left it before the summer heats in. It produced at once such irritation of the nerves as to make me dream of a bath of laudanum and such lassitude that even the grasshopper was a burden. Brilliant skies, fierce sunshine, torrid heat, dewless nights, sullen skies, resulting in thunderstorms; rains, which devastated rather than refreshed; almost constant winds; humid sunless heat; dust storms; hot gales from the north, and cold gales from the south, are the meteorological record of my diary.'

Marcus Clarke, circa 1875: '5 a.m. Awoke to find the window open, wind blowing in like a truant tornado, looking-glass broken and cold in my left ear. Glorious climate.

'7 a.m. The wind has changed. I should say it has. Dust: ugh! It's a north wind and the floor of this chamber is an inch deep in sand. Glorious climate.

'11.59 a.m. My hat blows off. A fat man running after his hat is the most despicable object in nature. My boots are full of protoplasm, and my heart is full of woe. The thermometer is 104.

'5.30 p.m. The wind has changed. It is now blowing all ways at once, and Bourke Street is like a flour mill. Glorious climate.

'7.10 p.m. Stroll out in white flannel and cigar. Cool as a cucumber. This is a glorious climate.

'7.15 p.m. Rain. Buckets full of it. Gutters run over. Half crowns hopping on the pavement. Omnibuses full, and my flannel soaked to the skin. Glorious climate.'

These are just a few examples from my dossier. It may be said that some of the most eloquent passages ever written about our beloved city have been devoted to its climate. This rich public-relations material should not be wasted. Any city which can produce so many varieties of weather in one day could only be inhabited by alert, exciting, brilliant people. I mean, Sir Henry has been promoting this overseas for years — it stands to reason that nobody would want to start a large industrial enterprise in a calm, warm, eternally beach-loving climate. Given a few more Test Matches, our city will have all the rich, world-wide weather fame of places like Manchester.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The Woeful Fat Man Says It's a Glorious Climate, The Bulletin, 16 January 1971. Accessible via theNational Library of Australia's Trove database.
