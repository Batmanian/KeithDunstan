The Very Hard Week That Was

5 min read.

First published 06 Nov 1971

A report on Melbourne Wine Week, centred on a grand tasting at the Two Faces restaurant, with notes on the calibre of South Australian entries, the heroic endurance required of serious wine judges, and the state of the Australian wine industry as it faces a bottling boom from South Australia.

FOR THOSE sympathetic to wine, Wine Week is a very hard week. It circulates between Melbourne, Sydney and Adelaide and so comes back to us every third year. If only it were restricted to official happenings sponsored by the Wine and Brandy Producers Association all would be well, but almost every considerable function associated with the wine business is designed to happen at the same time. Only wine-lovers, toughened in the event, can survive.

Orlando, for example, released a new wine called something-Riesling, coming in a dazzling new bottle of milk glass. High looks like the raw old pressuretype cod liver oil. Also like cod liver oil, the glass is so opaque it remains a mystery as to how much fluid is left in the bottle.

Wynns announced a new barrel pack which holds the equivalent of 100 bottles. It has a nylon laminated sack inside, a stainless steel valve and a little tap and as the wine goes down the pack collapses. According to Wynns, lamination and according to Wynns even if you pour from the pack over several weeks the wine remains fresh and marvellously fresh.

There was a vast marquee for the tasting at this wine at the Hotel Australia. There were 400 guests and the bottles stacked in galvanised iron tanks full of ice like overgrown rubbish tins. Penfolds pack was a gallon tin painted gold. This is a perfectly good idea. A Wynns pack is a box and it is painted a brick-like colour. On the plus side, David Wyans asserts as that the Wynns model is only infinitely more, infinitely more refined than the Penfold design. There was considerable interesting and sherry casks.

However, it brought together in Chapel Street, Prahran, opened new wine cellars. There were Persian carpets throughout. It is nice to have a livingroom atmosphere while buying your claret and biscuits. Crittenden in Malvern did, I believe, also build something complete with art show. Their new premises had a living-room reception and a high vaulted ceiling with stained glass. At your buying time your eyes get a sanctified feeling, not exactly highly romantic, but with it, at least decently Evangelical.

There was something of all was Seppelts' new factory, or should we call it 'facility,' at Great Western in Bayside. Both the place and the event were spectacular. Seppelts plan to do 80 percent of their bottling there and the wine will come over by tanker from South Australia. They intend to bottle 19,000 gallons daily.

There was a vast marquee for the 400 guests and the champagne never stopped flowing. Seppelts had the bottles stacked in galvanised iron tanks full of ice, like overgrown rubbish tins. Each guest received two bottles of a special bottling of Australian hermitage at a big wine. In 1971 they are doing this very day. What's more, each bottle had a very fine pedigree and it was a rare edition to become of priceless value in 100 years in your collection.

Sir Henry Bolte, of course, did the opening. Good day, Sir Henry. Now Seppelt originally had 14 children six sons, eight daughters — and all had fine progeny. So if you forget everything about the family it is a pretty good gamble if you just kept on saying: 'Good day, Mr Seppelt. Good day, Mr Seppelt. Good day, Mr. Seppelt.'

He said the consumption of wine had gone up 17 percent over the last five years. 'And this would have continued but for all the strikes,' said Sir Henry. That went down awfully well with this particular crowd. Someone asked could he be sad only: 'I'm glad to be in a place where the management knows who is running trains.'

The Great Western Week banquet took place in the Great Hall at the Arts Centre. Presiding as the Minister at the time was the Minister for Primary Industries, and Ian Sinclair, who showed maybe one tenth of the vocal and polling range of Sir Henry.

But the event of the week was the great annual asparagus spears competition at the Two Faces restaurant in South Yarra. There were lovely asparagus spears with prosciutto and cheese overthrow, followed with fine herb noisette, artichokes filled with puree, a brochette, truffled sweetbreads, port wine sauce, vintage cheddar and a rich black truffle peach with passion fruit sauce. Among the 11 wines there were a 1955 Gramp Hermitage, a 1949 Tyrells Dry Red, a 1955 Glenrobin McWilliams Announcements, a 1955 Gramp Hermitage and the bottom earth floor of the Old Craiglee Claret. Douglas Seabrook extracted the cork with great care to the bottom earth floor of the Old Mossgreen Auction House.

The one handblown bottle was brought to the table and Geoffrey Seabrook extracted the cork with great deliberation. He was immensely at repose as if he had found a bomb in the middle of the paddock. Then in beautiful mathematical precision the Mossgreen Auction House put on the glasses.

It was dark in color, still fragrant, with a lovely soft maturity, almost like a great Médoc, and it produced no sense of guilt. It seemed an act of God to taste it: 'Something interesting thing in its 100th year on the very verge of its centenary.'

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The Very Hard Week That Was, The Bulletin, 6 November 1971. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
