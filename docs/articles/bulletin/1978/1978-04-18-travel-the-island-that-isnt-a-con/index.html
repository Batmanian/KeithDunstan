The island that isn't a con

8 min read.

First published 18 Apr 1978

A visit to Kangaroo Island — where tourists must be protected from predatory wildlife, pelicans do high-marking catches like Australian Rules footballers, penguins invade caravans at night, and 1000 kilometres of fiendish dirt roads lead to the discovery that after dark absolutely nothing happens.

MY LIFE has been spent looking for things which aren't there. I have been in Kansas at the height of the harvest to find not a restaurant serving corn on the cob. I have been in Mildura at orange time to discover that every motel serves that ghastly processed orange juice. I went to Ireland with the pathetic desire of hearing a dinkum Irishman say "Begorrah" — not a chance. In Vienna I tramped around the coffee houses and little restaurants pining for an orgy of apple strudel. Would you believe it was not to be had? I didn't see a buffalo in Buffalo. I never saw a crocodile let alone an alligator on the Alligator River, but by golly, there are kangaroos on Kangaroo Island.

Indeed a man can go further. It is the first place I visited where the predatory ocker tourist has to be protected from the ocker wildlife. Almost the entire west end of the island is fenced off for the Flinders Chase reserve. We drove in across the cattle grid, pulled up at the picnic area, wound down the windows for some fresh air and immediately three kangaroos thrust their heads inside. There was almost a bushranger attitude of "Stand and deliver."

We did the right thing — stepped outside, pulled out our booty, a bag of bread, and delivered. This was the signal for all the rest of the dozing wildlife to converge. One gentleman kangaroo was so keen he put his paws on a lady's shoulders. Disturbing.

But the character to watch was the emu. He was standing in the midst of the scrum and with his fierce eyes he did not look as sweetly lovable as the roos. He would lash his long neck forward and literally snatch the stay-fresh sliced loaf out of one's hand, plus a portion of the thumb.

So do you know what they do at Flinders Chase? The ranger has some picnic tables surrounded by a fence. You can sit in there and eat your sandwiches while kangaroos, emus, Cape Barren geese or whatever, are fenced outside. It is a little uncomfortable sitting there while glaring eyes, ranging from brown to bright yellow, all hungrily observe every mouthful. It is then that one understands how Marie Antoinette felt — slightly before the French Revolution.

Kangaroo Island is that very large island off South Australia just beyond Yorke Peninsula. Always it has had a marvellous, unreal, escapist quality, rugged Eldorado away from the torture of civilisation. The early escapists were break-outs from Adelaide jails, sailor drop-outs who could not stand the brutality and rapacity of life aboard the old sailing ships, fishermen and farmers, through to company directors who could not bear the brutality and rapacity of board rooms in Melbourne and Sydney.

Captain Matthew Flinders arrived at Kangaroo Island on March 20, 1802. In his journal he recorded his pleasure in finding so many delightfully tame creatures that had never seen a human being. Some they shot in the eyes with small shot and others they just knocked on the head with pieces of wood. It was easy.

He wrote: "The whole ship's company was employed this afternoon, in skinning and cleaning the kangaroos; and a delightful regale they accorded, after four months' privation from almost any provisions. Half a hundredweight of heads, forequarters and tails were stewed down into soup for dinner on this and the succeeding days; and as much steaks given, moreover to both officers and men as they could consume by day and night. In gratitude for so seasonable a supply, I named this south land as Kangaroo Island."

So perhaps it was appropriate that this first land in honor of our national creature was named to celebrate its slaughter.

Getting to know Kangaroo Island is less difficult now. You can fly there in 35 minutes aboard an Ansett Fokker Friendship or you can travel in a twin-engined Cessna with a firm like Emu Air Charter. We had bicycles so we took the MV Troubridge, a sturdy roll-on, roll-off ship, a sort of scaled down Princess of Tasmania, capable of carrying all types of vehicles. The fare was $26, we sat in Pioneer bus-type lay-back seats, and the trip took three and a half hours. The catering arrangement was a small bar which sold beer, sandwiches, and execrable pies and pasties.

The Troubridge berthed at Kingscote just after dark. This is the main town, 1100 people, and it looks like any seaside, rural, South Australian centre... but older. Actually it dates from 1834. An utterly sacred relic from those days is a mulberry tree at Reeves Point. There it is, propped up like a stroke-ridden pensioner, yet it still bears fruit. John Downing, the librarian and tourist officer, claims it is a botanical miracle that it is still alive.

Maybe the prime tourist area is American River, not named for any modern Coca Cola culture but for American sealers from the brig Union who dallied there back in 1803-4. Local population is 110 which swells to around 2000 in January. For a start it is not a river at all but an inlet which almost cuts the island in two, and at the end of the inlet there is Pelican Lagoon, where there are really pelicans, squadrons and squadrons of them.

Up on the hillside Ed Loveridge has a motel called the White House. He is one of those escapists. Until his third heart attack he used to be deputy managing director of a big company in Sydney. Now he has eight units — nine would be too many, he says — and the pelicans put on the floor show right there in front of the motel windows. The birds never like to get up too early; let's say the squadrons scramble by early afternoon.

Frankly, these pelicans are so large, so fat, it's a miracle how they get airborne. On the beach we found George King, of Linnetts Tourist Resort, cleaning fish and he was hemmed in by pelicans. Every time he had a waste piece he would call out, "All right girls — heads up." Their enormous kit-bag beaks all went in unison to the vertical. They looked exactly like high-marking Australian Rules footballers. There was one "Mr Percival," so big, I swear he came up almost to my shoulder and he was the champion ruckman. His flying beak took two fish out of three.

Yet best of all we liked them on patrol duty, when they were dipping and soaring up to 500 feet. And coming in to land against a stiff southerly, that was another thing — one after another they would come down almost as if on a runway, full flap, undercart down, shaking almost on the point of stall, ZZZZZZZZ right parallel to the incline of the hillside, shuddering down like an old Tiger Moth. Marvellous.

The island is 145 kilometres long and an average of 50 kilometres wide and the farmers live mostly by grazing sheep and cattle. Right at the western tip there are the Remarkable Rocks, the sort of things Jim Mollison would give $10 million of government money to have shifted to the National Gallery in Canberra. They are pure Henry Moore, but Kangaroo Island got in first by about 500 million years.

Rex Ellis, camel lover and naturalist, who runs his own wild life sanctuary at McLaren Flat, was aboard. He said there were no foxes or rabbits on Kangaroo Island and undoubtedly it was the last untouched, natural piece of country left in South Australia. Here was the place to find native fauna as it once was, and he made it clear that if you were seen there with a rifle a shudder would go through the whole island.

We stayed in a caravan park almost on the beach run by Ed and Ev Hudson, escapists from Frankston, Victoria. The first thing they do is to warn car drivers to be careful driving into the caravan park at night for fear they might run over a penguin. Now these penguins are almost as odd as the kangaroos. They don't have the military discipline of the fairy penguins at Victoria's Phillip Island. There they march up the beach together, precisely at dusk every night. Here they wander up to feed their young at all hours after dark.

They have their rookeries under rocks, under bushes and if you leave the door of your house open they are quite likely to go in there. At nesting time the noise is amazing. They were all around our caravan. Sometimes they sounded like hysterical cicadas, sometimes they whistled, sometimes they squawked and they did everything but indulge in community singing. But it was obvious that as one parent went off for the day to do the fishing, his mate had a special noise code to bring him back home again to the rookery as if by radar. So, as one tried to sleep, it was possible to hear the penguin orchestra in full cry.

Of course, like all islanders the locals have that nobody-loves-me sense of persecution, a feeling of cut-offness. Everything depends on the MV Troubridge, which they call the "mobile bitumen," which oddly enough is owned by the SA Highways Department and loses around $800,000 a year. It takes the sheep and cattle to market, brings back the superphosphate plus all the supplies. Apart from the Troubridge there are only the airlines and two small coastal ketches. If she breaks down, what then? Already she is elderly and a replacement is at least five years off.

The sources of prosperity are farming, gypsum, fish, and above all the tourist. But if you are the type that thinks the Gold Coast is everything that is perfect for a holiday maybe there are several points we should get straight. There are only 130 kilometres of sealed road on the island and over 1000 kilometres of dust and sand-laden highways that are utterly fiendish. The wild life, not the night life, is the entertainment. After dark literally nothing happens.

The biggest deal in Kingscote is bingo on Tuesday nights and if you are dead lucky you might get a hand of euchre on Saturdays. There is probably not an electric guitar on the whole island. Need anything more be said?
