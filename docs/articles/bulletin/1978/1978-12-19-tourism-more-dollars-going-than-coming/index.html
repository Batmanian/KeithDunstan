More dollars going than coming

4 min read.

First published 19 Dec 1978

The Australian Tourist Commission's 1977 Statistical Review reveals that while 563,000 visitors came to Australia — with New Zealand, the UK and Japan leading — nearly twice as many Australians (971,000) went the other way, spending $580 million abroad against visitors' $343 million, with 51.9 percent of arrivals never getting beyond New South Wales and the Japanese moving through in under a week.

THE PROBLEM with tourism is that Australians are eager to get out and see the world, but the world in return is not so eager to see us. Perhaps in the past we have over-emphasised the kangaroos, the koala bears and the glories of the wide open spaces.

The Australian Tourist Commission has just brought out its Statistical Review for 1977 and it contains some fascinating information.

The total number of visitors in 1977 was 563,281 compared with 426,403 back in 1972. Meanwhile the number of Australians going away has spiralled up the graph. It was 504,519 in 1972 and 971,253 in 1977. We have, however, been chastened by the economic flop. There was a slight drop between 1976 and 1977.

Here's our Tops of the Pops for overseas visitors. New Zealand is way out in front with 162,563. Next on the list are the UK and Ireland, 82,228; the US, 76,236; Japan, 30,704; Papua New Guinea, 24,919; Canada, 18,018; Germany, 15,207; the Netherlands, 10,473; Malaysia, 10,347; and Italy, 8690.

The big growth stock is Japan. The Japanese have doubled their numbers from 1972. So too have Malaysia, Germany, Italy and the Netherlands.

The data is all analysed down to ages, reasons for coming here, the lot.

Males outnumber females who come here by 68,367, but the females are older. The biggest group among the males are those aged between 36 and 49. For the females it is between 50 and 64. The girls, too, can rally well with the over 65s.

The miserable truth is that most of the visitors come to Sydney and never get any further. The survey does not put it as crudely as that but it does say that 51.9 percent make New South Wales the main point of their stay. Victoria gets 16.8 percent, Queensland 11.6 percent, Western Australia 6.5 percent, South Australia 3.3 percent, Canberra 1.5 percent, Tasmania a miserable 1.0 percent and the NT 0.7 percent. A curious 6.7 percent did not reveal where they were.

The curious thing is that after all the expense in getting here how brief the stay is. Sixty-one percent of them come here either on holidays or to see relatives and 27 percent are wandering round in under a week and just on 18 percent stay less than two weeks.

The Poms are good stayers, half of them stay for a month to six months. The New Zealanders hang around pretty well. It might even be hard to get rid of some New Zealanders, but the Japanese move through with the speed of light. They have the biggest percentage of holiday-makers of all groups and 61.5 percent stay less than a week and 20.7 percent stay less than two weeks. What's more, the fellers tend to travel on their own. We had 21,746 Japanese males and 8958 females, although it must be conceded that among the holiday-makers there were 8745 males and 6117 females. Even so, the statistics prove a fascinating difference between Americans and Japanese. In the US as soon as the old man goes to his grave, his lady takes off. We had, for example, 16,620 US male holiday-makers and 18,347 females.

Those of us who went away spent $580 million: visitors who came spent $343 million.

Now here is the Tops of the Pops of the places we went to. New Zealand was number one with 20.9 percent. Then came the UK and Ireland, 18.3 percent; the US, 9.2 percent; Fiji, 5.1 percent; Singapore, 4.0 percent; Hong Kong, 3.9 percent; Italy, 3.7 percent; Greece, 3.2 percent; Indonesia, 2.9 percent; and Papua New Guinea, 2.2 percent.

It is fascinating to study which are the trendy places. Hong Kong and Singapore are way down from 1972, but you've got to take a trip to the Greek Islands — that's up from 14,000-odd to over 31,000. Trips have doubled to Germany, and Japan is terribly IN — 7316 to 17,231. New Zealand is booming, 113,508 to 202,703. The US has more than doubled but the greatest growth of all is sheer, old-fashioned love for the Old Country. In 1972 the numbers were 43,912 and in 1977, 177,389. When we go away, 58.4 percent go for a holiday, 19.4 percent to see relatives and only 10.9 percent for business.

Source: More dollars going than coming, The Bulletin, 19 December 1978. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
