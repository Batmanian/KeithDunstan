A strange path to rectitude

5 min read.

First published 21 Feb 1978

The 1978 Craft Festival opens with Premier Hamer and Eric Westbrook in YOU CAN MAKE IT T-shirts, 10,000 ceramic bricks to decorate, and a stained glass panel for the front door that the wife quietly had a professional redo — leaving Dunstan to wonder if by December the whole city will be praying for the return of Norm.

LADIES and gentlemen, I am trying to do the right thing. Back in the 1880s the government of Victoria was obsessed with morality. It tried passionately to keep us in church pews, to stop us from drinking and smoking, and to keep us respectfully quiet on Sundays.

In the 1970s there is a strange new morality. We have a government which becomes very disturbed if we don't pant, jog, and indulge in off-beat cultural activities like pot-making and hanging on the wall rugs of vegetable-dyed, shaggy, hand-woven wool.

Last year the Life, Be In It Campaign was big. All through the parks the government built these peculiar obstacle courses of green-treated timber, enabling one to vault, jump, climb, chin the bar and such, during one's jog. There was a huge "Norm" TV campaign inspired by Monahan, Dayman and Adams.

Norm was the slob who didn't use his muscles and sat drinking cans in front of television. Now I see that Norm is to be retired from Victorian TV, at least during 1978. Apparently too many people decided they would love to be like Norm and stayed in front of their boob tubes.

So now we are on to a slightly different tack. We are having the Craft Festival, which is to last the whole darned year, and we had the grand opening last week. It all took place in the Domain on a Sunday morning in front of the Myer Music Bowl.

Premier Hamer and Director of the Arts Eric Westbrook both were there wearing T-shirts with the message YOU CAN MAKE IT across their chests. Dick Hamer told us that with shorter working hours, earlier retirement, long-service leave and such it was vital that we get stuck into this type of personal fulfilment. Thereupon thousands of balloons were let off into the sky.

Now here was the personal fulfilment available for us, that very minute. A kindly firm had supplied 10,000 unglazed ceramic bricks. The idea was that each of us take a brick, decorate it with our own inspired pattern, hand it back, then they would fire it and use it in a path through the gardens. Thus our personal creativity would be preserved for ever.

We all sat on the grass and began to gouge our bricks. By 1 pm the great brick path was approaching completion. Forty percent did pictures of animals, flowers and plants; 40 percent did freedom patterns; and the last 20 percent gave us messages.

We will have a brick there for ever which says My Mum Is Great, another which says Sydney Loves Melbourne, another which says This Tile Was Done In Memory Of Me, and at least 20 were created out of devotion to Elvis. Would you believe someone drew a picture of Dick Hamer and added Hamer Made it Happen. And there was the one which said obscurely This Tile Is Dedicated To Invisible Gnome Radio.

All through the gardens there were booths with craft people right there, in full cry so to speak. There was a bearded fellow with a bloody great adze and he was thudding at a table top right there in front of our eyes. Another team was making leather saddle bags; there was a chap making stained glass windows, a wood turner and a basket maker.

I have been the first to co-operate in all these government enthusiasms. Stained glass was first. I was dying to have a stained glass panel for our front door. So I went to the glass place, bought a glass cutter, lots of sheets of stained glass and lengths of lead.

You need much space, and I concede considerable patience was shown when there was broken glass and dripping solder all over the dining room table. The finished result, I thought, was superb. The light shining through all those pretty glass flowers. Admittedly there were a few trifling apertures of open daylight and my wife did not comment on them. The following week she called in a professional stained glass person and had it re-arranged somewhat. She didn't show me the cheque.

Last week, with some excitement, we made genuine sourdough bread, the dinkum stuff right out of the Klondike. It didn't rise quite as much as I might have anticipated. Indeed the loaf was only slightly over an inch high, but the flavor was marvellous — once you got used to it.

We haven't got into pottery yet. But some of our friends are into it very deeply. One of the problems in this is: What do you do with all the pots you make? You give them to your... ah... friends. Already we have received some remarkable pots and no doubt there will be more during Craft Festival '78.

Who knows? By December we may be praying for the return of Norm.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: A strange path to rectitude, The Bulletin, 21 February 1978. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
