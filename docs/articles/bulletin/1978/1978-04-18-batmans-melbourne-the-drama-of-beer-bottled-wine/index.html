The drama of beer-bottled wine

5 min read.

First published 18 Apr 1978

After attending a Brown Brothers Home Bottling Seminar where the winemakers proved a 1962 red sealed with a crown cap beat the same wine under cork, Dunstan overcomes his cultural inhibitions and finally makes the switch — bottling his cabernet-malbec in beer bottles, then wondering if the BYO restaurants will still treat him with charm.

FRIENDS, I have to confess it to somebody; I have just done my first wine-bottling in beer bottles with crown seals. It is difficult to describe the emotional shock, the magnitude of the cultural breakthrough and the hours of pondering that went into this decision.

Oh, the ground rules were clear enough. Would you believe that Brown Bros at Milawa, Victoria, have just held a Home Bottling Seminar. Now Brown Bros is a distinguished and very old (1889) family vineyard. They have been to the home bottlers what Veuve Clicquot has been to the champagne trade.

All right, at this seminar Ross Brown, who was acting as senior lecturer, looked sad, and said, corks were not what they used to be. "We have found it very difficult to find a cork that absolutely meets our requirements," he said.

So at the vineyard they conducted an experiment. Back in 1962 they put a red wine into two bottles. They put a cork in one and a crown seal on the other. Two weeks ago they opened them up. Both wines were still good, except the bottle with the crown seal was quality drinking wine — better colour, better finish.

So the conclusion was obvious. If you did not plan to put down your bottles as if you were an ancient French chateau, what was the point of corks? Corks cost around six cents each. Crown seals are nothing — you get a whole bag full for 50 cents and you can put them on in half the time.

So I went home to worry. I remember four or five years ago the president of one of the great champagne houses visited Melbourne. If I remember correctly he was from Clicquot. I put this question: "In all the early stages of champagne making you use crown seals on your bottles. The bottles you sell to us even have a lip for crown seal. Would it make any difference to the champagne if you sold it with crown seals?"

"None whatever, my friend," he said. "We would adore to sell our champagne that way. The champagne cork is big, it is layered, it is very expensive and sometimes unreliable and has to be wired down. The crown seal would be more efficient. The only trouble is the drama."

"What would you have?" There was a beautiful shrug of the shoulders, "No POP, just Phut."

As I rolled in bed debating my decision, I thought back to the heyday of home bottling in the early sixties. We used to own our own oak barrel which we would rail up to the vineyard and Brown's would send it back at around 12 shillings a gallon for high quality red and white wines.

Then they started to use aluminium beer-type barrels and now it is all plastic; the bulk wine comes in 25-litre plastic containers. In the old days a bottle of wine cost you two shillings a bottle — now your crown sealed object is at least a dollar.

And I remembered, too, how trendy it all became. Around 1966-67 it was considered important to put a little tone into one's bottling. We used fancy long corks and we called at the Balzac Restaurant on Saturday mornings to go through their empties. We would pick them over for the very best and most beautiful French bottles. I used to go particularly for the pale amber ones, the Montrachets and such.

Yes, the hunt went all over for the right wine bottles. Some of my mates in the Press gallery at Canberra used to get some terrific coups on rubbish tin mornings outside the French and German embassies. We always picked rubbish tins in Toorak — St Georges Road and Heyington Place residents were always the most superior drinkers.

And that wasn't all. The ultra-trendies used to call in artists and design their own gorgeous labels for their home bottlings. There was one label which depicted a lovely Michelangelo-type cupid and out of his dear little penis a stream of wine curved up into the air and down into a wine glass. That label on the empty looked utterly splendid.

The big moment came yesterday. There was no trouble at all in gaining sufficient bottles. Our street doesn't dig much Montrachet. Some families get through two dozen Fosters before every rubbish bin day. So all the seals went on last night — plunk, plunk, plunk.

But a man must tell you more about the home-bottling seminar. We have just reached the stage where red wine is coming out of the nation's ears, and for the first time in a decade really high-quality bulk wine is available for home bottling.

So Brown's had their seminar, two sessions a day over two days and Ye Gods — enthusiasts turned up by the hundred. Brown's PR man, John Elton, did the demonstrating, showed them how to put the tube in the container, suck the wine through, how to get the siphon going, how to fill up the bottle, how to put on the crown seal or drive in the cork.

Apart from the crown seals he told us another interesting thing. Good kitchen cleanliness is sufficient. Provided your bottles are unstained and haven't been used for the garden arsenic or killing aunty, washing with hot water around 82 degrees Celsius will get rid of those wild yeasts and such. Fancy chemicals are not necessary. Put your plastic tubing right down to the bottom of the bottle and treat your wine at all times as something that detests oxygen.

So friends, that is my confession. One thing is worrying me now. What happens when I go to one of these smart BYOs — Clichys, Baxters or something like that? Will they treat me with the same courteous charm when I hand over my cabernet-malbec in a beer bottle?
