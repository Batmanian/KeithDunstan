What did YOU do for private enterprise, daddy?

5 min read.

First published 17 Jan 1978

Bob Ansett's relentless battle to break the airport car-hire monopoly held by his father Sir Reginald Ansett's Avis franchise, told through his series of increasingly elaborate PR stunts.

IF ONE could make a 1978 prediction it is as inevitable as the sun coming up that Bob Ansett is going to win his battle to get into the airports. His determination is awe-inspiring. For one thing he has this anal complex. That is, everything in sight is spotless, not a sheet, not a chair, not a flower out of place.

It has been said that an anal complex is caused by your mother not changing your nappies often enough. It is a very suspect theory. Whatever caused it in Bob Ansett he always has this beautiful, clean desk, nothing on it whatever, not even a loose pen. Everything is always filed. An untidy desk is an untidy mind, he says. It makes one flinch at the thought of the devastation of one's own desk.

His father now owns Avis through Ansett Airlines. Therefore there is the incredible situation that Ansett even controls the airport rent-a-car monopoly, which young Bob is fighting so desperately. His most recent fight has been to challenge it through the Trade Practices Commission and we await the result with interest.

It is untrue to say that he is fuming in a corner refusing to speak to his father, Sir Reginald. He is prepared to concede that he must separate the two, Sir Reg his father and Sir Reg chairman of Ansett Transport Industries Ltd. What about that printed statement that you couldn't think of a worse organisation to take over Avis than Ansett? "It was taken right out of context," he said. "I can think of somebody worse — TAA."

It is not generally realised what a formidable opponent Sir Reg has in 40-year-old Bob. They are two very different people. Sir Reg is brilliant, tough, austere, not easy to approach. Until recently it was almost impossible to get him to appear on any kind of public affairs TV program. Young Bob is all smiles with the open-handed "come in" approach. It smacks a little of Dale Carnegie and how to win friends.

Most of us are well acquainted with the story of how his parents were divorced, how he lived in the U.S. from the time he was 10 years old until he was 30, how he became a gridiron footballer, had football scholarships at the Universities of Utah, San Jose and Santa Barbara, how he became marketing manager for a bakery in San Diego, all that sort of thing.

He returned to Australia to manage Budget for Eric McIlree of Avis and upon his death he acquired the firm as a separate deal backed by the Australian Guarantee Corporation. That was three years ago and ever since there has been this single-minded, non-stop battle to get Budget into the airports.

"Yes, I admit it's been an obsession," said Bob. You have seen that TV ad of course. There's Bob standing lonely outside the cyclone fence of the airport, like one of the faithful banished from his promised land. But there have been lots of other things and it seemed a good idea to get Bob to list his stunts.

No. 1. He organised a procession all through the streets of Melbourne. He had Des Tuddenham, the footballer on a pogo stick. He had Vic Browne, the marathon rider, on a bicycle and he had Paul Jarman, the jockey on a horse. They were all carrying labels: "This is the only way I can beat Budget's prices."

No. 2. He hired a twin-engine Aero Commander. He painted it in Budget colors, called it the Budget Bird, put seven executives inside, plus some gorgeous Budget PR girls. Then they flew to 20 cities around the country. They went wherever there was a Budget office and an airport. They collected 10,000 signatures protesting about the airport monopoly.

No. 3. The American Chamber of Commerce runs an ad man of the year competition. This was Bob Ansett's stunt which he did in every capital city. I ask you to visualise a large dining hall filled with top business executives. On every plate there is a Budget Rent-a-Car T-shirt. Suddenly there is a mass entry led by Bob, followed by his executives and a whole stream of pretty Budget birds all in Budget gear. He tells all these staid people to take off their jackets and put on the Budget T-shirts. He has himself interviewed by a radio man, then he gets the whole dining hall community singing to the Budget Rent-a-Car song. Incredible.

In December 1976 he won the Victorian ad-man award and was runner up for the national title.

No. 4. He now has his own Melbourne Cup, complete with gold cup. Just before the other affair he throws a party for executives, borrows a tape of an old Melbourne Cup and puts it on the screen. The difference is that he has changed all the names of the horses and he has a new dubbed-in commentary. So before the screening nobody knows which horse will win and the guests can lay bets. You can imagine the names of the horses. He has Freedom of Choice by Monopoly out of Cyclone Fence; Airport Entry, Overdue Reform and even Bashful Bob.

Naturally the winning horse for the last Cup was Airport Entry. The event will be on again this year.

Are you starting to get the picture? The Ansett v Ansett struggle should be one of the most fascinating for 1978.
