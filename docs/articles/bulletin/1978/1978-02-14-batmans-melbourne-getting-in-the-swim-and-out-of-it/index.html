Getting in the swim, and out of it

5 min read.

First published 14 Feb 1978

The reality of South Yarra swimming pool ownership — an hour's labour a day vacuuming leaves, wrestling with pH levels, replacing pumps at $200 a time, and suffering exploding underwater lights — proves more exhausting than a hectare of lawn.

THE JANUARY 31 edition of The Bulletin had a special section on swimming pools. Foolishly, the editor did not ask Batman for his expert opinion. Stand by for Episode Three in the great Batman Moving Saga.

When we were searching somewhat desperately for a new dwelling, a clever real estate man took us to South Yarra and showed us the place of our dreams. Glory be, out the back there was this swimming pool.

The man purred: "You will note," he said, "that here is a house that requires practically no labor. The front garden is exquisitely paved. In the rear you have this lovely swimming pool. No need any longer to go to Nice, Antibes or Mordialloc. Here you have your own glorious, sheltered watering place. You laze here in your deck chair by the water, and will you please note, there is enchantment without a blade of grass. No longer will you have to pull out your dragon lawnmower..."

It was the word "lawnmower" that got me. I couldn't get to the bank manager quickly enough to start writing cheques.

Friends, before you suffer a similar fate to me, listen. Swimming pool owners are the new laborers of the suburbs. Remember that old line about car ownership being a substitute for sex. You get your sex by polishing, out there rubbing the duco every day. That doesn't happen any more. The cars go to the car wash. Instead you get your sex cleaning and rubbing your swimming pool.

I reckon a swimming pool is worth an hour's labor a day. Hell, an acre of lawn was easier. We looked out the window on the first morning, expecting to see this lovely aqua scene. You could hardly see the water for gum leaves and soot.

There's a big gum tree up by the south fence. "You'll always get gum leaves when a south wind is blowing," said my wife consolingly. OK. The south wind blew for a week and daily I was fishing out leaves with a fish net.

The wind switched to the north and, fair dinkum, the pool was filled with the debris of three liquidambers in the front garden. Liquidambers are marvellous, they can shed stuff all the year round. It is unbelievable how a swimming pool will pick up leaves. A leaf, happy on its course half a mile away, will change direction if there is a possibility of falling into a swimming pool.

It's not just leaf catching, there's lots more. Two or three times a week you have to vacuum. You have a long thing, twice the length of a barge pole, attached to a long pipe. You have to be careful of all the tubes. A lawyer bloke down the street got his foot caught several weeks back and took a header into his "Bali Hai Freeform" fully clothed.

OK, slowly and dolefully you push your vacuum up and down the pool, getting rid of all the soot and pollution.

Then your pool might go cloudy, or it might go green. You've got to make sure your pH factor is right and they give you a little box of chemicals. You check your pH every second day, very mysterious, something like a pregnancy test. Then you have to change the diatomaceous earth in the filter. You get an enormous bagful of stuff resembling plaster of paris, and that messy job has to be done every couple of months. It gets all over the rhododendrons. The chlorine goes in every second night.

Then they give you a what-to-do chart — what to do when your pool goes green, what to do when your pool goes hazy, what to do when it precipitates like a vintage white burgundy.

I can tell you we had our crises. First we had air bleeding. I mean air was getting into the filter system and the whole darned thing wouldn't pump. Then one terrible morning our pump expired. "Sit down and have a whisky dear, I have bad news," said my wife. I knew immediately how bad it was, the swimming pool. We had to get a new pump. That was $200.

Then we have an underwater light. This is your star turn. You are having friends for dinner, you throw a switch, and lo, there's your pool outside in a flood of light like the Acapulco Hilton. Twice we had a disaster. The light went for three minutes and PHUTT. The darned thing filled with water and expired. That cost us around $70 a time.

I mean it's like three children, an afghan or an English sheepdog, it's a running continuing problem that's always there, even if you are away for a week at Aunt Mildred's.

But how do you get out of it once you've got one? I understand that in the United States it is a very specialised trade turning swimming pools into tennis courts. Alas, ours is not big enough and I don't think it is really the right shape for a wine cellar.

But I do have another thought. It would make a ripper of a compost heap.
