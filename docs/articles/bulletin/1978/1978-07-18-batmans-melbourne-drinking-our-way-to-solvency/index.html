Drinking our way to solvency

4 min read.

First published 18 Jul 1978

Bob Hawke's public pledge to give up alcohol triggers a meditation on guilt and national finances — the Federal Government raises $475 million on beer and $62 million on spirits, meaning every glass of scotch contributes to some decent lad's public service salary or even the new RAN submarine, a civic duty that wine-drinkers and smokers share in their own way.

MR HAWKE is a most unusual fellow. At a Press conference he made the formal announcement that he had given up the drink. He had been "on the wagon," said he, for several months and now when he wished to imbibe in public he drank straight tonic.

I know how he feels. Those of us who indulge in what we used to think were fine manly vices are surrounded by horrid feelings of guilt. There has not been such a forceful campaign against drink since the early days of World War I. You will remember then it was the thing to do to sign the pledge in public.

Lord Kitchener, with that awe-inspiring noble gaze of his, announced that he would fight the Kaiser by giving up liquor and he hoped others would do the same. King George V banned wines, spirits and beer from the Royal Household for the duration of the war. His private secretary, Lord Stanfordham, announced: "The King is prepared to set the example of giving up alcohol in order, so far as his Majesty is concerned, that the rich and poor may be treated alike."

Thereupon similar announcements came from most of the members of the Asquith cabinet, the Archbishops of Canterbury and York, leading government officials and judges and the former Governors of Victoria, Lord Brassey and Lord Sydenham.

One can only presume that Bob Hawke's act will have a similar effect throughout the trade union movement, that leading members of the ACTU, the Amalgamated Engineering Union, the Waterside Workers', Postal Workers', Engine Drivers' and others will follow his valiant lead.

Regularly we get these stories about the menace of drink. The Victorian Temperance Alliance told us the other day that on any one night in Victoria 10,000 men were totally drunk.

But if you think the drinkers are suffering from feelings of guilt, imagine how the smokers are feeling. Suddenly the anti-smokers have become extraordinarily aggressive. The prime example of this took place just last month in France.

Monsieur Denis Valet, a noted anti-smoker, was sitting in a no-smoking compartment of the Strasbourg-Lyons Express. He was distressed to see the passenger opposite light a cigar. Politely M Valet pointed to the no-smoking sign. Impolitely the cigar merchant puffed a cloud of Havana smoke in his face. The furious Valet pulled the emergency stop handle. The train screamed to a halt. The guard fined Valet on the grounds that the alarm was to be used only in a case of specific danger.

My informant for this information is London's Daily Mail. "Precisement," said M Valet — or to get back to the translation — "I found myself in great danger since this smoker was endangering my health." Valet then sued and the French Railways had to cough up 2000 francs for "the moral and material damage" to Valet.

We had a similar case in NSW last April when a magistrate made a decision in favour of Brian McBride, president of the Non-Smokers' Rights Movement. McBride sued a man whom he said assaulted him on a bus by blowing smoke in his face.

As a result of all this, non-smoker movements are militant both in New South Wales and Victoria. The object clearly is to reduce smoking only to consenting adults in private.

However there is a vital point unsolved in all this. If you have a feeling of guilt over the demon drink then I have a reverse feeling. Every time I have a glass of scotch or a bottle of beer I know that at least half of it is going towards the salary of one of our decent lads in the public service, in the armed forces; or maybe I am even drinking towards the cost of that new submarine we have just bought for the RAN.

According to the latest year book the Federal Government raises $475,963,000 on beer and $62,847,000 on spirits. I don't smoke but I wonder if the smoker as he lights up gets a feeling of pride that maybe he is contributing to a widow's pension or supporting the Australian Ambassador in Paris. The tax on cigarettes raises $453,739,000 and on cigarette papers $806,000.

Yes, I am doing my humble bit with my whisky and beer drinking. However if the Prime Minister would care to switch my allegiance in these matters I would be happy to give it my careful consideration should he care to stop raising money on liquor and switch his tax to yoghurt, carrot juice and orange. Indeed, I am sure Bob Hawke would be more than proud if he placed a tax on tonic water.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Drinking our way to solvency, The Bulletin, 18 July 1978. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
