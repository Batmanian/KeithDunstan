Cashing in on nostalgia

4 min read.

First published 11 Jul 1978

The Victorian Government's $100,000 competition for a "Melbourne Monument" to rival the Eiffel Tower prompts Dunstan's winning entry, rebuilding Old Melbourne complete with Cole's Book Arcade, cable trams, the Port Phillip Hotel's punkahs, and all the great Victorian drapery stores with their real counters and real service, a nostalgia trap that would draw tourists by the million.

NOW THAT the Victorian Government is offering $100,000 for a "Melbourne Monument" naturally we are all interested. This is almost as much as it normally offers for finding a murderer or a rapist. They want something on a par with the Eiffel Tower, the Pyramids, the Statue of Liberty or Disneyland — something which will bring droves of tourists.

Barry Humphries has suggested a scale model of Ayers Rock, which will be named after Mayor Irvin Rockman and will be called Rockman's Rock. It would help to dwarf the great Nauru Corporation building which already has ruined the Melbourne skyline.

A comedian named Rod Quantock, who is seeking election to the City of Melbourne Council, suggested a large mechanical man who would stride up Swanston Street, a landmark for Melbourne, something never to be forgotten. It would have a mechanical arm that would pick up demonstrators on their way to the Vietnam games found at the other end, and tuck them under his armpit.

Now I have my own ideas on this. People don't want to see things as they are or will be; they want to see things as they used to be. I would bring back Cole's Book Arcade, the greatest bookshop in the world. You will remember you could wander for hours among the books, a full-time orchestra, a cage full of live cockatoos, monkeys and parrots. Children were allowed to slide down the banisters and invited to whistle inside. Then outside the front door there were the little mechanical men who turned handles which operated the big gyratory fairground machines. Some of these I found up at the Science Museum and it would be presented with exactly the same sort of Stiffy and Mo comedy that it would have had in the 1890s.

I should mention that in the 1890s many people used to come to this area on a Sunday afternoon. The monster stores even had picture galleries, free and open to all. Sport only during peak hours — other times have waits of anything up to half an hour.

So let us bring back the cable trams, running them every four minutes in, out and around the Melbourne Monument. Naturally we would keep all automobiles out of the area. Other vintage services might intrigue the tourists. We could have two postal deliveries a day. We could even have the post offices open on Saturdays.

This has all come about because Melbourne is nervous about its ability to attract tourists. Therefore we should look at causes. Melbourne is the prime example of Marshal McLuhan's global village. We are so utterly un-different. We've got McDonalds, we've got Colonel Sanders, we've got Pizza Hut and we have a range of vertical towers superbly like Dallas, like Kansas City, like Phoenix, like Atlanta, like almost anywhere.

I would bring back Menzies, Scotts, the Oriental, the Barclay. Soon for the other Melbourne hotels. You will remember the Port Phillip was just by Young & Jackson's and it was a marvellous labyrinth of bars. In the big public bar they had great punkahs and on century-hot days they would creak back and forth sending gentle ripples across the beer. I would bring back the little oyster bars where you could go in, buy your pot of beer and have a dozen oysters for two and six. I would have all the old drapery stores — Mutual Store, Craig Williamson, Robertson & Moffat; yes and Ogg's Pharmacy. These would have their old awnings and splendid verandahs. Then the stores would have fair dinkum counters with people really serving behind them and when you went in you could ask for what you wanted and these people would go away and get it for you. The modern tourist would get very excited over that.

So that's my entry for the $100,000. It doesn't mean that I am against the statue to Henry Bolte. It might even look rather picturesque but I wouldn't want it to be more than 20 feet high. Sheets, you notice. We won't have any of that metric nonsense anywhere near the Monument.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Cashing in on nostalgia, The Bulletin, 11 July 1978. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
