The perils of a bicycle built for two

5 min read.

First published 05 Dec 1978

A two-year odyssey to import a custom-built touring tandem from California, with its Taylor Brothers frame from Stockton-on-Tees, French cantilever brakes, Japanese disc brakes and Italian seat pillars, ends with a $225 air freight bill, $1063 in customs duty, five months of letters to manufacturers and ministers to claim an exemption, and a final cost of $3000 for a bicycle that does everything promised.

IF YOU are contemplating a luxury import then please take note of my extraordinary and dismal tale.

It is five years now since first I became fascinated by tandem bicycles. They have a number of advantages. For one thing if you are travelling with your wife there are no arguments about pace. She can't burn you off on the hills, you are compelled to travel at the same speed.

For this reason it is a gloriously sociable machine. You can chat all the time, and the person who rides behind, known technically as the stoker, can give helpful advice. My wife gives a great deal of helpful advice. Indeed a frightened stoker is the original eloquent backseat driver.

Most people who have tried it agree that tandem riding is a thing apart, as exhilarating as skiing or high speed skating. Feed your stoker adequately, give him/her plenty of muesli bars, and you can get cruising speeds around 40 km/h.

But most tandems have a deep and abiding problem — it is impossible to stop the darned things. They build up tremendous momentum and require three and a half times the stopping power of a normal bicycle. Their spokes break and they go through cotter pins like peanuts.

In late 1976 I decided it would be lovely to have a tandem that suffered none of these problems, a touring machine that would take you, with your luggage, over 1000 kilometres or more.

I noticed there was a tandem shop in London so I wrote them a letter, asking them to make me one. Poms tend to be like that, they didn't even reply. I tried valiantly all over Australia to get a machine of my specifications, but no manufacturer could get the parts.

Then I saw an advertisement in an American magazine: MAIL ORDER TANDEMS WITH A DIFFERENCE, it said. "All you have to do is install the pedals, adjust the seats and ride them home from the airport. Yes, we ship to the nearest jetport, wherever you are."

The ad, in small print, did say that the average price for these tandems was around $1200. Good tandems are made to measure. You must have the right frame size for the pilot up forward and the right size for the stoker down aft.

Well, the fellow who was making these tandems was Bill McCready, of Bud's Bike Shop, Claremont, California. I was so desperate for this machine I telephoned him STD. He took the order and gave his recommendations for the ideal machine. The frame was to be made by the tandem specialists, the Taylor Brothers of Stockton-on-Tees, Cleveland County, England. The gears, cranks and chain wheels were to come from France. The brakes, ah they were something, massive cantilever caliper brakes front and rear from France plus rear wheel disc brakes from Japan, enough to stop a truck. Each wheel was to have a remarkable 40 spokes and there would be special sealed hubs and bottom bracket from the US. The saddles also were American and the seat pillars from Italy.

So I hung up the telephone and dreamed of the beauty of this thing.

Nine months went by and not a thing happened. Inspired by those tearful Telecom ads on TV I rang California again.

"What's happened to my bike?" I asked Mr McCready.

"We still haven't received your frame from the Taylor Brothers in England." He explained that Messrs Taylor made all their frames by hand and then indulged in the most beautiful paintwork, lovingly applying coat after coat after coat. I could get the picture, it was like producing Lancias for Malcolm Fraser. I suggested that if they were so slow why didn't he get his frames elsewhere.

Mr McCready was shocked. He said: "When you are dealing with the best in the world, there isn't anybody else."

What with the passage of time, the bicycle which originally was to cost $1200 rose to $1600. It was in July, just over a year since I first contacted Mr McCready, that a call came from the Customs agent.

"Your bike has arrived."

Oh joy.

"There are a few problems," he continued. "Qantas want $225 air freight."

"Ye-es."

"And you won't get the bike until you have paid a bill of $1063.13 sales and customs duty."

"WHATTTTTTT. You've got it wrong."

"No, that's right, 1063 dollars and thirteen cents."

"But... but nobody ever paid duty like that in history. This is a bicycle. Do they think it's a Mercedes?"

"I'm sure you will get a refund. What you must do is write to two bicycle manufacturers and get letters from them explaining that they do not propose to make such a bike in Australia."

I didn't write to two, I wrote to three, and between them they covered 90 percent of the bicycles made in Australia. I wrote to every one in sight including the Minister for Business and Consumer Affairs, Wal Fife. My appeal was rejected. There was a manufacturer in South Australia, Elliott Bros, who made tandems. Malcolm Elliott very kindly wrote a letter saying, sure he made tandems, but not touring tandems of this kind. Canberra then produced yet another bicycle builder in South Australia and the arguing had gone on for five months.

It was obvious that unless I could find a tandem lover in a ministerial role my battle was hopeless.

My wife did some arithmetic and she was very understanding: "Do you realise," said she sweetly, "your bicycle has cost just on $3000?"

However, I must admit that the bicycle is everything I hoped it would be and my wife is in a very sound position. Where would I ever find another stoker who would put up with me?
