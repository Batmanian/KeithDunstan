The phoenix that rose from the slums

4 min read.

First published 28 Nov 1978

A Melbourne University seminar on the inner suburbs, tracing five periods from migrant gateway to working-class heartland to light industry to postwar Italian and Greek settlement to the current trendy invasion, confronts Dunstan with his own gentrification of a former working-class South Yarra street, complete with cast-iron twiddledy bits, hanging baskets, and three Porsches within 100 metres.

THIS IS a confession. I'm a middle class trendy. That is I'm almost a classic statistic. Twelve months ago I moved into a South Yarra street which a decade ago was indisputably working class. Now the houses in our street have fancy high brick walls, lashings of new cast-iron twiddledy bits have been added to the verandas, hanging baskets have come in Babylonian style, the chic young architects have left Victorian villa fronts like J. C. Williamson scenery and completely remodelled down aft with plate glass, air-cond, ww carps, c/heating, and en suite bth rms.

There is virtually nil space for garages but within 100 metres of our place there are three Porsches, two Lamborghinis, three Mercedes, two Range Rovers and a $36,000 12 cylinder Jaguar. Do have a pang of sympathy for those vehicles. They spend their lives parked out in the street.

I'm telling you all this because I have just attended a fascinating seminar at Melbourne University titled "The Inner Suburbs — Past, Present and Future."

We had it officially about the trendies. Dr Graeme Davison, author of The Decline and Fall of Marvellous Melbourne, told us there were five distinct periods in the life of the inner suburbs. Between 1840 and 1870 this was the home for the migrants when almost everybody was a migrant. Between 1870 and 1900 they provided the homes for the working class. From 1900 to 1945 factories and light industry moved in. Then from 1945 on this was the great Arthur Calwell era when Carlton went Italian, Prahran Greek and the inner areas once again received the immigrants. As they gained affluence they moved out and after 1970 the trendies started the new invasion.

Oh, we saw the inner suburbs from all different angles. The Lord Mayor, Cr Irvin Rockman, told of the danger of Melbourne turning into a doughnut. Population in the inner city was steadily declining.

However the doughnut theory was anything but tasty to some of the academics. Dr Michael Jones, director of the Australian Centre for Local Government Studies in Canberra, said bluntly it wasn't the function of planning to bolster up real estate values in Bourke Street, Collins Street and elsewhere.

He claimed the doughnut theory was all nonsense, and he came out with statements that would have turned the hair grey of many a city councillor. He claimed that the city was too centralised now, and there were too many job opportunities in the city area. Too many people were fighting their way into the centre of the hole.

He gave the figures that between 1947 and 1976 there was a 36 percent decline in population in the inner suburbs, but not because of any dreadful ailment. It was affluence, people were upgrading their houses and congestion was less intense. A quarter of the population loss was due to this, a quarter was due to the building of hospitals, offices and such and half was due to the disappearance of the old depression-wartime classic, the boarding house.

Michael Jones was very keen about the inner suburbs. He even told of his antiseptic life in Canberra and how he missed Punt Road, Melbourne. He was even prepared to swear that he could sleep better in Punt Road with the traffic still drumming past at 4 am.

He is concerned about us trendies. He said: "I used to love to go into a Richmond restaurant and find it filled with Italians and Greeks, now I only find people like me."

He warned too that the inner suburbs would only remain attractive provided that they maintained a suitable population mix of all types. "It would be a great pity if we allowed in too many trendies."

Dr Miles Lewis, of the Urban Conservation Project, said the inner suburbs had been extraordinarily flexible. They had absorbed every large population movement right from the beginning and his advice to government was to intervene as little as possible.

Ann Latreille of The Age gave a paper simply on the joys of living in the inner suburbs. She reported that she had eight neighbors around her, a polyglot collection of everything from Maltese to Greeks, complete with dogs, cats, budgies and ferrets. There were four little corner shops in the street, a girls' home, a coffin factory round the corner.

"I get twitchy when I think of ever living anywhere else," she said. And she told of the life of never needing a car, going off with a shopping trolley and being able to walk to town during Bourke transport strikes. And it was a community, people all around, none of this utter aloneness of the outer suburbs, she said.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The phoenix that rose from the slums, The Bulletin, 28 November 1978. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
