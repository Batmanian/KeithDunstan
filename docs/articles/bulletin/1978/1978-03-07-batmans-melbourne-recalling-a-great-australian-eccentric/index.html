Recalling a great Australian eccentric

5 min read.

First published 07 Mar 1978

A Melbourne University Gallery exhibition marks Percy Grainger's birthday and directs visitors to the remarkable Grainger Museum at the university, a repository Grainger designed and partly built himself, containing his towelling suits, his mother's clothes, freeform music machines, intimate confessions, and 21 PhDs worth of yet-unexplored material.

IT WAS Percy Grainger's birthday last week, 27 years since he died in New York, aged 79, but his reputation continues to grow. He was, of course, the most marvellous eccentric that Australia ever produced; eccentric because he believed the conventional way was usually most absurd for doing anything. But then his eccentricity tended to obscure the fact that he was a pretty fair all-round genius — pianist, composer, painter, writer and innovator.

This week the Melbourne University Gallery opened an exhibition on Percy Grainger which will run well into April. It starts with the art work that Percy created from the age of four, pencil sketches of people, steam trains and such; there are his first attempts at making boats, self portraits of Percy in the nude, absolutely delightful water colors done by Percy when he was a young student in Germany back in the 1890s; cover designs for his music by his own hand, musical instruments that he invented...

Then there are such items as the painting of Barstow, California. It is heart-rendingly labelled "In Memory of My Beloved Mother. Birthday gift July 3, 1922. Barstow where we were happy." The significant thing about that date is that three months earlier his mother had thrown herself out of an 18-storey New York office window; a tragedy that for a year almost drove him to insanity.

There are lots of paintings of Percy in the exhibition including two beautiful oils of the young Percy by Rupert Bunny and another, not so good, by his wife Ella. Ah, but wait, Ella's is an extraordinary painting, it depicts Percy in his towelling clothing.

Back before World War I Percy and his mother, Rose, became enchanted by the idea of towelling as the ideal clothing, cool in summer and warm in winter. So, inspired by the colorful Maoris and the residents of the South Pacific, they made their own, to their own brilliant design. In the 1930s, Percy and wife Ella were still doing it. They were particularly delighted with the brilliantly colored Turkish towelling that they could buy in Australia. One of Percy's suits still on display at the Grainger Museum consists of a pair of towelling shorts, voluminous as worn by the British Army, Singapore rig, circa 1940, with Tyrolean type vest.

The exhibition is not to be missed, but the truth is that it gives one an opportunity to tell you about the Grainger Museum, also at the university, one of the nation's most remarkable institutions, getting better by the hour.

Percy Grainger founded this museum back in 1934. The celebrated pianist-composer lived as a vegetarian on cheese and hard tack, travelled second class, slept sitting up in railway carriages and spurned taxis, just so that he could save money for this museum. One time in New York he walked from the railway station to his hotel pushing all his gear in a wheelbarrow.

So he designed this building himself, even laid some of the bricks himself and you don't get any thanks from the hard-working archivists inside, Kay Dreyfus and Jan Whiteside, if you say it looks like a Brighton Comfort Station. It does from the outside. Percy's theory was that he wanted this Museum to be a great repository for English and Nordic music, to tell the story of an era.

And so it does, but for more than three decades it was closed, except by special invitation, and sorting the mountains of material has been an epic effort. As one lady told me: "There are at least 21 PhDs in there, just waiting to be picked up."

Percy, of course, gathered material from all his composer friends like Grieg, Delius, Quilter and such. He wrote to Cyril Scott, Roger Quilter and Balfour Gardiner and actually demanded suits of clothes. "Nothing in poor condition," he said. They're on display on sort of dummy Myer models. Every letter that Percy ever wrote to anyone in his family and vice versa is there, thousands of them. The clothes his mother wore back to the 1890s are there, so too are Ella's clothes, his clothes, locks of his hair back to the age of four — literally nothing has ever been thrown out.

There are cartons simply labelled "Unidentifiable Erk," which contain odd things like household lists, what's needed in the way of groceries, income tax invoices. Ella is still alive in New York, aged 89, married to Stewart Melville, archivist of the Percy Grainger Library Society. The word is that the house at White Plains is still full of stuff and still nothing is being thrown out, and unquestionably another mountain of material will descend upon the museum.

The museum is now open to the public — there's a room full of antique pianos, there's the old music room in which Percy used to practise in London, re-created with the original furniture; in a glass case there's the little yacht Percy used to sail on Albert Park Lake when he was about 12 years old. There's his note on it to say that he carted it all around the world — Germany, England, USA. He never sailed it again, but it remained one of his most treasured possessions.

There are the extraordinary freeform music machines made out of everything from bicycle wheels to Scotch tape tins, photographs of his family, photographs of Princes Bridge, Melbourne, designed by his father no less. Buried away are his own fascinating confessions that he believed in flagellation, in whipping oneself, for releasing dangerous sexual violence. Everything is in the Grainger Museum, maybe the most honest outpourings ever from the soul of a Melburnian. He claimed that its true worth would be revealed 200 years after his death.

And I suspect that is absolutely right.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Recalling a great Australian eccentric, The Bulletin, 7 March 1978. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
