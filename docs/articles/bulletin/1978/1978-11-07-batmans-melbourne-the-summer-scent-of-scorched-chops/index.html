The summer scent of scorched chops

4 min read.

First published 07 Nov 1978

The annual barbecue season descends on Melbourne with its black pall of smoke, $3000 paved outdoor living spaces with redwood cedar tables, coin-in-the-slot units along the Yarra, the curious male prerogative of actually cooking, and the unfailing rule that the only suitable drink for scorched meat is an incomparably awful rough red out of a plastic bladder.

MY GOD, isn't it awful? We're invited to four barbecues next week. Repeatedly we are told that the national dish is the meat pie or the dim sim. It is not — it is the scorched chop and I for one am ready for a national unscorched meat policy.

In our part of the world the barbecue season always gets under way in November and a black pall of smoke rises above the city. The ecologists blame it on the unfortunate automobile. That's not true — the smoke is coming from burnt cow.

We have these coin-in-the-slot barbecue machines all along the Yarra, around Albert Park Lake and in all the smart city parks. If you want to get use of them you virtually have to book ahead as if you were trying to get in to see Joan Sutherland.

In the past decade the barbecue thing has got out of hand. From one end of the country to the other Australians have developed a passion for searing flesh out in the open. There's a word for it now and it appears in Sidney Baker's classic Australian Language and that's "barbecorgy" — an over-eating of scorched meat.

The truth is that "barbecue" is not a trendy new word, and it is not Australian. It dates back to the 16th century. The American Indians used to have a sort of wooden framework on which they called a barbecue. When we were children my father maltreated flesh from the butcher over a kerosene tin, which had a few holes in the side to let in the air.

That's not good enough any more. Barbecue parties come at all times, particularly Friday and Saturday nights, but the trendy time now is Sunday mornings. Out our way the trendy ones call it Matins or even worse, Morning Prayer. The gentlemen wear gay floppy shirts, fancy bits of silk around their necks and Italian sandals. The girls wear considerably less.

The equipment has certainly come on from the kerosene tin era. Could one suggest that a true barbecorgy scene would cost you $3000 or more? You've got to have your paved outdoor living space. Myer once called it the Outdoorium. Your paving should be in vintage handmade bricks. Then it is essential to have your redwood cedar table, seats, sling-back chairs, sun umbrella and your utterly authentic St Kilda Road street lamps lit by gas.

You've got to have your barbecue and you can get a little portable job for under $10. But if you do you will be terribly non-U. You can get an executive model for around $400.

I get the impression that crude, naked flame is almost finished. Electric and gas barbecues with heated volcanic rock are all the thing out our way. You can get them in stainless steel so that they won't rust in the rain. Super refined VIP models even have electric rotisseries.

Cooking in the garden is at least four times as expensive as cooking inside. It is fairly important to have your special barbecue tongs and your special floodlight to see whether the chops are finally turned to ash. The St Kilda Road gaslamp is only good for atmosphere.

It seems to be the last area where women's liberation has not invaded. It is a curious male prerogative to take over the barbecue. There are still occasions when I get forced into it. Two things always happen in consequence: I go out to buy chops for the barbecue. It becomes a day of total fire ban or it rains. I can remember some particularly poignant occasions when I have stood out in the rain under an umbrella chop-cooking while the guests have watched from inside.

Another thing I have against barbecues is the drink. There is a current belief that if you are eating scorched meat then the only thing that will go with it is an incomparably awful red wine. Some wine companies even produce a special Barbecue Rough Red. One suspects that this is so defiantly gutsy they are not even game to put it in their plastic casks, or their plastic bladders as I prefer to call them.

Dear friends, I try to give you the haunting atmosphere of summer in suburbia. All up and down the street there is the bushfire smell of carbonised sausage. The screech of the cicadas is trying to compete with the throb of the electric guitars from the hi-fis. The blowies are circling like Spitfires overhead and there is panic in at least four places because guests have parked across driveways. When you have a city barbecorgiastically inclined, Sundays can be very trying.
