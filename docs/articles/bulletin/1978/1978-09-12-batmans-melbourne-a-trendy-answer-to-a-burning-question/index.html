A trendy answer to a burning question

5 min read.

First published 12 Sep 1978

After moving to a house with Victorian iron grates and marble mantelpieces, Dunstan discovers that going back to open fires means hunting down extinct chimney sweeps, having custom-made ash grates fabricated in sheet metal factories, paying extortionate amounts for a firescreen, foraging in parks for kindling, and hauling wood home in the boot of the car, all worth it for the right to ask snootily at cocktail parties "What do you burn?"

THE LATEST New Yorker magazine to hit these shores has a funny cartoon. It shows one of these snooty characters at a cocktail party. "What do you use for heat?" says he. "WE use wood." Do you get it? Fossil fuels like oil are out. It's terribly trendy to use wood and natural fuels.

OK, we're right into wood-burning at our place. That's another point. It's trendy to "get into" things these days. You "get into" bridge, you "get into" patchwork quilting or you "get into" making your own yoghurt.

All right, here in Melbourne we like to spread the message that we have a climate which is a slight improvement on the South of France or the Greek islands. Obviously this year something went wrong with the computer. I can't tell you how shocking it has been. You wouldn't do worse in Pudsey or Manchester.

Last year we moved house and we swore that from now on we would spurn all the frightful modern technology. We had a dream, we would go back to lovely open fires and on winter evenings we would sit back in our armchairs and look at faces in the coals.

Don't think I haven't noticed the trend. The glossy home magazines run pictures of ready-made fireplaces with fancy copper flues. They all have names. "Mr Viking" or the rugged "Davey Crockett."

You know those pictures they used to have on the covers of the Saturday Evening Post. An old feller in a grocer's store with his feet up on a mid-Victorian stove, all those twiddledy bits. They're IN too. There's a firm out at Eltham which is making "exact replica" pot-belly stoves and they can't make enough. We got invited to dinner one night, and our hosts had a magnificent wood fire going and they have their own chain saw and they bring home the wood from their Peninsular farm every weekend.

Now to get back to our problem. One thing sold us our present house. It has the Victorian-style iron grates and marble mantelpieces. Even in the height of summer my wife could hardly wait to start burning.

Well I tell you, it wasn't so easy. In one room the fireplace had been turned into a cupboard. That was the trend in the 50s. You whipped out all your fireplaces and if you designed things correctly the old space in the chimney would take six or seven dozen bottles.

Do you know where to find a bricklayer who can remake a fireplace in the classic style so it won't smoke? The last one died about 1940.

Then one must find a chimney sweep. There were maybe a dozen of them in the yellow pages. The first three: "No Sir, we're only interested in industrial chimneys." Three more didn't answer or didn't come. Finally I got a fellow who was so busy I had to make an advance appointment and one got the impression he was about as important as a top psychiatrist.

Our turn came eventually and he arrived looking superb in spotless white overalls. I asked about black face, brushes and things, even little boys. He looked at me with contempt: "We don't do it that way any more." He had special screens and a big vacuum-operated machine which sucked out all the soot without distributing a speck. Then, while he did this, he discussed esoteric matters like antique furniture and modern art.

Now these old fireplaces of ours needed little grates to catch the ash. Every ironmonger sold them back in the 30s. "I'll nick down and get a couple," I said. Ye Gods, I went to nearly every ironmonger in Melbourne. Not a chance. Eventually I had to go round the sheet metal factories with a blueprint and specifications. The fourth factory finally agreed to take it on — at a price.

The next move was to get a firescreen. Have you tried to buy a firescreen lately? "Get one cheaply at the Target or K-Mart," said my wife. "I've heard they're wonderful for things like that."

At the K-Mart the girl said: "I don't know what you mean. What's a firescreen? Are those the things they have in picture theatres?"

Once again we went around the ironmongers and hardware stores. "Aw, I used to stock 'em. But no call any more. You see, when TV came in people gave up open fires. I mean, you sit around TV... everyone went to their Vulcans."

Eventually we got our firescreen at an antique shop from the 50s. I won't tell you what it cost, but the drawbridge for the Tower of London would have been as cheap. By now the mounting cost was considerable.

Now we came to our ultimate problem: what to burn. In the old days kindling was no problem. Remember how all your vegetables came in wooden fruit boxes? You don't get those any more. Fruit comes in cardboard.

So what did I have to do? Make a series of forays to the park for fallen branches and pine cones. And where do you find a wood merchant? Once upon a time he used to deliver it and put it in the wood shed for you.

Now he comes home with it in the boot of the car. And the car boot looks lovely after half a bag of sawdust.

Where do you put the darned stuff? The wood shed was pulled down years ago — to build the swimming pool.

So you can see, it's been no trouble at all. We sit back in our armchairs on the frigid winter evenings, look at faces in the fire, gaze at the burning pine cones, the smouldering Mallees and it's lovely. At all cocktail parties snootily we ask: "What do you burn?"
