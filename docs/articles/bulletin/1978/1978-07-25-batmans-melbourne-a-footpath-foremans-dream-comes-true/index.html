A footpath foreman's dream comes true

6 min read.

First published 25 Jul 1978

A lifetime of building-watching culminates in a two-hour preview tour of the new ANZ Tower in Collins Place — with its 48th-floor boardroom views to Port Phillip Heads, royal-blue carpets, executive bathrooms in white marble, unisex hairdressing salon, sauna, gymnasium, revolving door that stopped a thousand employees moving in for a month, and an underground swimming pool that the nuns never knew how to drain.

MAYBE my fascination with new buildings began back in the 30s. I wandered over every new block of flats that was going up between South Yarra and Caulfield.

We would wait until the workmen went home and then there was the marvellous thrill of climbing the ladder to the first floor then walking across the rafters before the boards went down. There was the joy of writing our initials in the sand that had been dropped by the carpenters and burrowing in the bricklayers' sand.

This nosey-parker attitude only got worse. I have tramped through every new city tower while it was still spawning. The ICI was the first, that came in the 50s. For a brief period it was the tallest building in Australia and, glory be, we called it a skyscraper.

Then came the CRA, Shell, the National Mutual, BHP, Prudential and a whole heap of others. But I am particularly moved this week, because the first tower in the Collins Place project is almost complete, the new illustrious headquarters for the ANZ business.

They aren't exactly naming the day because that's something you don't do with the Collins Place project. After all they have had a few surprises, like a rise in costs from an estimated $87 million and now they will be very lucky if they get through with $250 million. What's more they have had something like 300 industrial disputes.

But as an old building watcher, could I pay tribute. It all began back in 1971 with the wrecking of the Oriental Hotel and other buildings at the corner of Exhibition and Collins Streets. Then the Mainline Corporation dug a vast hole to take foundations of the twin 50-storey towers.

I had observed the digging of the Chevron hole at Kings Cross and the AMP hole at Circular Quay, but this was bigger and undoubtedly the most satisfying hole in Australia. After the hole, Mainline built site offices. Now these were site offices to end all site offices. They cost $140,000 and they were built over the Exhibition and Collins footpaths. They were double-storey, 32 individual offices, sound-proofed, cedar plywood finish on the inside, vinyl tiles on the floor, tea-rooms and lavatories on both floors, sick bay with nurse in attendance, reception rooms with sliding glass panels, the lot.

You all know what happened, there was stoppage after stoppage, and the Mainline Corporation came apart at the financial seams. The morning I remember best was September 18, 1974, when the Mainline staff had a Retrenchment Champagne Breakfast. The Collins Place project must have been dead and silent for four months. Some of us even wondered whether work would ever be done on it again. The scaffolding, bare and harsh, pointing at the sky, looked like an enormous rusty iron sculpture and we thought that given time, weathering and tradition, Collins Place could rival other great ruins like Rome's Forum or England's Stonehenge.

But then E. A. Watts moved in and now four years later the ANZ Tower is ready for opening and the Collins Tower next door is half way up. Last week I went back for a preview and a two-hour walk through the ANZ Tower. Could I say that in a lifetime of building-watching never have I seen such finish, such attention to detail, such downright quality.

Our tour started up on the 48th floor. The word is that the ANZ board room will be up there and the view is beyond belief, from Port Phillip heads around to the Dandenongs, and you can look right down on the ICI — what a curious little box it is right down there.

The Watts men have a special stunt. They ask you to stand right in the corner of the building overlooking Collins and Exhibition, then look straight down. You get a weird, uncomfortable optical illusion, and you'd swear they had put up the building right in the middle of Collins Street.

Next we went to the 16th floor and we stepped out on to a lovely, thick, royal-blue carpet, so good you wondered whether to take off your shoes. This is the Staff Amenities floor. If you didn't dine there, then there was also the executives' dining room. The part which impressed me was the spiral staircase up to the amenities floor with lots more royal-blue carpet. On the left was the billiards room with a four-table billiard setup. On the other side was the TV room, then there was an unisex hairdressing salon, and the gymnasium was splendidly large with a special, padded, non-slip surface underfoot. I believe I did hear mention of squash courts and for a moment I dreamed of those idiotic days back in the 60s when we spurned the idea of a bank career for our sons.

Round the corner was the sauna bath — one for the boys, one for the girls — complete with changing room and showers.

The 15th and the 14th were executive floors. Some walls were lined in aspen veneer, a pale, beautiful wood from England, and the rest was done in a brilliant, shiny, reflecting finish almost like ultra-smooth brown marble.

ANZ have gone in for landscaping with partitions and filing cabinets (exquisitely designed so as not to look like filing cabinets), all 1.4m high.

Down below again is the managing director's office which is right on the corner looking out towards the Melbourne Cricket Ground. I was puzzled by a brown cylindrical affair between his main office and the ante room. So I opened the door and, by golly, it was just the sort of bathroom you have been looking for, white marble floor-to-ceiling, a big, round circular mirror, an all-clear-glass shower recess to your right, and a clear-glassed-in lavatory to your left. Very nice.

The serious banking business with the general public is on the three levels down by the street. You enter by the most famous revolving door in Melbourne. Last month the building unions fighting for a $6-a-week rise refused to instal the revolving door. It was a smart tactical move because this stopped ANZ moving in with their 1000 employees. It stopped work for a month.

Then you look up aloft and lo, all this is lit by natural light. There are a series of clear plastic domes and you almost get the feeling that managing directors, super managers and such, in their suites overhead could look down and observe what is going on.

In the first-floor customer lounge there will be colour television for entertainment and information services. All this will look out on the Great Space.

Now the Great Space is the area between the two towers. It has a curious sort of wire grid over it and you can see what the architects were getting at. It is a thousand rotary clothes hoists welded together and this symbolises suburban Australia.

There will be a plastic shield overhead to keep out wind and rain and this will be a marvellous open area with shops, restaurants, and theatres. There will be trees. According to the architects these were planted elsewhere especially for the Great Space.
