Pedalling the case for cyclists

7 min read.

First published 27 Jun 1978

After ten years as a daily bicycle commuter averaging 160 kilometres a week, Dunstan surveys the remarkable progress from open hostility and near-wipeouts to the Geelong Bikeplan, the first systematic study of cycling in an Australian city, which has become a blueprint for the nation, backed by $1.6 million in state funding.

IS there any future for the bicycle? I often wonder.

It is just on 10 years since I first took up the bicycle as my go-everywhere form of transport. I was 43 at the time. I still average around 160 kilometres a week and somehow I manage to survive. It hasn't been easy. At first there was a fair amount of derision from colleagues, plus open abuse, even hostility from motorists.

One terrifying day a group of youths in a Holden drew up alongside and threw open their doors in an attempt at a total wipe-out. "Get off the road," they counselled, "ya mug."

There has been only one accident. Time: 5.45 pm. Place: Flinders Lane. Weather conditions: Dusk and driving rain. A driver came out of a side-alley without looking. I made violent attempts to avoid the car but my front wheel just caught the front bumper and I went flying into the gutter. No damage done, except a considerable bruising of dignity.

All these experiences turned me into a bicycle political activist and along with my friends I spent much time knocking on important doors. The chairman of the Victorian Board of Works smiled but was totally uninterested. The chiefs of the Country Roads Board seemed interested but did nothing. The Minister for Transport (not the present one) promised magnificently and forgot all about it. The man we liked best was a city councillor. He poked a finger on my chest and said: "What you've got to realise boy is that the bicycle in the 20th century is JUST NOT ON."

As officialdom did nothing, it was important to learn basic methods for survival. 1. Make yourself look as vulgar as possible, that is, wear brilliant visible clothing. 2. Never do anything that is unpredictable to the motorist. In other words, never deviate without a signal. 3. Oddly enough you are safe in heavy traffic, but avoid high speed arterial roads. 4. If you must travel at night, wear reflective clothing, light up like a chandelier with at least two rear lights. If you must bicycle at night for God's sake don't do it when it is raining. Visibility is lousy through a wet windscreen even in daylight. 5. Wear a crash helmet. At least 70 percent of cyclists who get killed die from brain injury. The best helmet on the market is the Bell, made in America, designed for cyclists and costs $46 to $50. It makes you look like a galah but it is better to be a live galah.

Does all this make it appear that I am terrified? I am, but I still think my children are safer on bicycles, well handled, than in cars and infinitely safer than on motor bikes.

What has happened in the past 10 years? The sale of bicycles has gone up six times and at last there has been a change in official attitude. Most government departments and most local councils now realise that the cyclist is entitled to be on the road and they must recognise him in their planning.

Victoria has been lucky. We have had the young bike-loving Minister for Youth, Sport and Recreation, Brian Dixon, the man who launched the Life, Be In It campaign. The best thing that has happened under his reign is the Geelong Bikeplan which could become a blueprint for towns and cities all over the nation.

In 1976, the Victorian cabinet decided to take a provincial city and use it as a test case for the bicycle and try out all the experiments. They chose Geelong because it was the biggest, 120,000, and the place most like Melbourne.

It began in January, 1977, under Don Hurnall, director of Engineering at the Geelong Regional Commission. They had some good stuff to work on from the accident statistics. Most bike accidents involve children. Children under 17 account for 75 percent of all accidents, and usually it is their fault. Sixty percent of all accidents occur at intersections and 42 percent involve right-angled collisions. A cyclist is 20 times more likely to die on a wet night than on a dry day. Have you noticed how many bike riders, incredibly, ride at night without lights? Forty percent of cyclists hit at night did not have lights.

The Bikeplan people started by making a massive survey — they charted where all Geelong's 20,000 cyclists used their bikes and when. The first thing they did was to establish a 40 kmh speed limit in all Geelong's residential streets, thus cutting down the speed of cars through intersections. They built a bicycle path three kilometres long to feed the schools in North Geelong, but they made some interesting discoveries. Bike paths, unless along a river, a water front, or feeding children through a park to school, are not the answer.

The survey proved that bike riders use all the roads in Geelong. They use them to go to church, to shop, to visit friends. If the State wanted to cater for their needs it would have to build hundreds of kilometres of paths, and the cost would be beyond belief. Even then they would not suit. The survey showed that if a cyclist had to go 10 percent further by bike path he wouldn't use it.

There are alternatives, bikeways or bike lanes. These still mean mixed traffic but they are preferred routes for cyclists. A bike lane means marking off part of the road for cyclists, but riders still have to go through intersections and, according to the survey, the American experience has been that often they are more dangerous than no lane at all.

Unless you are building a new suburb, or a new town, it is not possible to create a network of roads for cyclists. What is the answer? For a start you widen that left lane along which most cyclists travel. Usually the edge of the left lane is a misery of broken glass, manhole covers, setoles and debris, the sort of thing that makes the rider swing out into traffic. You impose greater control at the intersections where accidents happen and make room for cyclists. They are looking at new types of push-button lights, traffic islands and eliminating squeeze points.

In February this year the Victorian cabinet approved an allocation of $1.6 million to implement the Geelong Bikeplan over the next five years with an initial slice of $300,000. This was the breakthrough. The bicycle was recognised.

A seminar in the Geelong Town Hall was attended by planners from all over Australia, from as far as Perth. Newcastle invited senior officers of the Bikeplan — Don Hurnall, Michael Scott and Alan Parker — to go there to discuss implications for Newcastle.

The most interesting thing done so far on this is a comic called Sprocket Man, backed to the tune of $2000 by Brian Dixon's department and brought out by the Bicycle Institute of Victoria. Sprocket Man is a cross between the Phantom and Superman, who oddly enough rides a bicycle. Melbourne cartoonist Jeff Hook civilised him for the Australian public. The plan now is to get the book into the hands of every schoolchild. The BIV is charging only handling costs at the rate of $4 a hundred.

Cyclists feel that time is on their side and that as the costs of the monster motor car rise more, people will switch to the muscle motor. The biggest thing that holds them back is fear.

If you, too, are undecided on whether or not to try two wheels, could I list the advantages and disadvantages.

ADVANTAGES: Faster door-to-door in traffic situations. Healthy: It cures aches and pains. I had persistent rheumatic hip pains, not any more. Weight reducing: In two years I lost 1½ stone and can drink beer again with impunity. Parking: Park anywhere — even outside the town hall. Freedom: It will go anywhere except on water. Cheap: Sell your car, ride a bike and save at least $3000 a year.

DISADVANTAGES: Terror: Frequently motorists frighten you. Personal pollution: Summertime — people sit a little further away. Weather: You get wet and you become a benefactor of the dry cleaning industry. Social consequences: Friends tell you daily you are out of your mind. At times you suffer a sense of inferiority.
