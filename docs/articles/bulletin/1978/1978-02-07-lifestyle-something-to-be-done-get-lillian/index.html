Something to be done? Get Lillian

7 min read.

First published 07 Feb 1978

A portrait of Lillian Frank, Melbourne's flamboyant queen of hairdressing, whose 15-year reign of spectacular fashion at the Cup, seven front pages at Hair's opening night, and newly acquired role promoting Rocky Mattioli's world title fight proves she is as far from shy as Eva Gabor.

MELBOURNE columnist Ian Mackay has put the question: "What is a nice girl like Lillian Frank doing promoting Rocky Mattioli's world light-middleweight title fight defence on March 11?

I'm glad he asked. On the dull Melburnian personality scene Lillian stands out like the Eiffel Tower on the Nullarbor Plain.

In Melbourne if you want to raise a million dollars for a charity — you ask Lillian. If you want someone to put zap into a talk-back radio program — you ask Lillian. If you want to score a way-out picture on the front page of the Sun, the Age and the Australian all on the one morning — no one can do it but Lillian.

Lillian is the queen of Lillian's hairdressing salon in Trak Arcade, Toorak Village. They call her the grand dame, the prima donna of hairdressing. If we have a society register around here there's hardly a greying scalp (a pox on the new Austpost numerals) male or female that hasn't been under her fingers.

Lillian is warm-hearted, as fast on her feet as Mattioli himself and about as shy as Eva Gabor. She adores publicity, not really for business reasons, she loves it for itself. She's a splendid exhibitionist who is utterly hooked.

Columnists, fashion editors, photographers, on dreary opening nights, on Derby Day, the Oaks, anywhere, have breathed a hundred times: "Thank God for Lillian."

Take the opening night of Hair. It was pretty awful, but Lillian arrived in a sensational zebra outfit. Sensational? Wait a minute, she had eye lashes seven-and-a-half inches long, top and bottom. They were pasted on to her forehead and cheeks. On top of her own lustrous hair she had two wigs, spreading out all around — more hair than the world has ever seen.

"Darlingggg," she said in triumph, "I made seven front pages."

For more than... no we won't go into that... let's say that for a very long time I have observed her splendid antics at the Melbourne Cup. One time that leather-clawed columnist Claudia wrote: "When Lillian sweeps through the members' gates, the interstate Press is relieved. If no other fizzy ladies in frightening fashion turn up — at least we can rely on Lillian, they sigh."

"I have been reigning queen of Cup week for 15 years, darling, and I've never been to the races without being photographed — never," she says with the satisfaction of a cannibal counting heads. "And I have never been upstaged — never."

The interesting thing is that unlike the aforementioned Eva Gabor, she's an old-fashioned girl. She never wears anything topless, see-through or anything like that. One time she went all in beige, a new special color and she had a lace top. "Ach," she said, "I was verree naive. The Herald photographer said, 'You look very nice, I want a different angle. You stay there and I'll get up on the grandstand steps.' In the bar they were saying 'Ha, look at that woman' and my husband Richard came running out furious. But the photographer got the picture with all my cleavage."

What were my favorite years? I think 1969. Lillian has always been the terrible prelude to female fashions. In '69 we had yet to see leather in its tannery proportions. Lillian turned out all in leather with this vast ankle-length coat trimmed in blue fox.

"It was eighty degrees, and darling I nearly died," she said. "I lost three pounds, but it was worth it. I was in all the papers — you even had a sketch in your column. But I was ready for the heat. I had another outfit to change into after the first race."

She was also the first into boots: "Boots hadn't arrived in Melbourne then. I had to go to a little bootmaker and tell him what I wanted. He had to make a special last just for one pair. Ohhh, these boots came right up to the knee. Marvellous, centre spreads everything. A year later a fashion editor came to me and said, 'Lillian, last year we all giggled at you, now everybody is wearing them.'"

"Another time I wanted the plastic look, the newest thing, an outfit inspired by the French designer, Paco Rabanne. It was composed entirely out of black and white plastic discs. I had to go to a toy factory to get those discs, then I had to go to a hardware business to get the chains to link them all together." Seven front pages again. But the dress didn't go to waste. Afterwards she turned it into a shower curtain.

Oh, she's a very resourceful lady. There was the famous rose outfit — was it for the Oaks? Anyway she had a pink coat, a pink lapel covered in pink roses, a hat full of pink roses and roses all over her umbrella. She said people often asked her what she did with all those roses. "I took them off the coat, left it with a plain lapel and wore it for years. I used the roses to make a cover for the toilet seat."

Is there any back-biting? Any bitchery? No. She has so many friends, she really loves everybody and if anyone throws a knife, what's the point in worrying about it? Forget it. But there was the year of the white mink coat. "This was my first fur coat," she said. "I nagged Richard until I got it, then I decided to go the whole hog — a full length white mink coat. There hadn't been one in Melbourne before. All the female claws came out. I wore it to the Cup and overheard some women walking behind me, 'Of course that's not the best mink. Those are female skins, male are better.' Did you ever hear the like?"

Lillian was born in Rangoon, Burma. She is not going to concede the year. Her parents were Jewish. Her father was born in Turkey and her mother, an Iraki, came from Baghdad. Her mother belonged to the Solomon family, very wealthy coffee dealers in Burma. There were six children — four girls and two boys.

"Father was a school teacher," she said, "a very clever man. He spoke 11 languages. He met my mother when he was giving her English lessons. According to family legend it was all arranged for her to marry someone else, so they eloped. The brothers were furious. They beat Dad up when he returned. Eventually though he went into the family coffee business. We just got out of Burma in time. My parents somehow didn't really take the Japanese invasion seriously. Then when Dad heard of girls being raped, we fled a few hours before they arrived." They fled to Assam, then to Calcutta and Lillian went to school for three years in Bombay.

She came to Australia in 1953, worked for Elizabeth Arden and later went into partnership with Antonio Zappalla in the city under the name of Lillian and Antonio. Nine years ago Lillian and Antonio went their separate ways and she opened Lillian's in Toorak. Her husband, Richard Frank, was born in Warsaw, and had a dramatic escape from the Russians. He started in Melbourne as a waiter, bought his own coffee shop and has since owned a number of first-class restaurants. His latest is Petty Sessions at 459 Collins Street.

But to get back to this fight. How did Lillian get into the act? "I know Rocky Mattioli. He took out one of my girls in the hairdressing salon, a very nice boy. And I like boxing. Darling, I don't like it when they hurt each other. But I think it is very exciting. I used to adore TV Ringside on television.

"Four or five of my friends have money in this fight. I am good at promotions, ideas. So here I am.

"It's going to be marvellous. We'll take all the sweaty thing out of boxing. It's a world title and we must do it properly. There will be a civic reception. There'll be champagne and caviar at the ringside. I'm trying to talk them into having the first two rows for charity. All the wives must come along.

"There'll be a meet-the-champion reception... champainnnne... we will set up a ring in the City Square and in the Mall in Bourke Street... Rocky will give exhibition spars for Moomba. And the night at Kooyong, I promise, will be a brilliant affair."

I wonder what Lillian will wear.
