Hair today, gone to sorrow

5 min read.

First published 04 Jul 1978

The Georges Italy 1978 fashion show — with its wet-string hair, Afros, crimped 'electric chair' styles and $45 treatments — confirms Dunstan's fear that hair has not retreated since his brief optimism two years ago, and is now bigger in the economy than drink, tobacco, or any other vice.

TWO YEARS AGO I thought we were beginning to see the light at the end of the tunnel of hair. The whole frightful hair thing was coming to an end. My son went to the barber for the first time in 10 years and actually had it cut.

I regret to have to report that the glimmer in the tunnel was merely a glow-worm. The situation has actually become worse.

Last week Georges opened its Italy 1978 show. You must appreciate that Italy is In at the moment. Judging from the awful bills that are coming in to our place it is where the fashion all happens.

All right, Georges served Italian champagne, Italian food. There was zappy way-out music and they had a series of marvellous, under-nourished, sparkling girls in the new Italian fashions. There were no bras, little titties jiggling under the floodlights, nipples proudly pointing, proud red-lipped generous smiles. I loved every one of them and the fashions. Except the hair.

There was one girl, they told me, who was famous from Sydney to Rome to Paris. Her blonde hair hung in strings and it looked exactly as if she had stepped out of the bath. There was another whose hair hung completely lank. It looked as if she had been trying to make it the color of chocolate mousse but the milk curdled and it hung round her head like a floor mop.

There were three or four awe-inspiring Afros, the hair in a huge tangled ball all over the head. Back in the early 50s I did time in New York. In those days the hairdressers made a fortune straightening the hair of the black girls so that they would look like their white sisters. I suppose there is some sort of curious justice that in the 70s the hairdressers are making a fortune from the white girls trying to look like their black sisters.

The rest of the girls were wearing their hair in a further startling style. It looked as if they had been through some terrible experience, like being strapped for two minutes in an electric chair. The hair was covered with absurd busy little ripples.

One of our daughters came home with the crimp several weeks ago. At first there was the profound shock, looking for recognition. That's the problem these days. You can't recognise your old mates because they have covered their faces with growth and you can't recognise the girls because they look so strange with their new hair-dos.

So there was our beloved daughter looking as if she had been in the electric chair.

"Uh... ah... how much did it cost," I said.

"Only $45," she replied.

"FORTY-FIVE DOLLARS!"

"I suppose it would cost that much again to have it straightened," said my wife.

Of course, it was an argument we did not win and our antiquarian 1940-ish views were proved by the Georges Italian show.

But what disturbs me is the speed with which hair is taking over the economy. You must have noticed that since the tobacco ads were banned on television it is the hair that now has taken over. On some nights I would swear that 40 percent of the television commercials are associated with hair — all the various unguents, conditioners, shampoos made from mysterious herbs gathered on the slopes of the Himalayas, shampoos made from yoghurt refined from the milk of alpine goats.

And the cost of all these unguents in their exotic bottles is frightful, even worse than restaurant prices for home Coonawarra reds. I have pleaded with the girls in our family. I have pointed out that just possibly Melbourne has the finest urban water supply in the world. The water is soft and pure and almost certainly they can get the same result if they wash their hair with old fashioned laundry bar soap. But you're not in the hunt. A new brand of unguent comes into the house every week.

I can put up with the hair-washing routine. It is a ritual that can take up to 30 minutes in the bathroom. And when you finally get in there the resulting pea-souper is worse than any London fog. What finally makes me crack is stumbling into the shower recess and putting my feet on all those weirdly shaped shampoo bottles.

If you think this is a sexist article I do submit that almost all the aforesaid remarks apply to males also. The hair on top of the head and the hair on the face shows little sign of abating. My main prejudice is against the half beards, the Svengali trims, and oh God, those awful trendy Henry Lawson moustaches.

I tell you, hair is becoming bigger in the economy than drink, bigger even than pot.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Hair today, gone to sorrow, The Bulletin, 4 July 1978. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
