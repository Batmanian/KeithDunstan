The artful metamorphosis of a bush town

5 min read.

First published 21 Nov 1978

Swan Hill's copybook transformation from a straight-road country town into a year-round tourist mecca — built on the rescued paddlesteamer Gem, a pioneer museum that spawned a continent of imitators, and the new $1.25 million Campbell Street Shopping Park — is crowned by Peter Beattie's Riverboat restaurant, which serves witchetty grub soup canned at Monbulk and ships 60 percent of its production to restaurants in the United States and Japan.

THE TOURIST battle in Victoria has always been one of unnerving competition. What can you do for a town which is literally just a straight road through the inevitable shops, banks, supermarket, laundromat, mass-produced war memorial plus memorial park.

The battle centres around enticing your tourist to stay the night and spend money in the local motels. So you have to create tourist traps.

I have always had a soft spot for Swan Hill. Population 8000, it sits there on the Murray in an eternal glare of sunlight, 340 kilometres north of Melbourne. Until 1963 God knows why anyone would ever have wanted to go there and most didn't.

Now it has 10 motels, and is still building more, plus a year-round thumping tourist trade. It has been the copybook example of crafty tourist trapping.

Back in late 1962 a raiding party went from Swan Hill to Mildura and under the sleeping Sunraysia noses they bought the Gem, known back in the 1880s as "The Queen of the Murray." Then they towed her 523km upstream to Swan Hill. The Gem was a real prize, the biggest thing on the Murray. She was 285 tons, 24 cabins, three decks, a proper dining-room, plus a music-room forward for the orchestra, and a whopping 134 feet long. She was a lovely old thing; she reminded me of a North Queensland pub. All peeling timber, no vertical was quite vertical and no horizontal was quite horizontal. She was all wavy lines and she inspired Kenneth Jack to do some quite beautiful paintings.

Well, the Swan Hill enthusiasts restored the Gem and put her in a permanent concrete pond. No Tourist Trap.

For the next three years they built a pioneer museum. They did it on a grand scale and they did it in good taste. This was the first of the folk museums and they did it so well it has been copied now right across the continent. Indeed Australia has so many folk museums I understand the shortage of mint-condition, drop-log pioneer cabins, fair dinkum shearing sheds with thatched roofs, single-furrow ploughs, traction engines, antique winnowers and 1880 phaetons, buggies, and particularly plumed-horse-drawn hearses is quite frightening. That was Tourist Trap No 2.

Your correspondent has just been to Swan Hill for their Sunshine Fiesta and the opening of Tourist Trap No 3, which in effect is the main street, formally opened by the Premier, Dick Hamer.

The main street is now called "The Campbell Street Shopping Park," and it has cost nigh on $1.25 million. The idea came from Grand Junction, Colorado, and dull old Campbell Street has been utterly transformed. It is not a mall — due obeisance is still made to the god automobile with adequate parking, but it is a brilliant compromise.

The main street now with great subtlety snakes its way through the town. To relieve the eye there are lots of wrought iron railings, pretty wrought iron bicycle racks, tubs with trees, flower beds and even flower beds with petunias right down the middle of the street. The footpaths and pedestrian walks have been done in pale red brick and there are all sorts of marvellous little alcoves where you can sit in the shade and talk.

As part of the Fun Fiesta they had a great public breakfast in which most of Swan Hill came to Campbell Street at 6.30 am for sausages and eggs and bacon. Then they established a national cartoon contest for $1000 which they called the Etty Award in honor of Swan Hill's devotion to the witchetty grub. The grubstakes were won by WEG, W. E. Green of the Melbourne Herald.

Actually being a grub fancier I made a nostalgic visit back to the Gem. It looked a little forlorn because the old boat was sitting on blocks in a dry hole. Allegedly repairs are being made to the hull, but shipwrights are hard to find in Swan Hill.

However, Peter Beattie was there in command of his Riverboat restaurant, which he claims is the only restaurant in the country which serves dinkum indigenous food. He serves platters of yabbies, Murray Crays, witchetty grub soup and, at times, even snake and kangaroo.

The environment people have made things a little difficult for him. Snake is off the menu these times, because the snake is protected in New South Wales, although he does claim that a carpet snake or even a fat brown does make very succulent eating.

However his piece de resistance is the Squatters Special which consists of eye fillet of beef done carpet bag style with yabbies and sitting on a bed of damper.

Mr Beattie is famous for being the first man to can a witchetty grub. It is a complex business hooking a grub out of a tree. One uses a piece of wire and it has to be eased very gently out of the bark, so I must confess I was a little sceptical about Peter's soup and I wondered how it was done.

He turned on me. "I have been getting my grubs from a saw mill at little Barham for 25 cents each. Actually on the Willesee show last July I did complain grubs were hard to get. Then I received a call from a fellow at Clare. Now he's supplying me all I need." From then on, he explained, the witchetty grubs had to be handled with expedition, because if left too long they turned into white moths. He cooked up 500 gallons of soup at a time and the canning was done by STC Ltd at Monbulk.

And trade is booming. He said: "Over 60 percent of my soup goes overseas to the US, Japan, Hong Kong, places like that. There's a restaurant bloke at the Bronx Zoo, who is desperate for it. He rings me up on the telephone, long distance, and puts in orders. You wouldn't believe it."
