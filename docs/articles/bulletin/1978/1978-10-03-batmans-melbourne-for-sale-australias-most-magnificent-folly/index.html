For sale: Australia's most magnificent folly

8 min read.

First published 03 Oct 1978

Idylwilde, the Balwyn mansion built by chain-store magnate Oliver Gilpin in the 1930s — 230,000 pounds of Japanese gardens, canals, seven stone bridges, 35 aviaries with running water, a deer sanctuary, a hidden indoor swimming pool, and a bathroom the nuns never entered — goes to auction after the Missionary Sisters of the Sacred Heart can no longer afford the $15,000 annual rates.

FOR MORE than 20 years now I have been writing about the follies of the rich in Melbourne; follies that have ranged from the land boomery of the 1880s through to the Hoyts Super Deluxe of the 1940s. But I am upset to have to confess that until last week I had never heard of Idylwilde.

Idylwilde, on the highest peak in Melbourne, is pure Citizen Kane, or if it isn't Citizen Kane it is at least Mary Pickford and Douglas Fairbanks. Ripponlea used to think it was pretty good, but I tell you, Idylwilde makes Ripponlea look like a weekend shack.

Citizen Kane in this instance was Oliver Gilpin. Back in 1895, when he started in Korumburra, these spread until he had 94 of them in four States. He dealt in everything from crockery to drapery to household utensils and his passionate belief was high turnover at low prices. He had a fleet of trucks with outsize trailers and these were always on the move supplying his 94 stores.

His other great belief was that people in the country should get their goods at the same price as those in the city. He believed, too, in the organising skills of women. He employed them in great numbers and had them on his board.

In the 1930s he bought over 20 acres of land and started building Idylwilde in the heart of Balwyn. It was a very fashionable area. Barry Humphries has called it the new Toorak of the 30s. Others unkindly have called it the Sweet Sherry Belt, but now they would be into Coolibah casks at the very least.

How much Gilpin spent nobody knows. The official figure was 230,000 pounds, but now it looks like more than $10 million worth. It is known as Mary's Mount Convent. He had up to 140 men on the job, and according to Kathleen Dunlop Kane, who wrote the history of the Sisters of the Sacred Heart 1928-1978, he started by putting a high solid wood wall right around to keep out the curious. So the construction went on for four years.

The irony of it is that Gilpin never lived there. Legend has it that he didn't even put in one night. It was still being built when Gilpin, an old-fashioned pound millionaire, died in 1942 aged 68. So his empire dispersed and his stores went to G. J. Coles.

Idylwilde in those tough 1942 war years went up for auction and the highest bid was a pitiful 38,000 pounds. Eventually it went to a Catholic Order, the Missionary Sisters of the Most Sacred Heart of Jesus, and they used it as a retreat. They paid 45,000 pounds.

There is another irony here. According to Kathleen Dunlop Kane, Gilpin had an anti-Catholic bias and he would never employ one. The Sisters made few changes to Idylwilde, but you can imagine what happened. Now 36 years later the costs are beyond belief. The rates alone are $15,000 a year. So the Sisters are selling.

It will be sold in two lots. Lot 1 is all land. Lot 2, the three-storey mansion of 140 squares, a dormitory wing and approximately five acres of land and garden goes up for auction on October 12.

Biggin & Scott are the agents. Kevin Biggin tells me that they opened it up for the public for a few hours on Sunday, September 10. "We printed 3000 brochures," he said, "and we thought maybe a thousand people would be curious enough to have a look. All the time I have been in business I have never seen anything like it. By the time we locked up on Sunday evening 15,000 had been through."

Last week I was the 15,001st to go through. You start by driving through an appropriately huge pair of iron gates. It's like entering Windsor Great Park — the house is not even in sight. On left and right are green paddocks bigger than football fields. Each of these has strange concrete domes like mini pagodas. They were shelters for Mr Gilpin's deer, and are equipped with hot and cold running water. Yes, and put in at a time, says Kathleen Kane, when few of his employees would have had such an amenity in their own homes.

There are cypresses, palms, a vast circular rose garden and there is a two acre orchard, half-done with wire overhead to keep off the birds.

If you go down steps to the right you come to the lake. I think this startled me more than anything. There is a network of canals done to perfection in stone with seven Japanese-style bridges also in stone. There is a waterfall that tumbles down through ferns and rain forest. There are bores, there is a separate pump house that pumps water through sprays to keep everything moist.

It's just like Venice, with boat house and stone landing-stages here and there for the boats. There is an area for a zoo; kangaroos and emus were planned. Then there is the fish hatchery, also in stone. Kevin Biggin says Gilpin used his trucks to bring down large quantities of trout from the Murray.

The stone-work was all painted and in some areas it has the atmosphere of the Scenic Caves at Luna Park. Workmen must have blessed Gilpin during the depression. He would have been providing more work than the Susso relief.

Forgive me if I don't sound enthusiastic over the house. It is a mixture of art nouveau, art deco and Spanish Mission, containing so much concrete it would require a nuclear explosion to shift it. On the left is an enclosed area, Gothic, complete with windows which must cover more than half an acre. In here Gilpin had 35 — repeat 35 — aviaries and each of them was superbly equipped with feeding devices, slatted roof and its own running water. There were also glasshouses and a proper conservatory.

When you go inside it is suitably vast. I lost track of the number of reception rooms, bathrooms and such. Mr Biggin did too. He "thought" there were five bathrooms upstairs. These were vintage Hollywood-dream bathrooms of the 30s — sunken baths with lashings of mosaic — some mosaics had pictures of galleons in full sail, some flamingos, some rock pools.

There were more bathrooms downstairs but let me tell you about the swimming pool. You go into a room which looks like a ballroom. The Sisters would have used it for meetings and prayers, and there is a picture of the late Pope up one end.

Kevin Biggin said: "I'll show you." He pulled a handle, lifted a section of the floor and indicated a ladder down. Good Grief! There was a swimming pool down there and not a little one. It was 15 feet at the deep end and it was lined with green tiles.

"And see that ceiling up there," said Biggin, "that's a false ceiling. Up above it is all glass. It was freezing in winter and like a hothouse in summer. The Sisters couldn't stand it. See those handles on the walls, you could wind back the glass panels and get open air."

Next to the swimming pool there were tiled changing rooms complete with showers and the inevitable sunken bath. "Oh you haven't seen half yet," said Biggin. We went downstairs and there was a series of passages, ironing rooms, laundry rooms, a big steel safe. The Sisters used it for storing the Ajax and the Velvet soap.

There was a pantry bigger than our living room, two boilers for the central heating, big enough to drive the Queen Mary, and a 1500 gallon oil tank. "That's one of the reasons they're leaving," he said. "How do you run this stuff?"

Then in yet another room there was a billiard table bigger even than the other two. "Look at that," Biggin said, "It has never been used." It is very likely there for ever because the people who buy the house could never get it out, even if they wanted to.

The pool had never been used either, but there is a high water mark on the tiles. It must at least have been filled.

Upstairs there is a ballroom on the top floor. There are immense solid balconies where Mr Gilpin, had he lived, could have looked away and forever into the distance across his million or more customers.

He believed in using Australian materials and the walls are beautifully lined with Australian maple and walnut. The ceilings are decorated with themes from the room's purpose: the billiard room has clever designs of packs of cards, the music room has lutes, drums, horns and such. The dining room has bunches of grapes.

The billiard room has been converted into a chapel, but Kevin Biggin assures me that in the centre under the carpet there is a concrete block designed to take a full size table.

Kevin Biggin, in his prospectus, says with nice understatement: "I urge you to consider the potential of this magnificent property. It is difficult to contemplate the possibility of such a huge area of land being offered again almost on the city fringe — at any time in the future." Good luck.
