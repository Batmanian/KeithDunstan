The great Toorak paper war

4 min read.

First published 14 Nov 1978

The discovery that Prahran is Melbourne's top garbage suburb at 6.4 kg per resident per week — against Croydon's virtuous 1.97 kg — leads Dunstan to form the Anti Paper Club, dedicated to leaving shops without wrapping, though progress is slow when shirt boxes require 17 pins and hardware stores regard unwrapped goods as evidence of shoplifting.

AT OUR place we have just discovered the devastating news that we live in Melbourne's top rubbish suburb. Very likely it is the top garbage suburb in Australia.

There were newspaper posters outside every newsagent last week which read TOORAK TOP FOR GARBAGE. We were irritated by this.

The Environment Protection Authority had conducted a survey of seven Melbourne municipalities and Prahran had come out on top.

Well, Toorak is a strange little area. Half of it is in Prahran and half of it is in Malvern. But seeing a fair sprinkling of our Prime Ministers, knights, Rolls-Royces and Mercedes Benzes have lived there, newspapers like to give it a burst of publicity.

But first let me give you the run down. On an average each Prahran resident produced 6.4 kg of garbage a week during 1977-78. Coburg rated 3.62 kg; Springvale, 3.45 kg; Williamstown, 2.54 kg; Keilor, 2.64 kg; Whittlesea, 2.25 kg and Croydon, a miserable 1.97 kg.

Household rubbish, we discovered, had increased three percent in the past three years and the greatest increase was food waste. Here is the percentage run-down on a Prahran garbage can: food waste 35.9; paper products 26.5; glass 16.5; garden waste 7.3; steel 6.9; plastics 3.5; inert wastes 1.7; rags 1.2 and aluminium 0.5.

We don't live in Toorak, we live in South Yarra which is wholly contained in Prahran and virtually its turbulent heart. What's more we live in a highly productive garbage street. We possess only two rubbish tins but some proud families have three and even four rubbish tins.

Although Croydon, Whittlesea and Keilor obviously are shocked at our wanton waste I submit that it is not entirely our fault.

There is a serious danger that soon we will be enveloped, drowned, submerged in all that paper that is thrust at us. In our letter box every week we get three give-away suburban newspapers. On top of that we get vast publications from Myers, Waltons, two local supermarkets and an Art Union in Queensland which wants to give us a dream home on the Gold Coast plus a boat. There's a booze war going on in Chapel Street and they're fighting it out with cut rates for whisky, gin and everything from Ben Ean Moselle to Chateau Margaux. We get brochures on all those.

I tell you there is a superb science fiction story to be told about the society that is slowly smothered in waste paper.

In my own small way I am trying to fight back. Early in September I formed the APC. This is the Anti Paper Club. The idea is that members go into stores and refuse to have goods wrapped. This sounds simple enough but the truth is it calls for much courage.

There is a general feeling that there is something shameful, immoral, about carrying a raw unwrapped article down the street.

Salesmen even object to you taking unwrapped things out of their shops. I went into a hardware store and I bought some globes which were encased in cardboard and some screws which were all carefully emplanted in a plastic box. Very firmly I said to the salesman I didn't want them wrapped.

"Oh they have to be wrapped, sir, it's a house rule."

I tried to explain that they were very securely and expensively wrapped already. Had they been contraceptives or pornographic magazines maybe I would have wanted them wrapped, not otherwise.

Finally, after much argument, he admitted all. If I had my goods wrapped then this was indication that I hadn't indulged in shoplifting.

At the Prahran Market they wrap everything. Newspaper even goes around pineapples and bananas. God already has provided pineapples and bananas with excellent coverings, so all that paper goes home and has to be added to our 6.4 kg of rubbish.

One of the big shirt firms announced several years back that they proposed to sell their shirts, just as shirts, none of the awful packing and pins. However I must confess APC is still not making great progress with this one. The fancy little shops in 6.4 kg South Yarra all sell their shirts in vast plastic boxes and recently I set a new record. I had to extract 17 pins before I could put the darned thing on.

I am submitting all this as evidence that we Prahran-South Yarra-Toorak people are innocent. It's the retailers who are forcing the 6.4 kg upon us. The only possible saviour is a massive protest movement through APC. Carry a bare pineapple today.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The great Toorak paper war, The Bulletin, 14 November 1978. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
