How to keep Norm in his place

5 min read.

First published 24 Jan 1978

A visit to a friend's new video games set for a "sports evening" reveals the humiliating truth that the future will demand joysticks, speed-up switches, and total defeat at electronic tennis, squash, and shooting games.

YOU APPRECIATE, of course, that our society can only operate efficiently so long as it is primed by a sufficient number of useless gadgets. It is vital that you and I keep buying.

My friend Hilton bought his wife, Olivia, a video games set for Christmas. He saw it advertised in a magazine, sent for it, and it cost him $59.95. It plugs into the junction box at the back of the TV set. I went over to Hilton's for a sports evening last Wednesday night. He had his little machine, with all its little dials, knobs and switches, set up there six feet in front of the screen.

Hilton opened a couple of bottles of beer so that we could get in the right frame of mind before we did anything too strenuous. "It's terrific," he said. "Just by pushing buttons I can offer you a choice of six different sports — tennis, soccer, handball, squash, hockey or shooting."

Immediately I was very excited by this. You will remember that back in 1976 Victoria's Minister for Youth, Sport and Recreation, Brian Dixon, first launched the Life, Be In It campaign. There was a series of TV ads and the symbol was an ocker slob called Norm.

Norm was the slob who didn't use his muscles and sat drinking cans in front of television. Now I see that Norm is to be retired from Victorian TV, at least during 1978. Apparently too many people decided they would love to be like Norm and stayed in front of their boob tubes.

So now we are on to a slightly different tack. We are having the Craft Festival, which is to last the whole darned year, and we had the grand opening last week. And quite right too, but Hilton's invitation to a sports evening was irresistible.

We each had a knob to control our racquet up and down. Hilton was serving. I moved my bat to the right spot and sent the ball back across the screen — I beg your pardon — court. As the racquet came into contact there was an appropriate tennis ball "ping."

Hilton sent it back again, and this time... dammit... I missed. There was a different sort of noise and the scores were registered in pink letters on top of the screen. 1-0. Soon it was 2-0, 3-0 and until finally 15-2 and the game was over. All games registered up to 15 and Hilton went through me in straight sets.

Yet I was a quick learner and soon I could almost match him. Then he threw another switch. This doubled the speed of the ball. Then he threw another — this halved the size of the racquets. Now he was serving with the pace of Jimmy Connors and not for the life of me could I get my bat into position.

I can't tell you how fast I had to twiddle that knob. My Norm brow was beginning to perspire. Hilton won 15-1.

"The second generation of these sets," said the triumphant Hilton, "will be two dimension. Instead of having just a knob you will have a joy stick."

"What does that do?"

"Gives you complete control of Roscoe Tanner."

"My goodness," I replied. "That will be too exhausting. I will have to go into serious training."

The shooting game was interesting. You have a really little pistol, and electronic balls, like clay pigeons, shoot across the screen. You line up your gun, shoot, and if you hit your target there is the appropriate clunk. That's downright difficult. It was particularly so when Hilton flicked the switch and speeded up the targets. Although I must admit there is a lot of satisfaction to be had in feeling that you are shooting a television set.

After an excellent workout I went home for a shower. Next morning I telephoned a firm called Unitrex and asked what was next in the offing with video games. They said a brilliant future was ahead. By June you will be able to plug in cassettes. Each cassette will have as many as 27 different games.

Unitrex said you will be able to carry out battles like a battle out of Star Wars. There will be naval battles, air battles complete with anti-aircraft missiles, the lot. There will be street racing, the Indianapolis 500 complete with screeching tyres, sliding corners, etc.

There will be tank battles, World War I aerial dogfights with Fokkers, Sopwith Camels and such, contests in mathematics — "You too can be another Einstein."

Then there is a stunt game. The blurb says: "Jumping eight buses was a piece of pie, but thirty-two? Put your hands on the motorcycle grips, rev up the engine and give it all you've got... all the concentrated skill and nerves you can muster. What if you don't make it? Then it will be with all the noise of a real crash."

How about that? Bewdy Norm.
