Press-button pastimes

5 min read.

First published 28 Feb 1978

Unitrex Australia managing director Dan Presser returns from the Consumer Electronics Winter Show at Las Vegas to predict a future of family computers, projection TV, and supermarket shopping via digital telephone — while a New York Times man marvels that every living room will soon look like the control room at Newscenter Four.

DAN PRESSER, managing director of Unitrex Australia, is the awe-inspiring creature of the 1980s.

His Saab motor car is fitted with CB radio, radar check, stereo, air conditioning. On his wrist there is a digital watch which will tell him the day, the date, the month and the time without any alteration for the next two or three centuries. There is a computer on his ballpoint pen and in his pocket he has an automatic electronic blood pressure kit.

Dan Presser always has something new you haven't heard about. Recently he arrived back from the Consumer Electronics Winter Show at Las Vegas. What was the most cock-eyed thing that he saw? "Well, I think that I would give points to the Nature's Environment machine.

"Nature's Environment is like a whopping great cubby except that it has a hand-crafted teak interior, cypress deck and sliding acrylic doors. The blurb says: 'Here is a retreat that can bring you soothing pleasures of an ocean beach, ancient desert or tropical rain forest. Step inside and you'll experience the pleasures of your secluded oasis whenever desirable to you'."

Apparently you can program this thing to give you the lot, tropical rain, steam, sunshine, massaging spray and "Chinook Winds," all to cassette music to suit your mood. Price for that lot is $12,000.

Dan Presser is a commerce graduate of Queensland University and got into electronics while working for the UK trade office in New York. Ever since he started, he says, the progress has been beyond belief.

"Do you realise," he said, "that as recently as 1972-73 a hand-held computer cost $300? It didn't have memory, floating decimal or anything like that. Hell, you can get one now for $8.95."

He says this is the progression in the electronic hardware business. He talks a lot about electronic hardware and products even tend to get "consumerised." The progression has been minicomputers, digital watches and clocks, CB radio, and the big ones coming off the rank are video games and the family computer.

At Las Vegas there were four types of family computers on display and they ranged in price from $500 to $1500. The most simple were the Bally and the Video Brain. They looked like electric portable typewriters and you plug them in to your TV set.

The blurb for the Video Brain says: "Instant intelligence for your boob tube. When you hook up Video Brain to your TV set, it's like bringing teachers, financial advisers and a lot of entertaining companions into your home."

The idea is that you have a box of cassettes. One is the information for your family accounts, another gives you a read-out on all your stocks and shares, another is for learning music, another is a memory brain for all your recipes, another your Christmas card mailing list, another for preparing your income tax, your medical records, your maths tutor.

Naturally one wished to know whether the family computer would do such much-heralded jobs as brewing the coffee, turning off the pool filter and ordering everything at the supermarket. That's coming, according to Dan, particularly the shopping bit. "They are experimenting now in a town in the US. There are American digital telephones with two extra buttons. You press these to contact the local supermarket and up it comes on the screen. A roving camera shows you all the produce. Everything has a code number. Apples could be A3 or Alka Seltzer could be B5 and you order by pushing digits. You have a credit number and the cost is automatically registered either there or at the bank."

There was a New York Times man at the show and he was spellbound at the thought of all the electronic equipment that was being built into a living room. He said: "The 19th-century drawing room was based on the fireplace, the bookcase and conversation. Traditional furniture arrangements won't accommodate the movie-theatre-cum-operational-computer-centre that is going to take over the home." From now on he sees every living room looking like a "copy of the control room at Newscenter Four."

Oh, there are other things that are going big, according to Dan. Projection TV is IN. Yes, you project the TV image onto a big screen, so you can watch Jimmy Connors, Dennis Lillee or whoever on something like Cinemascope. That costs around $3000.

Frankly, I didn't enthuse over all this electronic magic and I didn't think my uncomplicated brain would ever stretch to coping with a family computer. "Maybe mine won't either," Dan Presser admits, "but I tell you every baby that is born into this era and growing up now will handle them as naturally as we handle the telephone."
