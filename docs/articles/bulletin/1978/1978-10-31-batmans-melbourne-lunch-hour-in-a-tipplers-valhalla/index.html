Lunch hour in a tippler's Valhalla

5 min read.

First published 31 Oct 1978

Melbourne's Expovin wine exhibition drew 100,000 enthusiasts to its 4½-acre floor of stands serving fine reds, pates and champagne, where Malcolm Fraser sampled only Australian wines, Wolf Blass was first in the queue, the Connoisseurs' Terrace poured 1955 Grange Hermitage, and a battery-powered home-bottling pump replaced the age-old pleasure of sucking on a tube.

ACTUALLY it has been a very hard week. Melbourne has just staged its third grand Expovin Wine exhibition. Sydney has also had an Expovin, its first, on the fourth floor at Centrepoint and it drew 38,000 people.

Melbourne was rather more exhausting. It filled the entire Exhibition Building and imbibed, shall we say, more than 100,000 enthusiasts. One of the directors, Robin Bradley, said: "I like to put it this way. Our exhibition extended over 4½ acres and that's bigger than the entire vineyard of Romanee Conti."

There were over 300 wines to be tasted plus an astonishing array of pates, cheeses, breads, sausages, biscuits and what-nots. It cost $3.50 to get in and with this you received a small plastic glass plus tickets to taste 12 wines.

Most citizens went there in the office lunch hour. If the citizen engaged the merchant or winemaker in sufficiently brilliant conversation usually he forgot to ask for his ticket.

The whole show and display were infinitely better this year. Many of the purveyors of Continental wines had turned their stands into little French cafes or bistros. They had little check tablecloths and you sat down to do your sipping.

Right at the entrance there was the Killawarra stand. The Killawarra people believe they have the perfection invention for the summer — the twin cask. It is hexagonal in shape, contains four litres with twin taps. You pop it in the refrigerator and one tap provides you with "cool red" and the other "cool white."

Part of their display was an enormous twin cask and through this they were pumping mock wines composed of tinted water. At least a third of the people who went through didn't appreciate this. They would fill their plastic glass with pink tinted water and say: "I don't know, a little bit thin don't you think?"

At the Lindeman's stand they had a curious sort of game of bingo. The contestants stood at a little desk and the compere would call out predictable names. "Cawarra"... "Montillo Sherry"... "Ben Ean"... "Lindeman's flagons..." As soon as the contestant heard the name he had to press the appropriate button and the result would flash on a screen. The first to get all the names won two champagne glasses.

Robin Bradley said: "I'll give you one guess who was first up to win a couple of glasses. Wolf Blass."

There were some very famous winemakers on the job. At the Rhine Castle stand I met Mr and Mrs Owen Redman from Coonawarra. As you know they have had extraordinary success with their 1976 vintage. Mrs Redman revealed a fascinating tale they'd heard from a gentleman who was planting a thousand vines in Tasmania. "Do you know what he did?" she said. "He bought eighteen magnums of our lovely '76 cabernet and as he planted each vine he poured in a glass as a blessing to give it the proper start in life."

Part of the display was an adjacent stand, Winesearch — "Importers of Fine Wines" — which had a wine decanting machine on sale. This was for delicately decanting your 70-year-old Lafite or Margaux. It had a system of gears, so as you turned the handle it went through an axis on a cradle with never a shudder. Price: $185.

I hurried past a sauna spa. It was a great open barrel filled with steaming bubbling water. It would take six to eight people, and the idea was to sit in there and stew and drink a glass of champagne at the same time. Marvellous for the aches and pains. Price: $2250 to $2500.

Ah, but the piece de resistance this year was the Connoisseurs' Terrace. This had carpet on the floor, potted palms, great tables, and there were beautiful bottles there. You paid an extra $5 to get in. That was to let in only those genuinely interested, you understand. They had some very fancy wines for tasting. For example, there was the Lindemans 1965 Hunter River Burgundy, Bins 3110 and 3100. These come in little individual boxes, dressed up as if they were 1875 cognac.

Robin Bradley, on three occasions, also produced bottles of 1955 Grange Hermitage. The first 25 "connoisseurs" to move past had a glass. Pre-release bottles of the 1973 Grange also were there for tasting.

But you could only get red wine at the Connoisseurs' Terrace — the Big Red Revival, you see. I was told there that the system for the BRR was to convince people that the connoisseurs, all the great palates, really preferred dry red. If you went into a restaurant and spotted a gentleman drinking a bottle of red, immediately you would say to yourself "Ahhh, there's a man of discernment."

One wasn't limited to dry reds. You could get into the old ports, muscats, liqueurs, and there was even a remarkable drink called Calypso Cream. This was a mixture of rum, coffee and chestnuts.

One stand had a non-alcoholic drink called Nothing Thanks. Maybe this was the most inspired concoction of the entire exhibition. I need to get on to Nothing Thanks for about the next month.

Duke & Moorfield had the latest invention for the home bottler. It was a little pump, operated by two torch batteries, price $9.95. It would pump for two hours before needing new batteries. All one had to do was put one end of the tube in the barrel, the other end in the bottle, and throw the switch.

Frankly, I was a little sad about this. One of the great joys of home bottling always has been sucking on the tube to get the siphon operating.

Malcolm Fraser arrived and no doubt he was going through his coupons. Robin Bradley reported the Prime Minister was splendidly patriotic. He said he wasn't interested in tasting any imported wines. He never bought any. He had a few in his cellar but these were only gifts. Furthermore, said Mr Bradley, he was right behind the "Big Red Revival" by tasting only reds.
