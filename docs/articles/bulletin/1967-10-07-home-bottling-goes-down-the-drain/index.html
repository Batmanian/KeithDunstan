Home bottling goes down the drain

6 min read.

First published 07 Oct 1967

A fond elegy for Melbourne's home wine-bottling culture, tracing the pleasures of bulk barrels, siphons and improvised cellars as rising wine prices and shrinking supply begin to kill the tradition off.

It is high time that an earthy character like Batman gave you the poor man's story. The dreadful truth is that there is a crisis ahead in home bottling.

Cellarmaster has warned all the year of the shortage in good wines and now it has reached us. The bad news is quoted from the current "Australian Wine Club News": "It is the low-priced 'binning' wines that worry me. All the winemakers, who were very glad to dispose of their wine in bulk to merchants who bottled it, now in time of shortage, are beginning to bottle and market this wine under their own labels — or sell it in bulk to the large national marketers at extremely low prices."

This means, fellow winos, that sources for home bottling are being cut off. Let me explain. There is little home bottling in Sydney because the Hunter River Valley vineyards have not always been amenable to bulk buying. Home bottling is a Melbourne and Adelaide practice, where the local vineyards have had plenty, as has been the custom, of bulk wine.

It is high class, let us keep up the tone — to compare the quality of home binnings on Sunday mornings.

High-class home binners own their barrels, send them off to the vineyard, and they are returned by rail to the local railway station, freight unbelievably cheap; the primary producer is assisted by the Government. After picking up one's barrel at the railway station, one places it on the garage bench, knocks out the bung, then puts in a length of 3/8-in. plastic tubing, then sucks furiously to start the siphoning arrangement. This is the lovely part. Often frequent suckings are required to get a smooth flow. Then one puts the tube into bottle after bottle.

Corks are purchased from any cork merchant — cost $3 a gross — and a simple corker costs about $2.50. A decent French corker made of brass, a high-speed operation, costs $10.

Now barrels range from little five-gallon jobs up to splendid hogsheads. A hogshead should hold 52½ Imperial gallons and thus provides over 300 bottles. One Melburnian bottling group has been known to organise home binning. The hogshead is kept on a cradle immediately above the cellar. A fat nylon tube goes from the hogshead down into the cellar and then into a lavatory cistern equipped with the traditional ball valve. This gives you turns off the wine as soon as the lavatory cistern is full — just as your lavatory cistern is full of your cabernet shiraz. From the lavatory cistern there are two 3/8-in. plastic tubes with taps. So the team bottles two at a time and can get through a hogshead in just over an hour.

The eternal problem of home binners is storage. Let it be said that the cellar is the ideal solution, but all too few Melbourne homes have cellars. Well, one has to improvise. For this reason it is now almost impossible to buy old wardrobes in Melbourne. Open any inner-suburban bedroom wardrobe and the old wardrobe is solid with bottles. Many couples have bottles stored under every bed, others use the woodshed, some fill their garage with bottles and keep the car outside. One character here lives in a house flat on the ground, but all the floorboards are loose. He stacks bottles on the ground under the floorboards.

Others put bottles in the ceiling. One home binner, a scientist, was asked: "But shouldn't wine be stored so that there is a variation of no more than five degrees?" His reply: "You can't hurt it as long as you don't boil the bloody stuff."

Let us go into the economics of this thing. Until now it has been possible to buy bulk wine of fair quality at $1.50 a gallon. Corks have cost, say, two cents each, transport there and back from the vineyard no more than 10 cents a gallon. You pinch your bottles for nothing in St. Georges Road, so that the bottle costs roughly 20 cents, which is cheaper than beer.

The wine is untaxed, so that many feel guilty when drinking their home binnings — what they are not helping the defence vote and the boys in Vietnam, but they are helping primary producers.

But why is the foregoing written in the present tense? There's no good bulk wine around at $1.50. Even if you know someone it is $1.80; and in many places, if you can get it, you're doing well.

One well-known wine buyer here — from a rather good cellar — said that things were getting so scarce at some vineyards where he used to buy, that one couldn't even get through on the telephone. Wine prices generally: the restaurants here are charging $55 for the 1954 Coonawarra, and the cellar in South Yarra has listed '38, '39, '41, '46, and '47 Reynella wines at $100 a bottle. (Please don't buy more than six at a time!)

High-class home binners, of course, believe deeply in at least .5 dinners for everyone. A .5 dinner is half a bottle of wine with it. But the ideal is 1.5. But this is no longer possible to organise from the cellar. For this reason it is now almost impossible to buy old wardrobes in Melbourne.

Home bottlers, of course, believe deeply in at least 0.5 dinners for everyone. A 0.5 dinner is half a bottle of wine with it. But the ideal is 1.5. But the non-home-binner is becoming very snooty and drinks only the nicest of bottles. One home binner, a university professor, binns in beer bottles with corks for wine but has turpentine-tin corkers — marvellous devices, fully utilised and all. But they look odd with the sterling silver.

And you must appreciate that home bottlers feel deeply about their binnings. Once the wine is in the bottle they feel as passionately as gentlemen who have returned from a trip with a crate of coloured slides.

I remember one home bottler who believed that his product was better than anything on the wine list at the Florentino. So he took products of his home binning to the Florrie for the evening meal. The regrettable part was he bottled in beer bottles. "What's wrong with a beer bottle? Slam in a good cork and it does a mighty job." However, the wine waiter, viewing this beer bottle, handled it as if it had been a mouse found under the table.

Perhaps the good times are on the way out, perhaps they will still come back, but just in case they don't, this little monograph is dedicated to the home bottler, in memory of a beautiful era.
