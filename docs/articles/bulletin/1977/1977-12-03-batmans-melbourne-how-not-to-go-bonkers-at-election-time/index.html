How not to go bonkers at election time

5 min read.

First published 03 Dec 1977

Australia's nine legislatures have produced a federal election every second year since Federation, and with both parties spending $2 million on advertising for the current campaign, Keith Dunstan offers the Bulletin Guide to Mental Purity: avoid TV between 6 and 8.30 pm, listen to FM radio (Wagner's Siegfried is safe), throw away your letterbox junk, read newspapers from the back page, and start conversations about whether the Pope would make a good Presbyterian.

YOU REALISE, of course, that we are the most thoroughly governed people on Earth. The editor of Who's Who In Australia, Stewart Legge, complained last week about the enormous number of politicians he was compelled to put in his publication.

"Look," he said, "we have the six regular State parliaments, then we have the Legislature of the Northern Territory, we have the Legislature in Canberra, ACT, plus the Federal Parliament. That's nine altogether."

We have been told that there have been seven Federal elections in the past 10 years and if you tot them all up we have had a Federal election every second year since Federation. Toss in the State elections and it is clear that you — you overworked creature — are being involved in an election every year of your life.

And if you take our nine legislatures, my God, you can take it for granted that at any one time there is sure to be an election taking place in Australia. It is all too much.

On this current campaign the two big parties are spending $2 million each on advertising through television, radio and the newspapers. During the 1975 campaign the Coalition and the ALP bought a total of 663 hours of television and radio time.

Do you see what I mean? It's all going on for an eternity.

We are not showing sufficient love for their TV politicians. We don't look upon them with the respect which is their due. The trouble is the more you hear, the more you lose faith.

Now my theory is that we would like our politicians more, and our electoral judgment would be far sounder if we saw, heard, and read less of them. If we could go through the 24 hours without our eardrums being thrashed by charges and counter-charges.

Therefore, today I propose to give you The Bulletin Guide To Mental Purity, or How to Live in Australia at Election Time and Actually Like It.

The problem is to steer yourself entirely away from politics.

RADIO: I understand that radio stations are using up to three minutes in the hour on political commercials. Most stations are on the air 120 hours a week. The ABC also is running all sorts of current affairs and discussion programs. Your best hope is Kindergarten on the national station at 10.35 am, or Evening Meditation at 11.10 pm. FM radio, however, is excellent. You are utterly safe from politics there. For example, on Thursday night I notice we are getting Wagner's Siegfried, from the Ring of the Nibelung. I defy anyone to think of politics during that. Then on Friday afternoon we are getting a Mahler symphony, one of those things that goes on for an hour and 25 minutes.

TV: Every party is battling to get on to peak viewing time. You would be well advised to keep right away from your set between 6 pm and 8.30 pm. It is not even safe to switch from channel to channel, because policy speeches and the like are on all four channels. Pick out a horror or an R-certificate film, something that will utterly digest your attention. Something good and dirty, pornography.

NEWSPAPERS: It is very dangerous to start reading but I wouldn't ignore your newspapers entirely. A simple rule is this. If you have a broadsheet like the Sydney Morning Herald, the Age, or the Melbourne Herald, you will find that normally they are divided into two sections. Just throw away the first section and read the second half. Those ghastly full-page ads never get into the second section. Tabloids are not so easy. For the Sun in Melbourne I would suggest you pick it up carefully and start reading from the back page. You will go through the sport, racing, funnies, women's pages, finance, and picture spread with perfect security. Give up at about page nine or 10.

LETTER BOX: A very simple rule, just drop everything into the garbage can that doesn't have a stamp on it.

PEOPLE: This is the hardest part of all. I find that even my dearest friends hold most peculiar views that I don't want to hear. The only thing to do is to start the conversation first. If you can't think of anything, here are a few starters:

Do you think the Queen likes horses better than people?

Would the Pope make a good Presbyterian?

Do you think President Kennedy went off with Marilyn Monroe?

Do you like the idea of consenting homosexuals being married in church?

Are oysters good for your sex life, or is there something better?

As a public relations gesture do you favor running the Melbourne Cup in Sydney occasionally?

Those topics I think would get you off politics for an entire evening. Just paste this page on your bathroom door and have a happy election.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: How not to go bonkers at election time, The Bulletin, 3 December 1977. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
