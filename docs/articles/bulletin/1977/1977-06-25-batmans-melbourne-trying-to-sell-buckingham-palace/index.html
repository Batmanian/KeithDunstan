Trying to sell Buckingham Palace

5 min read.

First published 25 Jun 1977

The ordeal of selling a family home — keeping it like Buckingham Palace in jubilee week while buyers arrive at all hours, inspect the dirty washing and criticise the kitchen, and estate agents dress it up with descriptions like "gracious Edwardian home" for a property with chip bath heater, "superb wooded garden" for morning glory holding up the garage.

I WATERED our monstera deliciosa for the last time yesterday and ran my eye over the cricket pitch area. Ah, that was something. In the longer summers of 64 and 65, by precise count, we broke the sun-room windows 17 times. Over there by the pink pearl rhododendron, that's where we buried little Midge, the corgi. And look at the chimneys up there. By golly, painting those was a hair-raising experience. Look at the way the paint has been spattered all over the tiles.

Yes, we move out next week. I can't tell you what a hard time it has been. You need patience and remarkable sang froid as the prospective buyers troop through. They come at all hours of the day and night. They look in all the cupboards, open the refrigerator, "drink a lot don't they?", climb on the roof, go through the dirty washing in the laundry. They were there even during breakfast. I was ploughing through my muesli enjoying my disasters-at-dawn newspaper when a character looked over my shoulder and said: "Your kitchen's not too good is it?"

In that terrible pre-auction month you have to keep the place looking like Buckingham Palace in jubilee week, beds made at 7 am, dishes washed by 7.30. You're not allowed to leave your underpants lying on the bedroom floor and, ye gods, the garden has to look constantly like something out of the Kew gardens.

Our biggest problem was our poodle, Bessie Bunter, normally a very kindly lovable person. She detested all real estate men and she looked upon every prospective buyer as a predator. Miss Bunter followed them from room to room barking and snarling inhospitality. It didn't help at all.

The greatest cultural shock comes when they put that enormous, vulgar board on your front wall. It brings a faint sense of guilt and humiliation, especially when the children read the glowing words and collapse in hysterical laughter.

They leered at the line, "Gracious Edwardian home." They got the giggles at the mention of "superb wooded garden." But what really got through to them was "spacious double garage with tiled roof." Our lovely garage is held up by morning glory, but let's be accurate. One day, with a little juggling, I did get two Mini Minors in there.

But, of course, the boot has been in the other door, so to speak. We have been looking for a new dwelling and we have become remarkably expert on real estate ads. These are from my collection actually penned on site:

"Price slashed. Was $82,600. Now $64,000. Only internal inspection will reveal the charm and brilliant design of this superb town house." We could hardly wait to get to this one. When we got there we received the tip off: "You want to try getting your car into the garage. I tried with my Merc, couldn't do it. Finally the agent took over. He backed and shoved four times before he got it in, then he couldn't get it out again."

We looked at "executive style home with prestigious address." The Board of Works and the local council thought so too. The rates and taxes came to over $1000.

"Ideal investment opportunity for imaginative purchaser" sounded nice. We discovered that this meant the house was beyond repair, but if your bank manager was sufficiently imaginative you could pull the whole lot down and start again.

"This home superb for decorating to your own individual taste." Indeed it was. It was the estate of an 80-year-old widow deceased and the chip bath heater should have been acquired by the National Trust.

"Architect-designed residence in central location." It was one of those houses an architect designs when he is 25 years old. He is trying desperately to make Home Beautiful or House and Garden and he has designed a mini arts centre with everything but the dripping butcher shop windows.

"Pretty and appealing. Fully tiled bathroom. Hurry for this one. The offer won't last." It wouldn't either. You had only to see the dry rot in the weather boards.

"Glorious bush setting. Room for tennis court and swimming pool." There was too, except that it was on a frigid, windswept plain the other side of Keilor, and four days' camel ride to the nearest public transport.

The final state is that you start attending auctions. Mostly they are on Saturday mornings. In Melbourne a real estate firm wouldn't dream of having a sale on Saturday afternoon because of the football.

We were stunned at the number of people who turned up for these affairs. There are professional auction lovers, who go to them all. They turn up in their Mercedes and their Volvos at 10.30 am, have a great old time snooping the house through from end to end, then stand by to watch the bidding.

At our first three auctions we were terrified. "No chance here," we said to each other. "There's enough money present to buy the Exhibition Building." As it turned out, despite a crowd of 50 or 60, never more than two or three engaged in the bidding.

But best I liked the moving address always given by the auctioneer: "Here we have a house that is unique in every respect. Look at the quality of the address. Look at the convenience of shopping centres, public transport. Look at the beauty of the design. I can tell you, it is rare indeed that we get a house on offer with all the brilliant attributes of this one. Don't come back to me next week and ask for another, because we won't have it. This is the opportunity you must grab NOW."

I wish my senior daughter hadn't giggled when she heard the same speech a week later. But what is it like to actually start moving your goods from house to house? You may hear more of this gripping serial later.
