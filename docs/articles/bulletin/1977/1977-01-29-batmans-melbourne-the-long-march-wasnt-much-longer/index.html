The long march wasn't much longer

4 min read.

First published 29 Jan 1977

A China-watcher reports from St Kilda Road on the queues for Melbourne's Chinese exhibition, which is bigger than Blue Poles, bigger than the Modern Masters, and even bigger than King Kong, with the jade suit of Princess Tou Wan drawing reverent crowds and their irreverent commentary.

AS A CHINA watcher of many years standing I have taken up my position in St Kilda Road. Ah, Melbourne this week is very Chinese indeed. The first thing I note is very clever Chinese PR. They have scored a front page in full color on the Sun News Pictorial. They have scored a supplement in The Age and scads of good space in the Women's Weekly.

They have red bath towels with Chinese insignias hanging from stanchions all the way down St Kilda Road and the Mobil building has gone disturbingly Peoples' Republic with a huge Chinese insignia on top.

On the evidence apparent to me, I can say that the Chinese exhibition is bigger than Blue Poles, bigger than the Modern Masters exhibition which scored 200,000 people. Friends, the Chinese exhibition is even bigger than King Kong. Both have substantial permanent queues but the permanent Chinese queue is precisely 10 times the length of the permanent Kong queue.

I tell you this as a hardened veteran. The Chinese queue starts way back in Sturt Street, goes round the corner into Nolan Street and then commences its long trek up St Kilda Road to the gallery entrance. I found that it took me over half an hour just to get to St Kilda Road and a full one hour 20 minutes before I was even to the front door.

The long wait is very hard on one's peasant legs and even worse for one's humble miserable back. When finally you get past the front door (entry price $2) there is a further waiting period.

Although a China watcher, I have never been an enthusiast for archaeological pots and mortuary relics. The show turns out to be not only larger than they expected but also infinitely more exciting.

The ace, the sort of superstar of the movie, is the jade suit of Princess Tou Wan, who departed this earth back in 113 BC.

The PR was crafty with this. They invited all the Press, radio and TV to a formal unpacking. I bet they had already unpacked it earlier and put it back in the boxes. There wasn't a sound from anyone. It was like one of those anxious moments when Dr Ben Casey is doing his surgery. I was almost listening for the panting of the oxygen machine.

The jade suit came in pieces: feet, legs, head, arms, hands. They slid it all together with almost breathless care and I liked the loving way they put the head so tenderly on its bronze pillow inlaid with gold and jade.

Now the biggest crowd gathers round the jade suit. Just for history's sake I jotted down some of the on-the-spot comments.

"Two thousand one hundred and sixty pieces of jade in that. Fancy making it all up without electric saws 'n stuff like that..."

"Some of the jade's a bit crook. Notice how a lot of it's gone brown?"

"I'd like to have a shoofty under the face."

"It took 10 years to make. How come they knew how big she'd be when they finished it?"

"Be lovely wouldn't it? Watchin' for 10 years someone making your morgue suit."

"I love the ears don't you? Beautiful round piece of jade."

"Fancy going to all that trouble. Didn't work did it."

"Come back Raelene. If you smash that glass the policeman will be on to you."

All the inscriptions on the porcelain, brass, wood, urns and such have been provided by the Chinese Government. Nearly all of them carry a nice little message. There are many references to the "enslaved" populace of the period, downtrodden by feudal landlords.

The inscription on the jade suit is a beauty: "Tou Wan's jade suit sewn with gold thread shown here served as material evidence to expose the extravagance and decadence of the feudal ruling class of that time."

And the thought occurs that if it had not been for all that splendid decadence the People's Republic wouldn't have had this lovely PR exercise to send around the world.

A great deal of money is changing hands. The gross take in Melbourne must go close to half a million dollars. The catalogues are $4 each and then they have a Chinese shop where you can buy your posters, postcards, and all sorts of artifacts like key rings, Chinese rings, brooches, and cuff links. They cost as much as $30.

What will they do with all this money after the exhibition has done its time in Melbourne and Sydney? I'm told that the Art Corporation is after Tutankhamen's coffin. Apparently it has been a sensation in London.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The long march wasn't much longer, The Bulletin, 29 January 1977. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
