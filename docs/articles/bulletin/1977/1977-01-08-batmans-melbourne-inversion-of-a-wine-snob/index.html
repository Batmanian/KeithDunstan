The inversion of a wine snob

5 min read.

First published 08 Jan 1977

Keith Dunstan confesses his long career as a fake wine expert — from home-bottling in turpentine bottles and pillaging Toorak rubbish bins for empties, through the painful stages of wine snobbery, to his current humiliating attempt to wean himself back to the cheap stuff.

DO YOU want me to confess the real truth? I'm not one of those people who can stand up and expound brilliantly on wine. Oh yes, I can look good. I can hold the base of the wine glass delicately between thumb and forefinger, put my nose repeatedly into the glass, and talk for 10 minutes non stop on any wine you put my way.

But the truth is I don't know. Haven't the slightest idea of the real origin of that wine. I'm probably the best liar in town. You may have noticed that the number of wine clubs has begun to proliferate. We have here wine and food societies, beefsteak and burgundy clubs, viticultural societies, things with august names like Confreres de Tastevin, Tuesday clubs, Friday clubs, affairs like Rothbury where you compete for your ribbon, like prize creatures at the show. You work up from your lowly white ribbon through to the royal purple.

Friends, sometimes I have been out tasting wine five nights a week. Suddenly an awful thing has happened. I don't like cheap wine any more. No longer can I afford my palate.

Let me explain my miserable history. My interest in wine began very happily 30 years ago. In those days I began buying wine in gallon bottles. The wine dealer never explained to me that claret tended to go off like milk. I used to take several glasses of wine out of this bottle every night and the deterioration was so steady I never really understood that it was slowly turning to vinegar.

Next I fell among the home bottlers. We started bottling a gallon at a time, then five gallons until I met a marvellous character who did it on a grand scale. He bought his wine by the hogshead, enough for at least 350 bottles.

He had a beautiful system. A carrier would deliver the hogshead, it would be rolled into place on to a cradle on a concrete roof above his cellar. Then he had a large polythene pipe which went downstairs into an old lavatory cistern which was fitted complete with a ball valve. As soon as the lavatory filled to overflowing the ball valve would cut off the flow. Now under the cistern he had two tubes with taps which fitted directly into the bottles. A skilled man could bottle and cork an entire hogshead in 90 minutes. Terrific it was.

One of the loveliest scenes I remember was that of home bottlers wheeling their wheelbarrows loaded with bottles home into the sunset.

There was no sham or nonsense about my friend. He had an in at a turpentine factory and he used to get those triangular turpentine bottles clean and washed. On one occasion he went to the Florentino with his own home bottling. It takes vast courage to do a BYO at the Florentino any time, but this was a matter of principle and believing in what you were doing.

The head waiter took the turpentine bottle of good north-eastern Victorian red and held it with thumb and forefinger as if he were holding a dead rat.

I think my deterioration began soon after that. I started to upgrade my home bottling. I started to actually put labels on my bottles. You could home print or Xerox your own prissy little design. Turpentine bottles weren't proper either, although I can't tell you how magnificently a triangular bottle stacks. Not a suggestion of movement. Oh, no, we began to bottle only in green bottles, then we went for the French.

We used to get these from the back of the Balzac restaurant, which was run by Georges Mora in those days. Otherwise we went to Toorak. I had a very good knowledge of rubbish bin days in Toorak and you wouldn't believe the magnificent empties that you would get beside rubbish bins in St Georges Road or Heyington Place, Toorak, around 6am.

When we were in the hogshead business we were bottling wine for around two shillings a bottle. It was then that I gained a lifelong habit — that it's absolutely essential to have a bottle of wine with every meal. I went to a temperance evening recently and it was very curious. My right hand kept stretching out involuntarily to the north-east all night ... for a glass of wine that wasn't there.

Under the insidious influence of all these wine clubs I discovered that turpentine specials didn't taste as good as some of the bottles that come with proper seals and names of famous companies.

Then I discovered that there were special years, special districts. I discovered that the sweet fizzy stuff I had been drinking at public relations champagne parties all these years, for God's sake, wasn't real champagne at all. I went home and opened a bottle and poured it back into the earth. We have been going inescapably upward, to the point where it now costs me something like 20 cents a meal and rising.

Do you see what I'm driving at? I don't want that awful thing to happen to you. You mustn't get to the stage where you can no longer afford your palate. Right now I am on a careful depalatalisation course. I have weaned myself off first growths like Margaux, Petrus and so on, and I am working back slowly through Coonawarra and by next year I expect to be on the happy waters of Wynvale.

It's not for myself, you understand. My publisher feels my diaries are a little drab. Orlando and McWilliam casks. Pray for me.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The inversion of a wine snob, The Bulletin, 8 January 1977. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
