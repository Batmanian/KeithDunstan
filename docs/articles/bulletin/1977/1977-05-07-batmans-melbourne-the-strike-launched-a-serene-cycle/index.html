The strike launched a serene cycle

5 min read.

First published 07 May 1977

The tanker strike turned Melbourne briefly into something like Amsterdam — cars banished, bicycles everywhere, the Fitzroy Gardens visible for the first time in years, 91,000 people walking to the MCG for Anzac Day, and road deaths dropping to eight — while the union chief's unlisted namesake in the phone book fielded bomb threats.

IT WAS A beautiful fortnight in Melbourne. One wonders whether we can do it at the same time every year, turn it into a festival and give it an Aboriginal name like Moomba. I am thinking, of course, of the tanker strike.

The weather was just right, sunny, mild and dry, everything propitious for the abandonment of the motor car.

As I say, it was beautiful. There were times when you had the feeling they were producing On the Beach again and you looked around for Gregory Peck and Ava Gardner. You could walk down the street and see not a motor car, only humans on rusty bicycles they had pulled out from the back of the garage.

You could see Melbourne from as far off as Gisborne with brilliant clarity. From Port Arlington right across the bay near the head the towers of BHP, Nauru House and Collins Place stood out like tombstones in a graveyard.

And going home in the evening you could get all those delicious smells of what people were having for dinner. Chops in number two, mixed grill in number four, liver and onions in six, marvellous garlic and spaghetti smells from eight.

Then all these years we have never really been able to see the Fitzroy and Treasury Gardens. They have been surrounded by an appalling stockade of automotive metal. For two weeks this was gone.

Whereas normally we tend to kill about 30 people on the roads over a long weekend this time we managed only eight.

There were so many bicycles on the road we began to get a faint idea what it is like in Peking or Amsterdam and for the first time in a decade many citizens actually got out and walked. The Melbourne Hilton and the Southern Cross, on the other hand, did fine business. Many firms just booked their executives into nice suites until the strike was over.

However, you must appreciate the withdrawal symptoms were awful and many citizens behaved in a very strange manner. There were bashings, knifings, there were men who went down streets with pick axes, driving holes through petrol tanks.

Sometimes, incredibly, we went as long as two days without a bank hold-up, we were having garage hold-ups instead. Petrol was being ferried from Mount Gambier, Albury, Mildura and selfless citizens who had struggled so hard to provide a service for their neighbors were selling it for as little as $7.50 a gallon.

The hospitals, furthermore, were looking after hundreds who had been sucking it through tubes when trying to siphon from one tank to another.

Those garages which were supplying emergency services were interested in the number of ladies who managed to produce yellowing nursing certificates and doctors who were working so hard that they would get their Jaguar filled at 9.30 am and then be back with a Fairlane at 10.

There were those who were prepared to queue all night and those who burned up half a tank in just restless touring and searching. It was interesting also to listen to the music of some once-noble machines that were trying to continue asthmatically on lawn mower fuel.

However, you will be pleased to know that Melbourne is a city of true value and sensitivities. We kept our petrol for the vital things. On Anzac Day there was a crowd of over 91,000 at the Melbourne Cricket Ground to see Richmond play Collingwood. The Sun News Pictorial took a picture of the surrounding parkland and I tell you, it killed Disneyland or any picture of a car park I have ever seen.

There was a fair amount of animosity against the Transport Workers' Union. There were nurses who wanted to black ban members if they ever came in for medical treatment. The union chief, the man who had his photo on the front page of every paper every day, was Ivan Hodgson. He wasn't in the telephone book, but Jan Hodgson, an engineer with ABV-2, was. His telephone never stopped ringing, particularly with calls from kindly ladies who wanted to cut his throat. Ultimately he began to receive bomb threats and he had to stay home for two days to protect his menage. The police gave him a bodyguard.

Meanwhile the Bicycle Institute of Victoria has kept up an attitude of helpful sympathy during the strike. It pointed out that this was just the start of the energy problem and General Motors and Ford would be interested to know that it was possible to produce 100 bicycles from the materials of just one small car and at least 130 from a V8 machine.

However, there was a real possibility that five or 10 years from now most automobiles could become redundant. Car disposal would be a real problem. So the BIV offered these handy hints.

You could carefully seal your car, pump it full of water and use it as an aquarium. This is ideal for station wagons.

Sound proof it, put it in your living room, so that it could become a parental refuge when children were playing hi-fi records.

Turn it into a cubby house at the bottom of the garden. Paint it bright yellow and use it as a special telephone box for the children. Ideal for Volkswagens, Hondas and Minis.

Cut the roof off, fill it with earth and turn it into a charming flower tub.

Take the seats out, put beds in and use it as a handy bungalow when the relatives visit.

Cut a hole through the centre and use it as a Henry Moore sculpture.

Yes, during our annual un-fuel festival we could even have an un-motor show.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The strike launched a serene cycle, The Bulletin, 7 May 1977. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
