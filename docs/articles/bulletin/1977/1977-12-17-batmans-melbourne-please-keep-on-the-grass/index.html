Please keep on the grass

5 min read.

First published 17 Dec 1977

The muesli-and-fitness revolution has overtaken Keith Dunstan's household — 5BX exercises, isometrics, Japanese cookers, vitamin E for immortality, lunchtime joggers fleeing their coronaries through the Treasury Gardens — but after mixing muesli in the bath and graduating to a plastic rubbish bin, the most remarkable result is a stomach like a five-gallon fermenting tank, and the next trend is grasses, herbs and weeds, with nasturtiums recommended to arouse the sluggish man from his torpidity.

MY TROUBLE has always been this — I believe everything I read, particularly the stuff from the latest health fanatics. At our place we haven't left a trend unturned. We were very nearly the first into 5BX — trying to do bunny hops and read the chart at the same time nearly drove me out of mind. Isometric exercises — our family was way out in front there too. I didn't mind squeezing the steering wheel at traffic lights so much, it was the pushing against the sides of doors and pretending to lift up my desk that made me feel slightly foolish.

We have put gardens inside, cooked with health-giving Japanese cookers outside, eaten vitamin C tablets with all the gusto of salted peanuts because they're supposed to stop colds. On the advice of Lady Cilento we have gone mad with the vitamin E in the belief that it'll make us live forever.

I've jogged and I've tried bicycles. The jogging thing is still booming and the most haunting sight you get in Melbourne now is the businessman lunchtime jogger. You see them in droves up and down Flinders Street, down Collins Street, through the Treasury Gardens and all around the Shrine, desperately fleeing from their coronaries.

Frankly, I won't be in lunch-time jogging. It makes me too sleepy when I get back to my desk at 2 pm. No, this week we are depending for salvation on muesli, and life is even more complicated than ever. Bacon and eggs is out because that kills you with cholesterol and you can't have toast and marmalade because you get too much sugar and too much starch.

The theory since last Tuesday is that fibre is the answer, so you've got to have fruit and rough cereals. When you start in this business naively you buy your cereals, already prepared in tiny little boxes and the stuff is practically as expensive as scotch. You learn then that you've got to mix your own muesli so you go down to the health food shop and buy your lecithin, wheat germ, coconut, dried fruit, raisins, apricots, muscatels, nuts, oatmeal, bran and mix it all together. That's another thing — you finish with half the muesli on the kitchen floor. We have tried mixing it in the bath, but a plastic rubbish tin is the best.

By the way, none of this is cheap. Perhaps someone will explain some day why dried pears and dried apricots are more expensive than chocolates. Forget that idea about putting your son into plumbing or bricklaying, a health food shop is the answer.

All right, you've got your muesli — what next? You add fresh bananas, some chopped orange and pineapple and then yoghurt on top. No, no, it's utterly not done in the pure muesli school to add common milk. Yoghurt's expensive too, so now we have to operate our own yoghurt-maker. This is a bit like an electric blanket designed to slowly cook skim milk. We get the hang of it sometimes and make beautiful yoghurt, but other times we produce a concoction which is a bit like the awful old-fashioned junket or that white stuff that gets on the top of the Yarra. But if you have proper mind control, you kid yourself it's wonderful.

And what does all this do for you? Let me just whisper something privately to you in a corner. One of the most remarkable results of eating fibre-charged muesli is that your stomach becomes like one of those five-gallon size brewing kits, in short a fermenting tank that rumbles constantly. A friend of mine puts it more succinctly. Never ride behind a muesli eater on a tandem bicycle.

As your top-trend expert and critic, naturally you are looking to me to find out what is coming next. So that you can be first with it in your street.

All right, get into grasses, herbs and weeds. I have noticed a proliferation in the book shops of weed and herb books. For example I have a little book in front of me now titled Eat The Weeds, a fascinating guide to sources of free, flavorful, delicious food by Ben Charles Harris. (Distributor Thomas C. Lothian, $2.95)

He goes on about clover — ideal in soups and stews. He is mad about rose hips — 60 times the vitamin C of lemon. Acorns? Great for soup stock and bread flour.

He is really wound up about nasturtiums, both leaves and petals. He says: "A sluggish man should eat nasturtiums to arouse him from his torpidity."

Personally, sometimes I yearn back to the old-fashioned breakfast. I notice that an Antarctic lone yachtsman said recently that the things he missed most when away were sex and bread and butter. I can particularly understand the bread and butter bit.
