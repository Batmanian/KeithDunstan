Practice makes perfect, but not wealth

4 min read.

First published 14 May 1977

At the first Australian Gold Panning Championships in Bendigo, where world champion Dorse Prosser arrives with his gold pan strapped to his briefcase and 81-year-old Jesse Starnes presides, David Henderson from Eaglehawk wins in 13.1 seconds, though he confesses 40 years of panning has yielded him only three ounces of gold.

THE TOURIST battle is becoming increasingly rugged in Victoria. We have had the Rabbit Skinning Championship of the World at Moyston, we had the Boomerang Throwing Championship of the World at Albury in which, for once, I took part. And now we have the Gold Panning Championships at the gold cities of Castlemaine and Bendigo.

You must appreciate that gold panning in North America is a deadly serious matter. In Colorado, Wyoming, Montana, Idaho, British Columbia, and the Yukon gold panners go on a circuit from contest to contest just like the great tennis players or golfers. The World titles take place every year in September in Taylor, British Columbia.

All right then, we have just held our first contest in Bendigo and the first prize was a trip to Canada for the big pan-out in September. I tell you it was really something — Dorse Prosser the reigning world champion, a tall, lean, taciturn gentleman with a moustache, arrived for the event. He had his own gold pan neatly strapped to his briefcase. With him was another hot pan, Jesse Starnes from Taylor, president of the World Panning Championships. Jesse admitted to being 81 years old.

There was a further similarity to Frank Sedgman, Jimmy Connors and Co. Just as the tennis men have a glut of tennis rackets, the gold men have a clatter of gold pans. Jesse proudly showed us the dish he had been using since 1913.

The contest took place at the Porcupine Pottery and the competition had come from all over Australia and even New Guinea.

The battle was divided into three parts. First the champions had to separate 10 fine grains of gold from a dish full of spoil in five minutes; then they had to separate five coarse grains in the fastest time; finally there was the 100-metre relay, three to a team.

Already there had been a bit of bother. The organisers had issued every man with a brand new pan. Can you imagine the horror, the shame of panning with a pristine shining new pan. There was more to it than that. The new pans had a film of grease on them which made it impossible for them to hold the fine gold. So all the pans had to be burnt over a hot flame to make them satisfactorily horrid.

Oh, there was a little grandstand for the crowd and the competitors operated over sawn-off 44-gallon drums, which were filled with water. The distinguished American visitors didn't compete — they merely observed and gave demonstrations.

There were seven in the grand final, but finally the battle narrowed down to two men — they passed the first two tests brilliantly without losing gold.

They were Cecil Shuttleworth, a railwayman from Stawell, and David Henderson, a pensioner from Eaglehawk.

Cecil was very formal in shirt, tie, blue trousers and shiny black shoes.

David Henderson was marvellous, straight out of an S. T. Gill painting. He had a grey beard, a floppy digger's hat with a piece of rope tied round the brim, big boots, nondescript shirt and trousers and for the ultimate in security he wore braces as well as a big leather belt.

I ask you to believe these fellows — no rush or violence, just glorious economy of movement with utterly precise circular flow of water washing across the pan. Cecil Shuttleworth, who told us later that he lived through the Depression by working as a prospector, found the nugget in 15.3 seconds. It seemed impossible to beat.

David Henderson was so nervous he couldn't stand still. He paced up and down. He prepared a little hole in the ground for his knee so that he would have a perfect stance. The organisers handed him the dish. He seemed to make about 10 precise movements and then he shouted "Gold" — 13.1 seconds — the first champion of Australia and our future world competitor.

Dorse Prosser won his 1976 world title with 10.2, but even so he was slightly awed by Henderson's panning power. He said the gummy red Bendigo mud provided appalling conditions and our man would do much better in Taylor.

Personally I was a little saddened by the whole operation. Mr Henderson said he had been panning for 40 years and in that time he had found only three ounces of gold. So if one has to take to the road during any coming depression a man will have to look for something more rewarding than gold.
