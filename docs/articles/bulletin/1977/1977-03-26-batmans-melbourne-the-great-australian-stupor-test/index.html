The great Australian stupor test

4 min read.

First published 26 Mar 1977

The Centenary Test brought magnificent cricket and magnificent crowds — but also yobs in towelling hats and thongs with eskies the size of coffins, chanting "Lill-ee, Lill-ee" whether Lillee was bowling or not, and booing brilliant cover drives by the opposition.

IT WAS a lovely week, lovely to talk to all those great old cricketers... Evans, Ames, Hutton, Washbrook, Bedser, Ikin, Compton ... the benign Moomba sun shone down and the organisation of the Victorian Cricket Association and the Melbourne Cricket Club for the Centenary Test was marvellous beyond belief. There was nothing they didn't do for their visitors.

One thing, however, is very worrying and that is the crowd. I have seen the yobs in action this year at country race meetings. I have seen them ruin the Rutherglen Wine Festival and I have seen these yobbos at the Perth, Adelaide, Sydney and Brisbane cricket grounds.

There's no question, the Great Australian Stupor is a frightening thing. There were very big crowds at the Centenary Test, but not the 100,000 capacity job that was predicted; always fine seats in the outer and the MCG crowd was never very witty. Perhaps once upon a time the MCG crowd was the most knowledgeable in Australia. Not any more.

The yobs' uniform: black footy shorts, towelling hat or battered Bazza digger's hat and thongs. Some of the T-shirts were beauties. I remember one character with a cantilever 18-stone gut and the message on the front was "I was a breast fed baby." Another had a shirt entirely covered with penises. There was a young lady who had "I am a virgin" on her bosom, but underneath in smaller letters one could read "But this is an old T-shirt."

One of the reasons they don't get more people into the MCG outer is that the eskies take up so much room. They are becoming bigger and bigger. There's a whopper job now which is literally as large as a coffin. It has got great handles on the corners and you actually carry it like a coffin. If you filled it to capacity it would hold more than 200 bottles.

The morning session is usually quiet enough. Between lunch and tea the yobs become very hearty. The idea is to get through sufficient cans to make yourself a little grandstand. The plastic lacing on the cans makes this ideal. Two dozen empties and you can raise yourself to about eight feet.

Between tea and stumps they get wild and abusive with four-letter words coming in an uninterrupted stream. There is a constant can clatter as they cascade down the concrete steps.

There were lots of flags and banners, Eureka flags, Australian flags, flags which just said "Lillee," "Walker" and "Dennis."

In 30 years of cricket reporting I can't remember any cricketer causing the sensation that Dennis Lillee is causing. The crowd even cheered him when he came in to bat. They cheered him just for being at the wicket. They were thrilled when he actually made contact with the ball. But Lillee making a run, that was bliss, that was the equivalent of Larwood knocking over three England batsmen in a row.

Then there's this "Lill-ee, Lill-ee, Lill-ee" thing. It goes on non-stop all the day he is bowling. But as it nears the moment of delivering a ball it becomes louder and louder like an approaching hurricane and when the whole crowd is in full voice it was awe inspiring and quite terrifying.

Those beautiful women in the outer who didn't appear to be drunken yobbos took part in the Lill-ee thing for most of them, but it was more of a gladiatorial blood sport thing for most of them.

When no wicket had fallen for a few minutes they had another tactic — bounce, bounce, bounce, and "Get Lillee to get the pommy bastards."

But "Lill-ee, Lill-ee, Lill-ee" is the battle cry which can be howled at any time, whether Lillee is there or not. When the day's play was all over and the crowd was at the Richmond railway station I was fascinated to note that they were still chanting "Lill-ee, Lill-ee."

Having said all that I would point out that on every occasion the yobs hooted the streakers for holding up the game and when each streaker was captured the police received an applauding cheer. That is entirely new.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The great Australian stupor test, The Bulletin, 26 March 1977. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
