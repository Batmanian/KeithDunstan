The FJ Holden is today's ideal car

5 min read.

First published 21 May 1977

Prompted by the petrol crisis and President Carter's demands for smaller cars, Keith Dunstan proposes reviving the FJ Holden, weighing seven cwt less than a modern Kingswood, capable of 34 miles to the gallon, and now acquiring a dashing romantic symbolism via a new Australian film.

SEVERAL WEEKS back your pioneer rode down Bourke Street with the Minister for Transport, Peter Nixon, in a very distinguished old motor car. It was a 1928 A model Ford.

We got to talking about its virtues. Here was an automobile that had almost everything to meet the needs of today, marvellous wide running boards for sitting on at barbecues by the river, solid bumper bars and magnificent fenders. You could push your thumb against them and it was clear that they were solid.

But best of all, the top speed for this car was around 55 mph. Its steady cruising speed, one that you could maintain all day on the Hume Highway, was 45 mph. Mr Nixon agreed that if only Australia were filled with A model Fords the Gallipoli that we are creating on the roads would be reduced by half.

Since then several things have happened. President Carter has demanded a change in the U.S. automobile industry. The high speed V8 guzzler is virtually finished, and similar advice is being given by our Energy Advisory Committee. It is calling for lower speeds and smaller cars. The solution is not really to switch to A model Fords, I have a much better idea. Bring back the FJ Holden.

The idea is full of logic. General Motors-Holden is in terrible trouble. It has been trying to lay off its staff because of yards full of unsold cars.

Meanwhile a strange mawkish nostalgia has swept the country for antique Holdens from the days when GM-H was going great and had 47 percent of the market.

Nostalgia for classic models is a very serious movement. In the U.S. specialist car builders have gone back to building Duesenbergs, Stutzes, grand old Mercedes and that sort of thing.

We have just seen the birth of a film called The FJ Holden and I understand when the film was being made the big problem was to get suitable models. Oh, they were around but their owners were so desperately in love with their perfect mint condition FJs they didn't want to part with them.

In a curious sort of a way the FJ is becoming the symbol of the dashing, free-living young. I haven't seen the film yet but I believe it opens with a young lady copulating with a couple of chaps in a very nice FJ.

So can you appreciate the possibilities of this. The FJ wasn't actually the first model, there was another before that and Mr Chifley first presented it to Australia in November 1948. I remember my father received his No 1 Holden that Christmas. The waiting list already had grown to two years and the prestige of having the first Holden was even better than owning a Rolls-Royce.

And how advanced it was; no chassis. It had what they called a monocoque construction, a "development of the streamlined aircraft construction." There were some interesting variations from aeroplanes. We noticed that when we slammed the door hard all the door handles popped off on the inside.

The handbrake was really only useful as an instrument on which to hang a handbag and the bench seats were covered with a material that appeared to be discarded army blankets. Ah, but it was light, it was manoeuvrable, easy to park and gave no mechanical trouble whatever.

Just for your benefit I have dived into the file room and turned up the 1948 comments of the motor writers. All right, the price was 733 pounds ten shillings, which included 117 pounds tax. Now for a Belmont you pay $5836 and the tax is $1259. For a good start in the nostalgia stakes Mr Lynch could well bring back the FJ tax.

GM-H claimed that this new Holden would do 37 miles to the gallon. Melbourne's Sun didn't believe it, so they despatched their man on a drive to San Remo. He claimed that he had four passengers. He stopped 15 times. He became caught in traffic jams, yet on open stretches of road he took that car up to 55 mph.

For the entire trip there and back he recorded 34.89 miles to the gallon.

Mr Carter is calling for lighter cars. The No 1 Holden weighed 19 cwt 102 lb compared with today's Kingswood which is 26 cwt 53 lb.

The motoring writer of 1948 recorded that the new Holden was perfectly stable; that it cornered well, that the engine purred along effortlessly and that it had a top speed of just over 80 mph.

The top speed is too high. My memory of the first Holden is that it tended to become airborne at 70. If we could get that top speed down to 65 mph I am sure the whole concept of the car would be ideal for today.

And think what GM could do with it in the U.S. Jimmy Carter would jump at the idea.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The FJ Holden is today's ideal car, The Bulletin, 21 May 1977. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
