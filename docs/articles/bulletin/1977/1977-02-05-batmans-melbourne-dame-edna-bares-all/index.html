Dame Edna bares all — in limp and stiff

5 min read.

First published 05 Feb 1977

Dame Edna Everage arrives by Rolls-Royce with a mounted police escort and the Dandenong Marching Grannies to launch her Coffee Table Book, a guide to gracious living featuring funnel web spider cake, Norm's prostate diagram, and poems denouncing Melbourne's massage parlors.

IT IS a startling, even unnerving experience, to observe Dame Edna Everage launching one of her publications.

By now you will be aware that the good lady has spawned a publication titled Dame Edna's Coffee Table Book. It was done in great style. She arrived by Rolls-Royce with an escort of Mounted police, the Tramways Band and the Dandenong Marching Grannies. The Marching Grannies were almost too good to be true. They first appeared five or six years ago. They felt if the 14-year-olds could do it, then so could they. So these venerable ladies, in their 70s and 80s got themselves marching girl uniforms and marched. Dame Edna thought they were "abso-loot-ly" something.

Dame Edna wore violet-blue hair, diamente butterfly glasses and a brilliant floral gown by Lillian "Luxury" Wightman.

There was a Greek flag on the Rolls pulled up for the reception at the Gas and Fuel Corporation and she explained: "I couldn't get a Union Jack, but a Greek flag is appropriate. There are so many dear, little Greek people in Moonee Ponds these days."

Then turning to a photographer who had a somewhat olive skin, she said: "Anhh, and are you a dear, little Greek person?" "No," replied the photographer, "I am a dear, little Roman person." Dame Edna was unabashed. "Isn't that nice. You've had your moments of glory, too. And you know I have been staying at the Windsor Hotel with that nice tinted delegation from China. And would you believe, outside one of the rooms yesterday morning I saw a dear, little pair of jade shoes... and I knew they must have belonged to a Chinese mummy."

When it came to the book launching, Dame Edna told of the high moral tone of her marriage with Norm Everage. "Never at any time in our marriage has Norm seen me naked," she said. "And I am sure he would never want to."

Then with almost demonic ferocity she read to us a poem titled "Dame Edna's Message to the Women of Australia." It began:

I have loved Australia all my life and it seems a terrible pity, that Melbourne, the city of my birth is called 'massage parlour city' How tragic that some tree-lined streets you wouldn't look at twice, conceal within their leafy shade pulsating dens of vice — How sickening, that, as I speak ey husbands like yours and mine, are slipping into bubble baths a stone's throw from the Shrine Right now in a St Kilda den, Someone's beloved father lies lewdly smeared from head to foot with baby oil and lather What tarnished trollop rubs them down? what hussy rubs them up? and if they get a cup of tea what germs lurk in the cup We women have a job to do to tempt our menfolk home and save them from those grasping hands which grope them neath the foam. It's up to you to make the most of hands and mouth and hair, that's why I wrote this little book its wisdom might just save you.

On your behalf I have read through Dame Edna's contribution and to be honest it barely merits the grand title "Coffee Table Book." But then to be fair there is a note on page 12 which states "This work is published in two editions: limp and stiff. The stiff edition is specially bound by craftsmen and signed by the authoress herself and strictly limited to 250 copies."

The book is every woman's guide to gracious living. Dame Edna shares with everyone her secrets on beauty care, freckles, pimples, superfluous hair and how she preserves her wonderful complexion. She tells all about her sex life and even gives intimate details on Norm's prostate complete with diagram, an absolute first for a coffee table book.

"Nowadays Norm is hooked up to a miracle prostate machine which chugs away cheerfully on his bedside table. The nurse makes sure it is kept as bright as a button, and my darling has decorated the area with personal knick-knacks. The wonderful Australian invention can also be operated manually, in the event of power failure or strike, though I hope there isn't one or poor Norm will have to pedal twice as hard — and guess who will have to mend the sheets."

"Travelling as much as I do and living out of my Vuitton or Mark Cross suitcases can put an awful strain on a middle-aged couple, particularly when the woman is famous, successful and still has her natural juices and drives. That is why it was a big relief to learn that Norm was to be more or less a permanent guest at the hozzie. Our family doctor felt my poor husband was well enough to spend the odd weekend at home, but thank God I had the presence of mind to get a second opinion."

A terribly important contribution to our culture is Dame Edna's chapter on recipes. We get Valmai's Indonesian meat balls, funnel web spider cake, Madge's apricot prune mould, jellied lambs brains a la Melbourne, Valmai's passionfruit crunchies, Anzac biscuits and Victorian eggie shapes. There were a few ethnic recipes such as braised emu leg, groper surprise, bandicoot stewed in milk and Jewish biscuits, but the sensation was the funnel web spider cake, bright green with a large funnel web spider on top. We each received a complimentary cake on our departure.

I am saving mine for a special treat.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Dame Edna bares all — in limp and stiff, The Bulletin, 5 February 1977. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
