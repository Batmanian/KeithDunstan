The human Melbourne Cup

5 min read.

First published 23 Apr 1977

At Stawell's centenary Gift — the professional athletics' Melbourne Cup — Warren Edmondson, a lean Negro from Los Angeles carrying a can of Fosters, psyches himself up for an hour before exploding off the backmark to win, while 94-year-old former champion Chris King recalls that in his day the shorts had to touch the knees for decency.

YOU HAVE TO appreciate that here in Victoria we are slightly peculiar. There are things that we are more earnest about than the rest of you such as money, footballers, horses, fines, going to bed early and would you believe — professional foot running. We are one of the last places where this is accepted with public enthusiasm.

What's more, out here in the Malcolm Fraser country, where life is expected to be easy, we have the town of Stawell, which is the professional athletics' capital of the world. Every year at Easter we have the Stawell Gift and this literally is the Melbourne Cup for athletics. They don't shoot and drag you off the ground when you break a sesamoid bone, but there are many similarities.

There have been occasions when athletes have been threatened, when they have had to be protected, times when there have been great betting plunges, when mysterious training sessions have gone on out of town.

The most revered man of all was Jack Donaldson, the Carbine of running, the Blue Streak, and there is a drinking fountain in his honor. Yet he never won the gift. He was just 20 and a brand new professional when he competed in 1906. In the big final a horse-shoe pitcher threw a shoe against the steel stake. Donaldson thought it was the gun and he was off. The judges penalised him a yard and E. W. Thompson beat him into second place by centimetres, a little tragedy that Stawell still regrets.

Stawell is one of those rare places where they have a bookmakers' ring on an athletics ground and they call the odds on humans. My father used to hand out the sound advice "Never bet on anything that can talk."

This year was particularly grand for it was the centenary gift. The Governor was invited, every former winner was invited. The oldest old boy there was Chris King, 94, who won the gift in 1908. He said little had changed. The gear was just as good then, particularly the high quality hand-made spikes. The athletes were just as fast, except the track now was maybe three metres faster.

"The big difference," he said, "was this. It was the rule that the bottoms of your shorts had to touch your knees for proper decency." Then he added that as soon as nobody was looking you pulled them up before you started running.

The star at the gift was Warren Edmondson, a lean, slight Negro from Los Angeles, California. He had already announced that he was the fastest athlete in the world and he was going to win "the bloody gift" if it was the last thing he did. He had performed at the gift before and already this season had been fined $500 for allegedly not performing his best at Ballarat.

Warren, though, was an amiable fellow and as a tribute to his sponsors he was often seen walking around with an unopened can of Fosters in his hand.

Central Park at Stawell is beautiful. It has a lovely old wooden grandstand, it has little statues and it is surrounded by beeches, peppers, planes and stately old gums.

On the big day Edmondson stood out. He remained alone with his manager in the centre of the ground and he went through the longest, slowest warm-up session I have seen. He wore a scarlet track suit and a red engine driver's cap; he writhed, he stretched, he knee-bended for hour after hour. When people came to talk to him — he didn't answer. He was getting psyched up.

After all, he had a task. He was the backmarker on 1.25 metres in 130 metres. Some of his opponents in the final had better than 10 metres on him.

Oh, they build the tension very well, step by step. They announce the finalists one by one, tell the history, let you watch them take their position, just like leading the horses out at the Melbourne Cup. Then they ask for silence and for minutes there's this awesome, unbelievable silence. The gun went off. Edmondson was way, way, hopelessly to the rear. There were screams: "Irvine's got it."

Edmondson, suddenly, seemed to wind up; it was as if he were projected by a spring. His strides were worth two of the others. He catapulted to the front to win. He danced, he jogged, he ran up and down shaking hands with the crowd. He did a catherine wheel. He hugged his trainers and he shouted "Wow, Wow, Wow."

As for his prizes, he received a cheque for $7000, a gold medal, a $300 silver tea and coffee service, a washing machine, a silver tankard, and a methuselah of champagne. That's the equivalent of eight bottles. The actual bottles are made in France and the deposit alone is $10.

I was really sorry for Edmondson. He could hardly lift the darned thing.
