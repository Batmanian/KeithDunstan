A fight over faded glory

5 min read.

First published 18 Jun 1977

The Regent Theatre sits in comic stalemate — the Municipal Officers' Association banning its reconstruction, the Builders' Laborers banning its demolition, and costing $800,000 a year while doing nothing — with enough colourful characters to cast a perfect film about its long and tortured story.

FOR A GRAND Melburnian debacle, which would you prefer? The King's Street bridge, the lower Yarra crossing, the Newport powerhouse, or would you have the Regent Theatre?

Agreed, the Newport powerhouse is a muck-up of majestic proportions, but don't you feel that the Regent Theatre thing would make a better movie — that's if you could hire enough off-beat comedians to play all the roles?

I would just like to consider the present brilliant position. One union, the Municipal Officers' Association, has placed a black ban on its reconstruction. The Builders' Laborers Federation has a black ban on its destruction.

Doing nothing with it is costing more than $800,000 a year.

We have a lord mayor who wants to pull it down, and a previous lord mayor who wants to reconstruct it. We have a Premier who wants to save it, but when the council tries to give the theatre to the State Government what happens? Acting Premier Thompson makes it clear he wants no part of it, and the Public Service Association threatens to take industrial action if the government takes over the cost of restoring and running it.

I like some of the opinions that have come out in the past week or two. The Regent is: "a decrepit chook pen"; "a crumbling old relic"; "a ghastly swamp of useless nostalgia."

The line about the decrepit chook pen came from the Victorian secretary of the Bricklayers' Federation, Mr Giles. You see, the bricklayers want to get on with the wrecking and building while the builders' laborers do not. Giles thinks that Norm Gallagher and his team are "half-baked greenies."

There was another touch. Lord Mayor Osborne called for a get-together with Gallagher. He told Gallagher that we had a series of disastrous theatres already, without another. Her Majesty's and the Comedy were up for sale and he offered to save Her Majesty's as a swop for the Regent. Gallagher said "no go."

And then followed the best comment of all. The mayor told Gallagher that if they restored the Regent as a theatre there would be huge operating losses. Gallagher replied: "If money losses are the sole criteria for demolition then why not axe the Shrine of Remembrance."

Just to help the plot for our movie let's give you some of the scenario. The Regent closed as a movie house on July 1, 1970. In the next seven years we had lots of proposals for it, which have included: an international hotel; an office block; an extension to the city square.

What with changes in the international finance scene, the $40 million hotel plan failed. Black bans fixed up the office block and the square extensions.

There have been two inquiries. The council, at a cost of $18,000, sought a special report from the Sydney town planning firm of Clarke Gazzard. It said the Regent should be saved.

That didn't get anywhere, and in the midst of the ghastly stalemate Hamer recommended a government inquiry which included board members Harry Miller, Ron Lyon, Milton Johnson and Louis Pyke. Their report came out in 1975, and they recommended that the theatre be converted into a 4000-seat centre for chamber music, ballet, opera and variety concerts.

Reluctantly the reigning lord mayor, Ron Walker, gave the go-ahead to restore the Regent at a cost of $2.5 million. Another report, by accountants, doesn't like the economic future and the new lord mayor, Cr Osborne, is trying to have it wrecked.

There have been other little touches. Loris Webster, president of the Save the Regent Committee, has fought valiantly all along, and we have had barrages like that from the 12 past presidents of the Royal Victorian Institute of Architects, who wrote to the Age pleading for demolition, saying that the Regent was "a poor example of architecture."

My idea is to restore the theatre and open with the film of the restoration. After all, when the theatre first opened, on March 15, 1929, the Herald described it as "the greatest theatrical event in the world for 1929," and it said: "Tonight 3500 people will witness a revelation. When you have seen the Regent, when you have sat spellbound by the pageant of stars of the screen, music and stage you will truly know the new entertainment that delights London and New York."

They said the movie, which starred Ronald Colman and Wilma Banky, would "wipe out the memory of the finest show you ever saw."

I don't see why our movie, to be titled Quince Regent, shouldn't do just that. The two Ronnies could play the two lord mayors. Noel Ferrier would be perfect for Norm Gallagher, Barry Humphries for Dick Hamer and Dawn Lake for Loris Webster.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: A fight over faded glory, The Bulletin, 18 June 1977. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
