A far cry — or yodel — from Bazza

4 min read.

First published 22 Jan 1977

On the set of Bruce Beresford's "The Getting of Wisdom," Barry Humphries plays a pious Presbyterian headmaster while a young Susannah Fowle takes the lead role of Laura — a world away from Bazza McKenzie, with the original PLC demolished and scenes shot at Illawarra's grand staircase.

HOW FASCINATING to observe Barry Humphries as a pious, awful, platitudinous, inhibited Presbyterian headmaster. He has grey hair, a long sad, drooping moustache with mutton chops. He wears the God collar, frock coat, gold watch and chain and mean little gold, wire-rimmed spectacles.

What a contrast to the exotic days of Dr Meyer Delamphrey, Senator Douglas Colin Monash Manton, Dame Edna Everage and all the awful rest.

One feels that Bruce Beresford is getting his own back. After all he was director, you could even say chundermaster, of two Bazza McKenzie films, and although they made immense sums of money and almost permanently altered the English language, they did give him a certain reputation.

The film he is directing right now in Melbourne is quite the opposite. It is "The Getting Of Wisdom," the novel Henry Handel Richardson wrote about her fearful days as a schoolgirl at Melbourne's PLC.

The book originally only had two editions and was utterly abhorred at PLC. Years later Henry Handel Richardson returned to her old school and she describes the experience in her autobiography. "When I tried to revisit my old school I was refused admittance and had to content myself with peering through the cracks in the paling fence."

Bruce Beresford says the book is now selling 25,000 copies a year: it is an accepted classic and all is forgiven at PLC. So much so the old school is doing everything possible to co-operate, and they are even lending some of the original furniture that was there in Ethel Richardson's day.

You will remember that finding the right cast, getting suitable schoolgirls, and particularly, finding "Laura" to play the starring role in the novel, was a tough problem. He ran an appeal through Women's Weekly, and at the time he didn't quite realise the awesome power of that magazine.

He received over 2000 replies and 500 of those came in the first two days. It wasn't so much the mothers, but the grandmothers, who were the most predatory. "They telephoned me at all hours of the day and night," he said. "I had applications from Alice Springs, Darwin and even way out in the bush."

Finally he chose Susannah Fowle, 18, who comes from Toorak, and who has just completed her schooling at St Catherine's (Anglican) Girls' School, Toorak. Susannah plays the piano and could possibly pass for a myself-when-young Henry Handel Richardson.

She is a vivacious, striking-looking girl and she is a marvellous contrast to Sheila Helpmann, sister to Sir Robert, who plays the headmistress or "Lady Superintendent" of the school. "I am the ultimate in old dragons," said Miss Helpmann. She is dressed formidably in black Queen Victoria style with a cameo at her neck and keeps her mouth pursed in a superb expression of profound distaste. Batman was almost too terrified to talk to her, but it was a stupid fear. She was charming.

One pointed out that in our office style book we were told firmly to spell Sir Robert with two "n's" and Miss Helpmann with one. "That's true," she said. "I tried to hang back with one 'n', but it all became too big for me, so I decided to tag along with the big star. Now I'm a 'two-n' Helpmann too."

Sadly for Bruce Beresford, the grand old PLC which used to be in Victoria Parade was wrecked a decade ago and now the school is glistening and modern.

Last week the crew shifted to Illawarra, another Victorian piece of land-boomer gargantua. They were there for the staircase. Laura had just arrived to start school, bubbling, enthusiastic, and she was in the process of being cut down by the terrifying Presbyterian pair, Humphries and Helpmann.

For example we had the scene:

Mrs Gurley (Sheila Helpmann): "Young ladies do not pass impertinent comment. Nor do young ladies desecrate the sitting room with music fit only for cheap entertainment."

Laura: "But I wasn't impertinent I thought..."

Mrs Gurley: "How dare you interrupt me. The first thing, miss, for you to do, will be to take a course of lessons in manners. Your present manners may have done well enough in the unfortunate position in which you belonged. They will not do here in the company of your betters. You will stand for one hour before the honor board."

So Laura stands. Mrs Gurley also informs her of the school rules. She is allowed only one book in her possession.

Yes, it's a good contrast to Bazza.
