On the money, but very seldom in it

3 min read.

First published 29 Jan 1977

A review of Ward McNally's biography of Charles Kingsford-Smith, finding it a warmly admiring portrait but too eager to defend its subject — the aviator who was given stingy treatment by both governments and the public despite his heroic achievements, and whose career was largely a series of frustrations.

IN HIS book Knockers, Keith Dunstan cited the treatment of Charles Kingsford-Smith as a baffling example of the Australian propensity to put down the famous. Everyone now accepts Kingsford-Smith's eminence in aviation history. Yet much opprobrium came his way, and his career was largely a series of frustrations. Ward McNally's book on him raises the question why.

The main source of hostility to Kingsford-Smith was the "Coffee Royal" affair in 1929. He and Charles Ulm made a forced landing at a remote spot in north-west Australia, to which they attached that name. During the search for them two airmen were stranded and died of thirst. Sydney newspapers charged that Kingsford-Smith and Ulm had pretended to get lost to attract publicity, and the public was remarkably ready to believe it.

When they returned to Sydney, crowds at the airfield shouted "fakers" and "cheats." This was less than a year after they had been given a big welcome on making the first-ever flight across the Pacific Ocean. An inquiry was held, and two witnesses said Ulm had spoken to them of the possibility of pretending to be lost. Nevertheless the inquiry found there was "absolutely nothing to suggest a hoax."

The idea that Kingsford-Smith would risk wrecking his plane in remote, dangerous country was improbable in itself. Sir Gordon Taylor, who made pioneer flights with him, never believed it. Yet in the public mind some of the mud stuck.

Kingsford-Smith also aroused mistrust in government circles. The British did not give him the first contract for England-Australia airmail, which went to Hudson Fysh of Qantas. The Australian Government treated him in a conspicuously stingy manner.

Partly he was a victim of the depression. The airline industry had scarcely begun, and it was hard for pilots, however good, to make a living. When he was knighted in 1932 Kingsford-Smith was giving joyrides for 10 shillings a time. Three years later, on a record-chasing flight, he died in the Bay of Bengal.

Ward McNally's book gives a warmly admiring portrait. It would be more persuasive if the author were less keen to defend his subject. Kingsford-Smith was rumored to be a Don Juan; McNally makes him improbably chaste. Kingsford-Smith began as a World War I fighter pilot, and he was what they call a fighter pilot type. In the early 1920s he was an adventurer, hanging upside down from aircraft wings for Hollywood movies, getting into fights in outback West Australian pubs. He tried hard later to make his way in the air transport business, but it is doubtful whether he would ever have fitted an establishment role.

McNally's biography contains worn phrases — planes wing their way, hunger gnaws, fate takes a hand. Yet it does convey that Kingsford-Smith, even if he had a larrikin streak, was a man in the heroic mould who received strangely grudging treatment. There is still room for more objective elucidation of the discomforting story.

The Man on the Twenty Dollar Note: Sir Charles Kingsford-Smith, by Ward McNally. Rigby, $7.95.
