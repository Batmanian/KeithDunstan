Stagflation is NOT blowing up deer

4 min read.

First published 23 Jul 1977

On the eve of another budget, with 38 percent of economists optimistic, 28 percent predicting ruin and 34 percent waffling, Keith Dunstan offers a seminar in economic vocabulary — spiralling inflation, stagflation, buoyant or rocky outlooks, haemorrhaging reserves, and the vital concept of "viable" — so readers can hold their own at dinner parties.

WE ARE ALMOST on the eve of another budget and I'm worried. Nobody seems to understand economics any more. Our good friends Hamer, Dunstan et al are calling for a lowering of taxes and interest rates as the only possible way to get the economy moving again. Messrs Fraser, Lynch and Co give us reverse information. The economy is balanced on a razor's edge (a new phrase they keep bringing out). Give us one more cent, and away inflation will gallop again.

Almost every week we get those solemn prognostations on the economy from the great banks, from the business efficiency houses and, of course, the gum economics professors. In our office we have a file labelled "The Economy." It contains all the newspaper clippings on the subject for the past six months. It stands about as high as a parking meter.

On your behalf, I have sweated through the lot. Forty percent of our experts think the economy is improving and we are likely to survive. Twenty-eight percent think we are going to ruin and 34 percent tend to waffle on brilliantly and you could put them down as having no opinion at all.

My belief is that none of them know, least of all Phillip, Mal, Gough, Bill and Bob. The only thing to do is to work it out for yourself. You, too, can be an economist, particularly if you have the right words.

As I went through that file I made notes of words which might be useful to me at dinner parties and bar room discussions. So would you get out your pen and paper, and we will move straight into the Batman Seminar.

It is vital for a start that you understand inflation. You have spiralling inflation, galloping inflation and snowballing inflation. You hedge against inflation and you always have a common goal for beating it. When you have a sophisticated audience and you are talking about its spiral galloping rate, then it sounds awfully good if you can work in "single digit" or "double digit" inflation.

And a real beauty to slip into your conversation is stagflation. I'm not too sure what it means myself, but if anyone asks you can say your inflation is galloping with a crook economy.

Oh, you don't think that's impressive enough. All right, shoot 'em some real economic meaningless gobbledegook:

"Yes the explanation is simple. An inadequate economic growth rate coupled with a relatively tight monetary stance leads to an ad hoc stagflation situation."

Now the economy doesn't go up or down. You must realise that we have economic upturns and downturns. On the other hand the economy can be buoyant, stagnant, gloomy and it is quite all right to refer to the bumpy or rocky road ahead.

Growth? Well, you can have real growth, slow growth, corporate growth or excessive growth and when it comes to recovery that is something which is "within our grasp," "slow," "tentative" or "may be in the offing."

Unemployment? That is usually chronic, and it can even be chronically high. Factors? You always have contributory factors. Signs? You have hopeful signs. Indexation? Always guidelines. Fiscal stimulus? Always a percent.

Thinking tends to be wishful, forecasts usually are gloomy, outlooks mostly are pessimistic, failure is invariably dismal. Goals are tough and reality is unpleasant.

Reserves tend to be depleted, but just lately in some of the financial papers I notice there has been a tendency to have them haemorrhaging.

Hopes can be high, but also they can be dampened. And attitudes are nearly always negative or positive. Attacks have to be blistering.

Never talk about "sooner" or "later." It is always "in the long-term" or "the short-term." You can get structural shake-outs or a deep malaise in the structure. Tariffs usually have a wall, and please don't gabble on about a buying spree. Always say a "resurgency in consumer spending."

And please try to use a little feeling about the way you refer to money. Talk about the burgeoning yen, even the propped-up pound, and the ailing or uneasy dollar.

I like "viable." It is very nearly at the top of the charts in all economic dialogue coming out of Canberra. So use it whenever you can. It is one of the growth words.

That's enough for the first session of our seminar. In our next lesson I will show you how to acquire a correct monetary stance, how to adopt a low profile and new methods for halting profit drift. Try to learn six new words every day.
