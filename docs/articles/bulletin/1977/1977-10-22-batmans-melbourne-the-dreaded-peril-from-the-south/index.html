The dreaded peril from the south

5 min read.

First published 22 Oct 1977

Premier Bjelke-Petersen's warning against Victoria's industrial disease prompts Keith Dunstan to defend his state's close ties with Queensland, Myer, Coles, Carlton Draught and 80 percent of Brisbane business houses, while Premier Hamer's delicate state of emergency during the spring racing carnival leads to a defence consultation revealing that all the Leopard tanks are at Puckapunyal, the paratroopers are in Western Australia, and the best available are 150 Cessnas at Moorabbin.

HERE in Victoria we are just a little bit hurt by the remarks of the Queensland Premier, Mr Bjelke-Petersen, that he did not want his State to become infected by the Victorian disease. He is referring, of course, to the efforts of some our stalwarts, Messrs Halfpenny and Gallagher, who are doing so much to boost our economy.

Mr Bjelke-Petersen has offended us, because Victoria always has had such a close relationship with Queensland. We love that State so much we have bought almost 80 percent of it. We have done our best to give them stores like Myers, Coles and Woolworths, lovely beer like Carlton, and it is generally agreed that four out of five business houses in Brisbane are owned in Melbourne.

All the time, or at least once a week, we loan them our very best directors for board meetings. Would we do that for someone who wasn't our friend? What's more, we have created our own little sub-colony up there, the Gold Coast. We have loaned them straight Victorian Bruce Small to preside over it, and we send great Victorian chaps like Phillip Lynch to stay there in their nice penthouse apartments.

One of the most fascinating operations is the annual migration. Many people believe it is a phenomenon in nature, the equal of the annual flight of birds from Siberia to the islands of Bass Strait. Late April every year the Jaguars, the Mercedes, BMWs and Rolls-Royces turn out in volumes from Georges and Lansell Roads, Heyington Place and Irving Road, then head north along the Hume Highway to the sun. Usually they go in one splendid flight, landing in their very own garages or nests at Surfers Paradise.

Now they have reached the end of the season and the flight is returning to its original habitat in Toorak. Don't you agree that Mr Bjelke-Petersen should be kinder to these very lovable people?

However, let us examine this disease. Maintenance workers with the State Electricity Commission stopped work for several months in the Latrobe Valley and building workers in the city stopped building their lofty buildings. Then, several weeks ago, the Premier, Mr Hamer, declared a state of emergency.

It wasn't entirely convenient for us to have a state of emergency at this time. After all, we were very busy playing two football grand finals and the most serious crisis that hit us was the possibility that there might not be enough electricity to provide power for the direct telecast of the grand final replay. But we survived that one and fortunately the televised replay went on live in all its color glory.

Another factor which has made a state of emergency inconvenient is that it has taken place right in the middle of the spring racing carnival. It is very difficult to cope with such matters when you are trying to concentrate on form for the Caulfield Cup.

I do believe that our Premier was sensitive to this. He rushed the bill through parliament to declare the state of emergency, but he didn't want to disturb anybody at such a delicate time. I mean every day he put off the evil hour when he might have to hurt somebody, like imposing a fine, or sending anybody to jail, or ordering somebody to work.

The fear, of course, is this. By so doing he might make the disease worse. Many union leaders were almost queueing in their desire to go to jail. Your correspondent tried to speculate on what Mr Suharto, Mr Brezhnev or one of our famous rulers in the Middle East would have done in such a situation. Obviously a crushing display of might would have been called for — they would have sent a parade of tanks down straight down Swanston Street.

All right, I did call on a defence expert and the conversation went like this:

"What about a marvellous show of Leopard tanks driving from the Carlton brewery right through to the Shrine?"

"It'd be a frightfully awkward operation. All the tanks are up at Puckapunyal. You couldn't bring them down the Hume Highway and you'd have to provide great transporters. Then look what they'd do to the tram tracks. It's just not on."

"Let's have paratroopers then, a terrific drop of commandos out of the sky into the Domain all round General Blamey's statue."

"No sir. They're all in Western Australia. Too far off."

"Aw, what have you got? There are a few F-111s and already we've just lost another. Dicey! And they're all up at Amberley, Queensland. I don't think Mr Bjelke-Petersen would be too keen on that."

"I tell you what, we might be able to do you a Hercules or two, or there would be no trouble in getting you half a dozen of those Victor trainers. What's more, there are at least 150 Cessnas out at Moorabbin."

It was at this stage that I pulled out my form guide for the Caulfield Cup.

Actually, it has been the best state of emergency we have ever had. The weather for October has just been superb.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The dreaded peril from the south, The Bulletin, 22 October 1977. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
