Art on a pastoral binge: wine, food, and fantasy

6 min read.

First published 06 May 1967

A tour of the gallery movement sweeping Victoria's provincial towns, from the Hamilton Shaw Collection to Ballarat's hotbed of Lindsays and the new Mildura Arts Centre, where the third Mildura Prize for Sculpture drew a washing-machine agitator with a Ford Thunderbird tail-light and a taxi driver who reckoned the winner was two giraffes mating.

The gallery movement is now utterly rampant in Victoria. In the old days the symbol of success was an atrociously large city hall done in the style of the Italian renaissance. City halls are now out and you don't rate unless you have a noble art gallery with a few Bunnys, a couple of Tom Roberts, a Dobell, and a Nolan, at the very least.

And to be fair, Eric Westbrook, the National Gallery director, and the Bolte Government are very good to this movement. They get their buildings subsidised on a dollar-for-dollar basis plus annual subsidies of around $2500 a year, and the chances are that all this will be doubled next year.

So now there are ten excellent provincial galleries in Victoria and four of these have full-time directors.

Perhaps the set-up at Hamilton is the most splendid. They have an $800,000 gallery and the $600,000 Shaw Collection. A grazier, Herbert B. Shaw, died in 1957 and left this marvellous collection, which ranged from Roman glass and Grecian ceramics to silver. Sadly, though, nothing has been spent on discovering what everything is.

Ballarat has been rebuilding its gallery and will soon reopen. They have 300 Lindsay items, some really splendid Norman Lindsay pictures, and a room from the old family home at Creswick donated by Mary Lindsay. You might say, quite accurately, the Ballarat gallery is a hotbed of Lindsays.

The Benalla collection is loaded with Frank Brangwyns and William Orpens. While one can go along with Brangwyns and Orpens a little of the time, they have a devil of a lot of them. Indeed, the Mildura gallery is sometimes called The Orpenage.

Mildura is a classic example of the new prestige battle. There was a wrangle in council over whether the city should have a city hall or a new art gallery, and by only a narrow majority, mercifully, the gallery won. So Mildura, now, must be one of the few cities anywhere that has no city hall, but a really first-class art gallery. To be precise, it is the Mildura Arts Centre and it was opened on November 12 last year, and was last week the locale for the third Mildura Prize for Sculpture, won by Sydney sculptor Herbert Flugelman, whose studio and art collection were burnt out last May.

Your correspondent was present at what the cognoscenti said was the biggest event in sculpture in the country. The city fathers and the art director, Tom McCullough, talked the Union Steamship Co. of New Zealand into giving a prize of $1000, and then persuaded other firms, McGlashens, to transport free all the sculpture from the capital cities, and, by heavens, they found an ideal site for a service station, and turned it into a sculpture garden instead.

And the roll-up was magnificent. One lady barrister said: "Isn't it nice! Anybody who is anybody is here. It's just like the Adelaide Festival."

A group of fun-loving local growers called "the witchetty grub tribe" even took Rudy Komon on a witchetty grub hunt. Rudy became a fan and his eyes shone. He said these would go well with a light, subtle, Rhine Riesling. That's the guts of the Aboriginal problem, the Aboriginals have not been given their witchetty grubs and Rhine riesling.

Your correspondent made a point of collecting unforgettable quotes. He was aware he was in a society so heady with art. At a formal dinner he heard: "Did you see the girl over there in the Pre-Raphaelite gown?"

"No, I didn't. I can't stand anything pre-Raphaelite."

Then there was the art critic who gazed off down the Murray through the red gums and said: "Ahh, don't you love it..."

Mystified local citizen: "Hey what's this Fred Williams business?"

Actually the citizens of Mildura were madly enthusiastic. They thronged through the gallery in vast numbers on the Sunday that James Mollison, director of the Ballarat gallery, said: "It's wonderful. Imagine! People actually queuing to see sculpture."

Yet the mystification did run deep. The city council bought two pieces for $600 and one councillor said: "Turn it up. Give me a welding kit and I'll do a better job for ten bob." The taxi driver who took us to the exhibition said of Herbert Flugelman's 'Equestrian,' 8ft. high in cast iron: "There y'are mate. What is it? I reckon it's two giraffes mating."

There were three judges, Tom McCullough, Laurie Thomas, and Dr. Donald Brook. Dr. Brook, in announcing the prizes, told of the dilemma of the modern judge. He said: "There are two very different types, between which it is difficult to make comparison. One is the type of sculpture of the renaissance, humanist, expressive. You can see into the soul of the artist. The other is inexpressive. It doesn't tell you about itself. It doesn't tell you about the artist's soul. You look at it. It's just there."

He pointed over to the riverbank to a mountainous piece of machinery: "It's just there, like Mr. Chaffey's marvellous pumping machine. You can appreciate its beauty. Actually it should be entered in the next competition. If it were, we would have to consider it seriously."

One nodded one's head and bet that he would, too. The prizewinner was "Phoenix II," by Michael Kitching, of Sydney. It consisted of a washing-machine agitator with a Ford Thunderbird type tail-light in the middle, plus a plastic umbilical cord, some children's play balls all surrounded with a plumage of pink perspex.

Another prizewinner was "Slotzyman," by a New Zealander from Auckland. They looked like folded engines, in love.

One entry, which I'm told just got by the sifting-out judges, was just four bits of 2 by 1 crudely nailed together. It was called "One For Paul" and courageously insured for $130.

One Saturday night Mildara Wines threw a marvellous party for all at the Merbein winery. There was turkey, smoked ham, chicken washed down by lovely white, opal, and Mildara cabernet-shiraz '64. After that there was the Mildura Arts Theatre. Not everybody made it to the plays. There was a gathering of art enthusiasts at the Grand Hotel. Late in the evening a group took offence at the very post-garde prints that adorn the walls of the Grand, and turned them in. The Grand was rather offended, but the word is that the culprits will replace them with something more avant.
