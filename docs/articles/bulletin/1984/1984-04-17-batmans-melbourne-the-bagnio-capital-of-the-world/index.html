The bagnio capital of the world

6 min read.

First published 17 Apr 1984

Victoria's Minister for Planning announces the decriminalisation of prostitution in massage parlours, prompting Keith Dunstan to survey Melbourne's long history with the "social evil" — from 597 fallen women in 1885, to the Duke of Edinburgh's visit to Sarah Fraser's bagnio in Stephen Street in 1867, to the 190 parlours now serving 60,000 to 70,000 clients a week.

OUR MINISTER for Planning and the Environment, Evan Walker, has announced that Victoria will decriminalise prostitution in massage parlors, come the middle of the year. As a veteran student of wowserdom, I will watch his progress with devoted interest.

In the old days, "prostitution" was a word so awful that we couldn't even mouth it out loud. Official parliamentary debates referred only to the "social evil" and prostitutes tended to be "fallen women."

In 1885, according to the Government Year Book, we had 597 prostitutes in a population of 282,947 but church newspaper the Spectator said this was nonsense — that we had more than 2000 fallen women — and English evangelist Henry Varley said that he had never seen a worse city for prostitutes than Melbourne in his 40 years' experience touring the world.

Of course, we have waxed and waned. It was just after 1970 that we first discovered the massage parlor and Chief Secretary John Rossiter actually announced in 1975 that massage parlors were being used for prostitution. This was interesting because it did appear that Melbourne had an extraordinary backache problem. A check revealed that the number of parlors had grown from 25 in 1973 to 116 by 1975.

Fascinating, too, was how the name "massage parlor" slipped into the language without much comment. As far as massage was concerned, only one portion of the anatomy was really involved. I looked up "parlor" in the Oxford dictionary and it reported (a): "An apartment in a monastery for conversation with persons outside" and (b): "The ordinary sitting room in a private house." Finally (c) there was "parlor car" — "a luxurious railway carriage." Immediately one could see what a boost parlor cars would become on our rather dull interstate travel.

The establishments always have had wonderful names — such as Bubbles, Caesar's Retreat, Cloud 9, Playful Pussycats and Peninsular Poppets. In the mid-70s we had one called Upstairs, Downstairs. I often wondered about that place. Did the parlor maids dress in Edwardian gear? Was there a massage girl called Rose? Was there a particularly shy little miss called Ruby? Was there a stern Mrs Bridges as madam? Or could you go upstairs and have a bubble bath and rub with Lady Bellamy.

Of course, all this was terribly tough on anyone who actually was a masseuse. They tried calling themselves "tactile therapists" but it wasn't easy. I remember one lady in Toorak reporting to The Age that she was from a very good family, had gone to a very good school, had done a four-year massage course and set herself up in business. She lost 90 percent of her friends. Not only did she drop down the social order, she went right out of it. One old mate explained that she didn't mind if the woman had become a prostitute, she still loved her. It was tough convincing even her parents that she only rubbed backs.

Actually, I would be frightened to go into a massage parlor because they are so technical. So many things are offered. I mean, you have just a "relief massage." That apparently is masturbation. "Turkish massage" is doing it under the shower. "French" is oral and "Spanish" is performing between the breasts of the parloress. "Disciplinary massage" means being whipped.

For nigh on 20 years, governments have huffed but done nothing about it. The police have been virtually powerless; they have said that they can't even enter a so-called parlor without permission. Chief Secretary Vance Dickie said in 1976 that the government would tighten regulations within months so that massage parlors could operate only in commercial districts. Nothing happened.

Brian Dixon, Minister for Youth, Sport and Recreation, released in 1980 a nine-page report which urged legalisation of massage parlors. There was even the bold statement that they should be called brothels. Ah, an ugly term.

So, here we go again. We are told that, under the new guidelines, massage parlors will be banned from residential areas but — with local government permits — allowed to operate in office, business and industrial zones.

Parlors are to be banned from within 40 metres of kindergartens, primary or secondary schools, scout or girl guide halls. Ah, but there will be no requirements to keep parlors away from churches or hospitals.

To define a residential area is difficult at any time but it is a fair guess that Melbourne has at least 190 massage parlors. They employ 2000 females and maybe another 100 not so easily defined and they have a clientele of 60,000 to 70,000 a week. They are spread through the suburbs almost as skilfully as milk bars but Melbourne, St Kilda, Richmond, South Melbourne, Caulfield, Collingwood, Fitzroy, Prahran and Footscray are strong rubbing areas.

Complaints have begun rolling in already about the new move. The councils of South Melbourne and St Kilda have said that they will not grant permits. So, you see, good citizens are still terribly sensitive about the social evil.

Back in the 1860s and 1870s, if you wanted a bit of high-class social evil, you went to Stephen Street and later to either Lonsdale Street or Little Lonsdale Street. Indeed, when Alfred, Duke of Edinburgh, visited as a 23-year-old in 1867, chief commissioner of police Mr Standish took him to Sarah Fraser's bagnio (early name for massage parlor) in Stephen Street.

When we put up the Exhibition building in 1880 we changed the name of Stephen Street to Exhibition Street to give it more tone. But, don't you see, if the councils really don't want the massage parlors, why not — in co-operation with the National Trust — revive Stephen Street? We could re-create this piece of history just as we did with China Town in Little Bourke Street.

We could restore Sarah Fraser "under regal patronage," bring back the famous Madame Brussells or the bagnio of Annie Wilson where the parliamentary mace is said to have disappeared back on the fateful night of October 8, 1891. We could even have missing mace re-enactment ceremonies.

We have something to live up to here — that's if we are really the bagnio capital of the world.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The bagnio capital of the world, The Bulletin, 17 April 1984. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
