The battle of the MCG

6 min read.

First published 21 Feb 1984

The Melbourne Cricket Club faces a deadly battle as the VFL demands Grand Final rights at the MCG, forcing MCC members from their stand, while the government drives reforms on women's membership and threatens legislation, leaving members wondering what equity they have in their own ground.

MELBOURNE, you must understand, is a devoted and conservative club town. We have the oldest, most comfortable clubs in the country.

It is debatable which is the older — the Melbourne Club or the Melbourne Cricket Club — but both came into being when the old town was little more than three years old.

Our clubs like to think that they are utterly private, like your mother's boudoir. The idea of allowing in any females was one of the most severe cultural shocks.

The Victoria Racing Club at Flemington had an ingenious idea for sexual apartheid. It had a line which ran right through the members' stand: males on one side, females on the other. If a male wanted to cross and indulge in a little female fraternisation, okay. But females couldn't do it in reverse. Females didn't understand the serious business of betting, anyway, and the male members had their private bookies.

Our Premier, John Cain, thought this unfair. The VRC was on public land and he forced it to do away with sexual apartheid.

Next came the Melbourne Cricket Club. We had our own members' stand, the secure place where females were never allowed and another place where we were social — ladies could come in on their own or we could have mixed company, a similar deal to the VRC. We were allowed the privilege of a vote and we voted to have the women into the club.

The vote didn't mean anything, really. The message from Cain was "Put your house in order or else." Back in the old days, before 1936, we used to get two ladies' tickets "free." You could take your own ladies or be a good fellow handing them round at the office. The first cutback came in 1936. New members received only one ladies' ticket, the old fellows still received two. Now, under the new regime, you get none. Your lady can become a member in her own right, provided you pay an extra $30.

It has been interesting. Women have been using the sacred confines of the Long Room, sitting in the seats which some members had occupied week in and week out for 30 years.

The new secretary, Dr John Lill, said that he actually welcomed the change. He believes that the female injection into maledom is about 10 percent.

Ah, but another government move is much more serious and some committee members believe the 126-year-old club these past few months has fielded terribly close to extinction.

The issue has been the deadly battle over where the Victorian Football League plays its Grand Final. The VFL, undoubtedly jealous of being in the hands of a cricket club, built its own ground at Waverley with the object of making it an all Australian Rules temple and home of the religion's greatest event — 1984 was to be the year.

The VFL, being a club, thought conceivably that it might have control over its own affairs. It wanted to play on Sundays but successive governments, including Cain's Labor Government, said "No." The VFA already played on Sundays; the VFL would ruin gates for its suffering "cousin."

Next, Cain ordered the VFL to have the Grand Final at the MCG on the grounds that the MCG was the greatest stadium in the land and it was preposterous to have the game anywhere else. The VFL dug in its footy boots and Cain threatened to legislate to have the game at the MCG for the next 10 years. The legislation could come up as soon as parliament re-opens.

So here was the situation: The MCG was being leased to the VFL for the Grand Final. Waverley members would get in for nothing. MCC members would pay and, at the same time, give up their members' stand.

Nobody was happy. The VFL believed it could make more money at Waverley and was being ordered into the MCG.

So it came to the point: What rights does a member have? Who owns the Melbourne Cricket Ground? Let's put a replacement price on it — $300 million?

The Victorian parliament reserved the land "permanently" to the club on December 10, 1861. The members paid for and built the whole place. The government did nothing except put in £100,000 out of £700,000 for the construction of the Southern Stand during the 1956 Olympic Games.

So, what equity do we have?

"None," according to Lill. "We are in the hands of the trustees as manager. They renew our lease from year to year."

The members took a vote. Were we prepared to give up our members' stand to the VFL on Grand Final day? The answer was a resounding "NO."

The trustees had to decide what to do next. If they forced the club to lease the MCG that would be the finish. The committee was prepared to go to the brink. Many an old member muttered: "After all, why should they push us around? We invented the damn game for them, didn't we? As a way to keep our members fit during the winter."

Then the trustees came up with what seemed to be a brilliant solution. Let the MCC members and the VFL and Waverley members share the members' and northern stands on Grand Final day. MCC members would pay and the VFL would kick in for its members. Lill thought that it would call for some nightmarish organisation on the day but was possible.

Then, on Wednesday night last week, the VFL agreed to play the Grand Final at the MCG under three conditions: that their members get in free and share the members' enclosure; that there will be an extra 4000 seats for the public; and that the VFL will not be disadvantaged. The VFL agreed only to play the Grand Final for 1984, so the wrangle will go on ad infinitum.

Many questions remain unanswered for the long-suffering MCC members. There are 19,600 of us and another 77,000 on the waiting list. If you want to get in, it is best to have your name put down in the pre-in vitro stage, while you are still an ovum.

Members believe the club has come a very long way in stable privileges. But will that continue if they have to pay heavily with little return?

There has been a controversy over lights for night cricket. Cost: $3.5 million. The local residents and the Melbourne City Council have fought it bitterly. Cain, tired of the controversy, said that the lights would go ahead but there is a restriction: only 10 nights a year and lights out by 10.30 pm. We pay.

If we are to accommodate the Grand Final, the MCG must be bigger, another tier on the southern stand, 15,000 more seats at a cost of $15 million. We pay.

There is unquestionable logic in everything Cain has done but there is a big message for every club. Their power has gone. Even clubs on their own land have only one weapon. If it walks out and says, "We have had enough," the trustees and government will have to run it on their own — a very, very expensive operation.

Let's hope they don't make it too costly to play in our own boudoir.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The battle of the MCG, The Bulletin, 21 February 1984. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
