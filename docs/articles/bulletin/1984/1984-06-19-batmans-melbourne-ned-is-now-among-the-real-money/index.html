Ned is now among the real money

6 min read.

First published 19 Jun 1984

At Glenrowan, insurance broker Bob Hempel has spent $500,000 — vastly more than Ned ever stole — creating a Disneyland-style Kelly show with computerised smoke, articulated robots, real rain down a window, a pub going up in flames on hidden gas jets, and Ned himself at the trap door with drums rolling. Business has been beautiful.

IT IS ONLY four years since we had the Ned Kelly centenary. There was such a gush of Kellyism then that I thought maybe his ghost had been exorcised. Not true. Sometimes I get the feeling that Ned Kelly seeps through the very pores of Australians; as early as 1942 a devoted historian produced a bibliography of all the major works on Ned Kelly and they came to 42. They have continued since then in a gushing stream — I have counted 21 full-length books on Kelly and his gang.

There have been three major movies, a rock opera, two plays, a ballet, a jazz suite, several novels, a ballad, innumerable songs, accounts in blank verse, re-enactments on television and he has inspired many great Australian painters and sculptors. No other Australian, whether he be writer, poet, sportsman, prime minister or explorer, has had even a 10th of this exposure.

The last great battle between Kelly and the police was at Glenrowan, on the main highway to Sydney, two and a half hours' drive from Melbourne. I can remember that, as recently as 15 years ago, if you went to Glenrowan and started to talk about Kelly and his brothers there would be a stiff silence. Oh, there were too many relatives still around.

Not any more. I called in there the other day and as you pass through, effigies of Mr Kelly are as common as TV aerials. There's a Ned Kelly wrecking yard and a Kelly food shop where they sell "a colonial pie floater," an awful traditional dish where you get a meat pie floating in soup. There's a Kelly Museum where they have built an exact replica of the house in which Kelly was born and a whole raft of things Kelly and his brothers are said to have worn, touched, looked at or been in.

However, the big event is the Tourist Centre. You couldn't pass through town and just not see it. It has life-size policemen crawling all over the roof and out front are two enormous figures in fibre-glass — Sgt Steele, who shot Kelly, and Ned himself.

Both are weighted with three tonnes of concrete so that devoted Kelly lovers will not steal them away. It's nothing to what is coming. A new Kelly totem is going up. He will rise four metres higher than the roof and be visible around the entire district, even from passing trains way over yonder. He will have moving hydraulic arms holding guns and his eyes will flash like headlamps.

A new man is in charge at the centre and as of this week he is giving us Kelly as he has never been seen before. Bob Hempel is an exuberant extrovert, an insurance broker from Wangaratta. Already he has spent $500,000, vastly more than Ned ever stole, and he's putting on quite a show.

Bob said he and his wife were visiting the Big Pineapple up in Nambour in Queensland. The Big Pineapple is, as the name says, a big pineapple about 20m high; you walk in and it tells the story of the pineapple industry. Bob said: "I read that the man there had bought the Big Pineapple for $500,000 and sold it for $7 million. 'Jeez, look,' I said, 'that's just a piece of fruit. Back home we've got the most exciting thing that ever happened in Australia, greater than Captain Cook'."

Now, many terrifying interviews with his bank manager later, Bob has something which might even unnerve Universal Studios or Disneyland. He actually gets his audience to participate in Kelly's last stand.

"The most brilliant show you will ever see in your life," roars Bob.

As Disneyland does, he has working, lifelike, articulated models. He called in the movie special-effects men, experts such as John Dowding, who worked on The Man From Snowy River and Phar Lap.

It all opens in a railway station in Melbourne, cobwebs everywhere, and a ghostly monk starts to narrate a story. Suddenly you are taken back to 1880, the clock on the wall spins backwards, real rain drenches down the window, lightning flashes, a fire comes on in the grate.

There's a shot and a scream! "My God! They've shot Aaron."

Oh yes, Aaron Sherritt, the police informer, has been murdered and a police detachment under Superintendent Hare sets out for Glenrowan.

The various characters, including Hare, stand up, gesticulate and start talking. This is craftily done. There's a projector, hidden in a box of ammunition, which lights Hare's face. You'd swear this was a man really talking; every grin and scowl is revealed.

A computer controls all the smoke that comes up through the floor, the sound, the movement of the robots. We go into the next room. Now we're trapped in Ann Jones' pub and there's Ned himself behind the bar, pontificating! "We are not the animals society has painted us. If anyone was ever driven to the limit of endurance it was me."

Quadraphonic sound assaults the ear: "COME ON OUT KELLY, WE'VE GOT YOU SURROUNDED."

Bob says that already they have installed special effects of bullets hitting the wall, plus puffs of smoke as if they are coming right inside. The computer will set off 100 of those.

And so it continues. We see the last shoot-out. A little more work needs to be done here — the fellers in their armor aren't actually moving. That will come later, according to Bob. Each time you put in a working robot, it costs $4000. Next, the pub is going up in flames, in the Universal Studios fashion, by courtesy of hidden gas jets. Nothing actually burns and it can burn up 12 sessions daily.

Now you enter the pub, with its smoke and smell of burned timber. One member of the Kelly Gang is dying and the charred bodies of two others are on the floor.

People adore to be alternately excited and frightened, says Bob. They get that all right. Keep walking and you go straight into the Melbourne Jail. Ned's there with a rope around his neck. Drums are rolling: "Ah, I suppose it has come to this."

KER-DUNK: the trap door opens and it's all over.

As we went out a woman said to her husband: "How about it? Give me a cup of Valium."

Business has been beautiful and Hempel has never stopped beaming. He said: "Kelly must have had 90,000 relatives. You wouldn't believe the number of people who come up and whisper — loud enough so everyone can hear — 'you know, Kelly was a relative of mine?' Although last week someone came up and said, 'Aaron Sherritt was a relative of mine,' that was a change."

Tickets are $5 for adults, $2.50 for children, with discounts for families, and while Bob sells tickets with one hand, he is flogging souvenirs with the other.

An extraordinary number of people want to wash their dishes with Mr Kelly, dry themselves after a shower with Mr Kelly, wear Mr Kelly on their chests, and even use Mr Kelly for their beer or have Kelly Biros in the shape of a .45 calibre gun.

I think it is a pity that it isn't possible to dry your dishes with Superintendent Hare or, at the very least, have a Sgt Steele bath towel. There must be some people on the side of the police.
