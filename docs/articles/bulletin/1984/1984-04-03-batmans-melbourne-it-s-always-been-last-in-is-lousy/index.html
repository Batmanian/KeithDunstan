It's always been 'last in is lousy'

5 min read.

First published 03 Apr 1984

Professor Geoffrey Blainey warns that Asian immigration is "well ahead of public opinion," prompting Keith Dunstan to survey Australia's long history of racism, from the Chinese, Irish, Italians and Turks to the treatment of Aborigines, and observe that it has always been the latest arrivals who cop it.

PROFESSOR Geoffrey Blainey, Dean of Arts at Melbourne University, is one of our most civilised Australians and he has a rare quality among historians — he writes so well, with a style. Maybe he was right when he said last week that we were letting in too many Vietnamese too quickly.

The professor had fears of racial agonies. Asian migration, said he, was "well ahead of public opinion." And he said that the tolerance extended to immigrants over the past 30 years could weaken or explode if the entry of Asians continued unabated.

Yes, but what a pity that he had to say it. Every bigot will be quoting Blainey. It's curious: it's always the latest arrivals who cop it. In my youth, it was the Greeks — the greasy Greeks and their fish and chip cafes. Then it was the Italians, "the Eye-tie wops" we called them, smelling of garlic. So they became respectable and then came the Turks. The despised Turks; they had to take the toughest, the least lovable jobs on the assembly line at General Motors.

Now it is the unfortunate Vietnamese, the boat people.

An awful reffo is someone who came 10 years after you. That was what the Eureka uprising was all about: it was the 1840s push showing their dislike for the gold immigrants of the 1850s.

Oh, the Irish were bad enough. How we trembled at their papish habits. There was even a suspicion that they sacrificed babies on the altar. If they were bad, the Chinese — the yellow Mongolian and Tartar hordes — were infinitely worse. The weeklies were full of cartoons about their uncleanliness, immorality, vice and leprosy and so we had the riots at Lambing Flat in 1861.

We didn't like the Jews much, either. The Bulletin, according to Manning Clark, had the joke on April 21, 1888: "Dwo tousand Shews vos oxpelled from Odessa."

Then came the agonies of Federation. But was the forging of the nation really filled with such feelings about freedom of man under the southern skies? No, one suspects that finally the disparate colonies came together only so that they could form some common immigration laws and get rid of the Kanakas from Queensland.

The Federal Labor Party platform had this tough little plank: "Total exclusion of colored and other undesirable races." Professor Clark quoted this verse from The Worker, Brisbane (1901):

Make a Chow cook a slave, Make black slaves of Niggers, Want to make white slave novel That Australia is to be...

And remember how tricky it was to get into Australia. Under section 3 of the Immigration Restriction Act of 1901, any person who failed to pass a dictation test of 50 words in a "prescribed language" could be declared a prohibited immigrant. Remember — back in the days of Arthur Calwell as Minister for Immigration — the wonder of, say, a colored gentleman from India, Africa or Indonesia being given a language test in, say, Romanian?

At Sydney's Rushcutters Bay in 1908 Hugh D. McIntosh staged the first world heavyweight title fight between a black man and a white, the Canadian Tommy Burns and massive Negro Jack Johnson. Confidence was widespread that the intelligence and skill of the white man would prevail.

More than 17,000 people crowded the arena. As The Bulletin put it, they hadn't come so much for a fight as to see a black contender beaten to his knees and counted out. Then Johnson thrashed Burns — slowly, mercilessly, precisely. The Reverend Henry Worrall, in the Bendigo Methodist church, proclaimed: "After all the boasting, bragging and betting, those 20,000 raving white Australians beheld their white champion beaten by the despised black man ... God grant that the defeat may not be a sullen and solemn prophesy that Australia is to be... vanquished by these dark-skinned people."

You can find a whole history of racism simply by studying that most fascinating of volumes, Sidney J. Baker's The Australian Language. The Chinese, for example, were Chinks, Chinkies, Chows, Canaries, Dinks, Johnnies and Dingbats. One called them "Dingbats" because normally a Chinaman was "as mad as a dingbat." Expressions taken for granted included "as awkward as a Chow on a bike," "mad as a Chinaman" and "fat as a Chinky's horse."

An Italian was a Sky, an Eye-tie, Steak, a Ding and a Dingbat.

A Jew was a Reff (short for "reffo") or Reff raff. Or he could be a refu-Jew. Poms always were "Poms" but not until the 70s were they refined into "Pommie bastards." Baker brought out the various theories that it was derived from pomegranate or POME (Prisoner Of Mother England).

However, the word Australians have used to describe almost any disagreeable foreign creature is "wog." You can have a Pom wog, a Yank wog, a Wop wog.

This possibly derives from an insect, loathsome disease or virus. Originally, when Australian soldiers went to northern Africa, they called the Arabs wogs. And so it spread to mean almost any Asian, any creature who didn't fit into Billy Hughes' idea of White Australia.

The racism goes even further with our Aborigines. Baker provides this list: Abo, Boong, Binghi, Black, Blacky, Black Skin, Murky, Dark Cloud, Unbleached Australian, Swatser, Kipper and Whistlecock. The last one refers to a crude operation to the penis.

For nigh on 200 years, we have destroyed the Aborigines by various means — poisoning, shooting, alcohol, neglect. Some say that there could have been 250,000 Aborigines in Australia in 1788. This was down to 60,000 by 1921 and back to 148,000 by 1981. One doesn't see too many of them in our top educational institutions, top political positions, on the Bench or even in our best clubs. I guess they had a problem: they let in too many white people too quickly and then found them too difficult to assimilate.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: It's always been 'last in is lousy', The Bulletin, 3 April 1984. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
