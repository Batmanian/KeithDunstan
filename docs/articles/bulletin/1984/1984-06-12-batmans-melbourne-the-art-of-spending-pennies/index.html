The art of spending pennies

5 min read.

First published 12 Jun 1984

The Rialto, William Pitt's magnificent 1891 Victorian Gothic building at the western end of Collins Street, is being recycled into the grand Menzies at the Rialto hotel, but its refurbishment stalls when the Historic Buildings Council insists on preserving the corrugated iron lavatory cubicles at the end of its galleries, arguing they tell the not-so-glorious story of "Smelbourne" in the 1880s, when night-soil men collected waste and the urinals drained into the Yarra.

IF YOU lived in Melbourne you would know the Rialto building. It is at the un-Paris end of Collins Street, the western end, and it is quite wonderful. The architects call it Victorian Gothic but it looks more like a cross between a chateau in the Loire Valley and a castle on the Rhine.

It was designed by a great romantic architect, William Pitt, and finished in 1891 — very dashing for its day. That end of Melbourne had been mostly warehouses with even a few breweries. The Rialto just got there at the dazzling peak of the land boom before the bank crash. It was recognition that this was a new world of high land values, time to build a fancy modern office building.

All the suites had telephones, you could talk from office to office through a tube system and a fascinating winch setup bore samples and display pieces to the upper floors. Even more impressive were the majestic hydraulic lifts which meant no wretched stair climbing.

The first moves to pull down the Rialto came in 1978. National Mutual wanted to build there and the insurance people came up against the full force of the National Trust and organisations such as the Historic Buildings Council (HBC). The insurers gave up the battle but in stepped St Martins Properties and the Grollo Group.

Last October they announced an astonishing proposition worth $330 million. They would build twin office towers, the taller would have 64 levels and would be the tallest office building in the Southern Hemisphere. Even better, it would be the second-tallest concrete building in the world.

Well, we were all pretty excited about this. Melbourne likes to think that it is Australia's financial capital. And, for a time, we did have Australia's tallest buildings. For example, in 1963 we had the Comalco building of 29 storeys but then Sydney put up its AMP building — quite deliberately we felt — to be 17.5 metres higher. Never again have we been able to wear the crown.

Now, the new Rialto is rising. It has reached more than 20 storeys already. I commend to you the glowing prose of the real estate people: "Now, at the western end of Collins Street... a tall, graceful structure is climbing toward the sky. Slim and glistening like sky-tinted crystal, it is the landmark Melbourne has been seeking. Its name is Rialto. Exciting in concept, sensitive in design. Rialto marries history with the future."

Ah! but here's the point: they didn't pull down the old Rialto; they decided to recycle it into a gorgeous vintage hotel. The old Rialto has a whole series of galleries on four levels with wrought iron balconies, a bit like San Quentin or Alcatraz. Next-door is the Winfield building, same style, completed 1893. They connected the two with a great glasshouse, creating a magnificent indoor space, an atrium. You must appreciate that atriums are all the go in the hotel business. There will be marble and stone paving below. Guests will take tea in the courtyard and, undoubtedly, a trio or an ensemble will play there.

We have been told that it will be called Menzies at the Rialto, in honor of Melbourne's late departed famous old pub. It is due to open this year.

All right, we thought that all the worries of the Rialto were over. But wait a minute.

At the end of each gallery on the south-eastern corner there was an enclosed section, a cubicle covered in corrugated iron with a couple of quite charming church-style windows.

Each section originally contained three porcelain urinals. The urinals have gone but the corrugated iron shelters are still there.

The owners applied to have the shelters removed but the council rejected that application on the grounds that they were of historic importance and their removal would ruin the period integrity of the Rialto.

This battle went to the Planning Appeals Board last week. In the left corner was the HBC, a government statutory body. In the right were the owners who expressed horror that their fancy five-star hotel would have 95-year-old lavatories at the end of its balconies.

Actually, the council's case was a first. It was fighting not on aesthetics, not on the beauty of the Rialto but on a pure matter of historic significance: Melbourne in the 1880s was going through a sewer crisis.

Here was a sophisticated, so-called modern city which was being called "Marvellous Melbourne" but it had no underground sewerage system. Sensitive noses were more likely to call it "Smelbourne" and the cans of human refuse were collected regularly by a gentleman who had the sweet, euphemistic title night-soil man.

Melbourne's Board of Works did not come into being until 1891 and buildings were not connected to sewer pipes until 1897.

The urinals at the Rialto deposited their waste directly into the gutter and, like everything else horrid, eventually it emptied into the aromatic Yarra River.

So, the council contended, if you set to depict the glories of affluent 19th century Melbourne, you should at the same time tell the not-so-glorious side.

The Grollo people complained that they were spending millions on creating something beautiful, that their guests would not want to be confronted with ghastly old lavatories.

The council said that this was history; if all were handled correctly, they would be fascinated. As soon as they arrived, they would want to know all about those enclosures at the end of the balconies.
