Let's move Sunday to Saturday

5 min read.

First published 05 Jun 1984

Melbourne's cherished sepulchral Sunday is being eroded by incremental liberalisations, Sunday hotel trading, soccer at the MCG, while hardware merchant Frank Penhalluriack wages a ten-year battle from South Caulfield, paying over $130,000 in fines. Keith Dunstan's new campaign: shift everything to Sunday and have absolutely nothing on Saturday.

THIS COLUMN has told before of the perfection of the boredom of the Melbourne Sunday. It has told also of the dangerous people who have tried to tamper with it and make it a common, vulgar Sunday as if we lived in New York, Paris, Sydney or wherever.

Way back in 1856 William Kelly, a gregarious Irishman, could not believe it when he came to Melbourne. He wrote: "I could almost fancy the echo of my footfall was reproving my trespass on the prevailing stillness. Even when I got into great Collins Street there was not a soul astir."

The astonishing John Norton gave a howl of pain in his Truth newspaper in 1906. He wrote: "It is neither a Scotch Sabbath nor a Jew's Sabbath, neither a Christian Sunday nor a white man's holiday, but a mixed up abortion of a thing ... Morning trains only carry folks to church, trams will not run until afternoon and there shall be no drinking in any hotel and no selling of any Sunday newspapers and no charge for admission to any public entertainment."

Seeking some release from all "the domination of monkeydom, humbug and folly," he cried: "How long, oh Lord, how long?"

Some of the monkeydom has been fixed. You can go to a movie on Sunday and get a drink on Sunday, provided you do it between extraordinary prescribed hours. But monkeydom, humbug and folly now concern trading hours and footy.

Successive governments, Liberal and Labor, because of union pressure have been terrified to use their judgment as to what we may buy at the weekend. Our weekend begins precisely at two minutes to noon on Saturday. Few places in the world do it so completely.

The laws on Sunday trading are very strange. If you want to buy a loaf of bread, an ice cream or a garden rake, that's OK. Garden accessories do not offend the Almighty, nor do things associated with the God automobile. But you are not allowed to buy a pair of trousers. It is wickedness to buy toys but you will get away with it if you go to a chemist, who seems to be able to sell anything.

You can buy books on Sunday. You can buy a ball hammer on Sunday because it is classified as a car accessory tool — but a claw hammer is out because that is a handyman item. A hacksaw may be sold on a Sunday but not a carpenter's saw or a nail. Hardware is terribly sinful, almost more sinful than drinking before noon.

But if you are really desperate you may be able to pick up a can of paint, a hammer or a coat hanger in a prescribed tourist area. They have different regulations. Even supermarkets are open in tourist areas.

For 10 years, Frank Penhalluriack, a hardware merchant at South Caulfield, has been waging a battle. He has kept his doors open on Saturday afternoons and Sundays. Whereas call girls and massage parlors have had little trouble with weekend trading, labor and industry department inspectors have called so often at the Penhalluriack shop that it is a wonder their payments for double time on Sundays haven't bankrupted the government.

Penhalluriack has tried everything. He has been on television almost as often as Bob Hawke. Since he started he has been fined more than $130,000 — some paid anonymously, some paid from proceeds of public auctions in his shop — and he has been to jail.

He has made dramatic Sunday visits to a hardware store in St Kilda. St Kilda is a tourist area but South Caulfield, which is practically next door, is not. Craftily, he put up signs outside his business to make it conform to the Sunday trading laws. It became a petrol shop, a retail plant nursery, a flower shop, a bookseller, an art and handicraft store and a boat, caravan, trailer and accessory shop. That didn't work. Once again he was arrested and hit for $4293 in fines.

The argument against him has been that if there is open go on Sundays, Coles, Myer, Safeway and New World will put the small trader out of business. The Shop Distributive and Allied Employees' Association has waged a continuing campaign against such a move. Penhalluriack says this is nonsense. He provides employment and does half his business at weekends. His case went to the Supreme Court. He lost and went to Pentridge Jail. However, he was out again soon. Joe Galani, managing director of the Saba furniture business, paid his fine. Galani said: "I have paid more than $250,000 in fines for weekend trading over the past year and strongly disagree with the court decision."

Meanwhile, for five years or more, the Victorian Football League has been fighting a piteous battle to get league matches on Sunday. The reason the Swans went to Sydney was to get Sunday football and let Sydney do our wickedness for us.

Premier John Cain consistently has rejected VFL pleas on the grounds that the Victorian Football Association and other junior leagues play on Sundays and the power of the VFL would put them out of business.

Recently Cain gave permission for four international soccer matches to be played on Sundays at the MCG, the first last weekend. They were just "one-off affairs," he said. This set the VFL alight. It has been hollering for Sunday football ever since.

VFL president Dr Allen Aylett said: "It is just unbelievable this sort of discrimination goes on from a supposedly non-discriminatory government."

Even more astonishing, there have been cries from the racing clubs. They want Sunday racing and Sunday observers foresee a situation in which we will have bookmakers giving us a seven-day gambling week.

Frankly, I'm disturbed. I always liked the sepulchral Melburnian Sunday. It was unique. Now that it is virtually lost I am campaigning to shift everything to Sunday — shopping, open supermarkets, footy, racing, the lot. The object is to have absolutely nothing on Saturday.

A sepulchral Saturday would be almost as nice.
