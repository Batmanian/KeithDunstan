Governors are on a good wicket

6 min read.

First published 06 Mar 1984

Keith Dunstan surveys Australian governors since World War II and finds the job goes overwhelmingly to generals and lawyers, suggesting it is time for imagination, and nominating ABC cricket commentator Alan McGilvray as the ideal candidate.

I HAVE ALWAYS wanted to be a governor. In Victoria, the job would be complete with tower, on the corner of one of the best damn parks in the entire world. There's a tennis court, a swimming pool, a chauffeur, a splendid chef and an opportunity to run beaut dinner parties at which you don't have to foot the bill. What's more, your wife doesn't have to cook or go to the supermarket.

Admittedly, there are drawbacks: you have to go to some pretty tiresome functions and have distinguished guests you could do without but, then, you don't have to get up early and the pay is jolly good. Alas, they wouldn't appoint a journalist or a writer in a fit.

You might have noticed that the latest man to get the job in Western Australia is a 60-year-old university product, Gordon Reid, Professor of Politics from the University of WA. He is supposed to be one of the country's leading authorities on the constitution and parliament.

At least it is a change and I'll watch him with great interest. In the old days, I used to get sent out to record the words of governors and governors-general and I yearned for one of them just occasionally to say something interesting. The best of them in the old days was General Sir Dallas Brooks. He could be amusing but from the others the words fell as cold and dull as frozen peas.

The present Governor of Victoria, a former judge named Holl, is at least a vigneron and I have heard that the wines served at Government House are excellent.

But what are your chances of cracking it as a governor?

I have been making a survey of Gs-G and Gs since World War II. It's not much point going back before that — the jobs were entirely part of the retirement plan for the British.

Since World War II, the Commonwealth has had three lawyers, five politicians, a field marshal and a royal. NSW has had an air marshal, a soldier-diplomat, a lieutenant general and a general. Victoria has had a rear admiral, a lawyer, a major general, a general and another major general. South Australia has gone for a lieutenant general, two clerical gentlemen — including an Aborigine — a scientist, a major general, a lieutenant general, an air vice marshal and a lieutenant general.

WA has had a rear admiral, an air marshal, a commodore, an air commodore, a major general and a lieutenant general. Queensland has tried a commodore, an air marshal, a lawyer, a soldier, a lieutenant general and a colonel. As for Tasmania, it has broken away with a diplomat, a lawyer, two lieutenant generals and three of the right people from the old country.

So the conclusion is obvious: if you want to be a governor, you go to Duntroon, behave impeccably and end up a general.

Vice-royalty has been set up as part of the superannuation scheme for the Australian army. We have had 18 military governors. We have had six lawyers and five politicians. Your chances are not so good with the other services. The air force has cracked it with five and the navy four.

I don't see any reason why these posts should be retirement pads for old lawyers and the de-toothed military. Not that I have anything against those professions; I'm just jealous. Other occupations should get a go.

It could be argued that special skills are required: the lawyer, for instance, needs to know when and how to dismiss a government.

The classic case is the dismissal of Gough Whitlam by Sir John Kerr. However, plenty of people would argue that Australia may have been better served had Sir John not possessed his legal skills and, reading various books on the subject, one says that he should have taken the advice of his prime minister and the other says he consulted his chief justice.

So, why not a governor who is a distinguished novelist or a playwright? Why not an engineer, a doctor, a bishop, a grazier, a fireman or a beaut wine maker from the Barossa?

Or could one suggest an actor?

The number one job in the United States is held by an actor. Back when he was campaigning, Ronald Reagan's critics thought it absurd that a former romantic lead in B movies should be running for such high office.

However, times have changed since the 19th century; Reagan's job is mainly communicating. All the decisions can be made by clever planners in the back rooms but few presidents have been as brilliant as Reagan at the warm smile, the firm handshake, the sincere and honest look — especially when he is telling 230 million Americans that he is cutting back on school lunches, reducing food-stamp benefits, cutting out free legal aid, abolishing the Department of Energy and firing up military spending, just so he can make a better life for all Americans.

He is good at interviews, good at repartee and even when he had been shot — when he was being carried into hospital with a bullet in a lung — he was capable of the marvellous line: "I think I'd rather be in Philadelphia."

The job of governor or G-G is almost entirely speech making and PR. We could have a specialist such as Laurie Kerr, chief of International Public Relations.

Whom else could we choose? We could please the women's libbers and give a job to Joan Sutherland. Joan and Richard Bonynge would be exciting occupants of GH. But they might charge too much for each performance.

Jack Thompson? No, Jack lives with pagan lovers. He might be too exotic for some.

Leonard Teale? He'd be terrific. Nobody is better at reciting Banjo Paterson or Adam Lindsay Gordon and, as an ex-TV cop, he would have a nice air of authority.

Reagan is an ex-disc jockey. Perhaps we should go for a radio man — Bert Newton? — or a sports commentator, Bill Collins?

Or — hang on! — why not Alan McGilvray, our favorite ABC cricket commentator?

He is dignified, he looks good, he is articulate and always he has that slightly melancholy look as if Australia is five wickets down for 82 but could do better in the second innings.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Governors are on a good wicket, The Bulletin, 6 March 1984. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
