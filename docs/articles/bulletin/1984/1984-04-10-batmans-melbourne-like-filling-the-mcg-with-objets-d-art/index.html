Like filling the MCG with objets d'art

8 min read.

First published 10 Apr 1984

Melbourne's Arts Centre, fifteen years in the making and finally opening in May 1984, gets Keith Dunstan's grand tour — from the underground theatres floating in primeval swamp mud, to Sidney Nolan's gift of 1350 Paradise Gardens paintings written on a plain sheet of paper, to the 75,000 floating brass baubles in the State Theatre ceiling.

FOR THE PAST DECADE, we have had a great battle of the arts. Sydney built its beautiful Opera House complete with soaring sails. Adelaide put up a very neat Festival Centre, a charming complex by the River Torrens, a proper little group of theatres. Brisbane fought back with a marvellous Art Gallery. They were a bit short of pictures to put in it but that will be rectified and beside it they are completing a splendid Concert Hall. Perth built an Entertainment Centre, the finest thing of its kind on the continent. As for Melbourne, we are building a wonderful thing, called the Arts Centre.

This darned Arts Centre has been building for more than 15 years and it will be finished in May. Hard to believe, really. As you may have heard, we look across our shoulders a lot at Sydney and I might venture to say that they look back at us. There wouldn't have been more than a million gags in Melbourne papers when they were building their Opera House. Oh, yes, Sydney built it out of lottery money and the price went up and up and up. Finally to about $120 million. Our National Art Gallery wasn't built that way at all and cost only $16 million.

The Opera House was subject to immense controversy over design but I thought, right there on the harbor, with those lofty sails shining in the sun, it was the most beautiful modern building in the world. No doubt about that. Ah, but here in Melbourne we noted that the stage was so small as to be almost impossible for opera and, incredibly, they had made no provision for car-parking.

All right, we had to do better. Beside our National Art Gallery we wanted to build our own opera house and the idea was pretty ambitious. There had to be a concert hall, a stage theatre suitable for opera which would seat 2000, a theatre for drama seating 850, a small experimental theatre, restaurants, shops, parking for thousands of cars; but here was the rub: we didn't want it to take anything away from lovely, tree-lined St Kilda Road — so all this had to be underground.

To say that they ran into trouble is an understatement. When they started to dig the big hole, the architects and engineers found a sort of primeval swamp. Sink a pile and it would go plop, disappear. Costs began to mount and mount and mount. They were very decent about it in Sydney. Sometimes they tried not to smile.

The result was that all these underground theatres, four of them, had to be built so that they literally floated in mud. That worked okay but, seeing everything was below deck, it was felt the complex needed a focal point — a tower, no less. The architect, Sir Roy Grounds, who died before all was completed, wanted a soaring spire sheathed in copper and illuminated by laser beams.

Well, the price of copper went off the graph; recession hit us all and the great soaring copper tower was never built. Instead we put up an open affair, rather like a spider's web erected high on a tower. Oh dear, the comments poured in. One gentleman described it as an enormous device for catching blowflies. Another critic said it resembled a gigantic inverted ice cream cone. A third dubbed it The Trifle Tower ... I liked that ... it was the Meccano Set, the Tower of Oroton and, of course, Germaine Greer remarked acidly: "It is the ultimate vulgarity only exceeded by the Sydney Opera House."

Grounds fought back with these words, almost his last comments: "With its anodised aluminium base, the spire looks like a ballerina with her skirts swirling. In fact, it all reminds me of a see-through negligee."

Well ... maybe. The Concert Hall opened about 15 months ago. The walls were lined with leather and the upper walls in the hall itself done in beautiful colors designed to represent the minerals of the great Australia Outback. There was one extraordinary story.

John Truscott, interior design consultant, was in the hall one day and a gentleman came in. "My name's Nolan; I'd like to make a donation." ... "Oh, yes," said Truscott. "Yes, I have these pictures, you see. I wanted a place to put them. About 1350 of them." Suddenly it all came together. This was the famous Sir Sidney Nolan, maybe Australia's greatest living artist. The series he was donating was a collection of matching works which had taken him years to paint. What were they worth? Heaven knows. But here was a man making a gift worth millions.

Well, it wasn't particularly easy. It was very late in the piece. Truscott had done all his interior design. He explained tactfully to Nolan, "You know, maybe it wouldn't work." "I think it will," said Nolan. And it did; not only did the paintings match the decor, the fit was almost uncanny.

Truscott, in some excitement, called in general manager George Fairfax: "Well, how are we going to go about this?"

"Simple," said Nolan. "Have you got a sheet of paper?" and he wrote, "I hereby give the collection of paintings, Paradise Gardens, to the Victorian Arts Centre in perpetuity," and it was signed Sid and Mary Nolan. No letterhead, nothing but an ordinary and now quite priceless sheet of paper.

So Paradise Gardens took its place in the Concert Hall, the perfect backdrop for the main concourse, foyer, whatever — the ultimate work of art upon which to gaze before taking one's seat.

Since then, work has gone on and on. We used to laugh at how much money our friends in Sydney spent. The bill to date for our Arts Centre is $232 million. It's hard to believe it will be finished in May. I must confess to a bit of home-town prejudice but I think it is the best arts-theatre complex that I have seen anywhere. It is certainly richer and has more to offer than either the Lincoln Centre in New York or the Music Centre in Los Angeles.

Let's move around it a bit. You can walk straight in from St Kilda Road or come straight out of the vast underground car park. If you come from St Kilda Road, the first thing you see is this gorgeous red carpet and a ceiling done entirely in gold leaf. Then straight in front is a mural which extends for three storeys.

This is a lovely thing by Hugh Oliveiro, depicting the theatrical arts. To gain a ceramic effect, he gave it 20 coats of enamel. I think even a Rolls-Royce gets only about 12.

You keep walking and you come to the Plaza. There's the sea of red carpet with the Victorian coat of arms in the centre and all around is black, reflecting glass. You go north up the escalators to the Concert Hall, over there down to the Experimental Theatre, down yonder to the Playhouse or through the concourse to the 2000-seat State Theatre.

Truscott's terminology is extraordinarily fruit-oriented. He says the Studio is in mulberry, the State Theatre is the color of raspberries and the Playhouse is in aubergine. Each has a bar. For example, there is the Rendezvous Bar, the color of good burgundy flecked with copper dust. On theatre nights it will stay open until 1 am.

It is hard to come to terms with the enormous space, the lavish use of Carrara marble, the glorious columns which have been hand-painted to give a marble finish, the red plush on the walls, the mirrors.

If you like statistics, the area underground is the equivalent of two-thirds the playing space of the Melbourne Cricket Ground or two American football grounds.

The jewel, of course, is the State Theatre. There are 75,000 floating brass baubles which make up the ceiling. When the light shines through them they look like a golden, sparkling waterfall. The seats remind me of those you find in a Boeing 747. Rather too comfortable, I feel, for one who has a dangerous tendency to go to sleep during opera.

You will remember I said that the Sydney Opera House had a stage so small that it made opera very difficult. Okay. Here in Victoria, obviously, they said: "We'll show 'em." This one ranks among the largest three mechanical stages in the world. I can't tell you how big it is. It goes on forever. According to Mike Hipkins, the project manager, it could accommodate a 115-square metre house. What's more, it is as lofty as a cathedral. They can move scenery in without dismantling anything. There's space for 115 flies. They can store above the stage all the scenery for three operas.

But the art works will make it unique. Everywhere you go there will be sculpture, murals, ceiling paintings, oil paintings. The best Australian artists have been commissioned: John Olsen, Tim Storrier, Charles Blackman, Roger Kemp, Arthur Boyd, just to mention a few. Jeffrey Smart, who now lives in Tuscany, has done a painting 10 metres long which depicts a goods train moving through the Australian countryside. Noel Turks has done another huge picture, titled Gloria Swanson's Last Picture Show. It depicts Gloria as a very white, scarlet-mouthed surrealist figure surrounded by an adoring audience, half human, half animal.

There's a great series of Aboriginal paintings and, of course, Nolan has provided another 504 of his Paradise Gardens series. One could go on and on. There's a restaurant, called the Vic, with Carrara marble and the most exciting hand-painted ceiling you ever saw.

There's a Country Visitors' Centre. Country people can go in, have a bite to eat, see what's on. The opening will be with Nabucco and the Playhouse with Zoe Caldwell in Medea. Fairfax can hardly believe it. He has been on the project for a fearsome number of years and must feel such as Gibbon must have had when he was completing The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire.

He said: "I'm getting a feeling of excitement now. I can smell the manilla rope, the timber, the canvas. My God, it's starting to smell like a theatre."
