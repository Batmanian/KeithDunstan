Australia's longest-running election

5 min read.

First published 22 May 1984

The Melbourne Anglican diocese has spent nearly a year trying to elect a new archbishop to replace the departed Archbishop Dann, with two board members themselves candidates, constant leaks splitting the board, and an open letter suggesting Alan Bond, his dedication to causes well known, his media image well managed, as a suitable alternative.

THERE IS a beautiful, measured progress about the way the diocese of Melbourne creeps toward finding an archbishop. In Sydney, the archbishop is elected at synod and it can be done in a matter of days. It normally takes three weeks to get a new pope.

The cardinals have to fly in from all around the world. But, once locked up, they normally find a new pontiff within a couple of days. When Brezhnev passed on, the USSR had a new leader for 274 million people within 48 hours.

Ah, but the diocese of Melbourne has had nigh on a year to find a new archbishop for its flock of 750,000 and even now we are not sure when he will arrive. Archbishop Dann gave notice on June 29, 1983, so that the electoral board could get things moving nicely before he departed on October 30. They didn't find a replacement. At the cathedral office, they say these things cannot be hurried and that it is not abnormal to take a year to find the right archbishop. The diocese is in the excellent hands of the administrator, Bishop James Grant, and it goes on as normal.

Regardless of calm at diocesan HQ, there has been much criticism out in the parishes and a feeling that the board should be locked up and not given another meal until it makes a decision.

The Anglican church needs inspired leadership as never before. Good man though he may be, would an administrator dare pre-empt the thoughts of an incoming archbishop? So the diocese waits and waits.

Melbourne elects its archbishop through a 12-person board — six clergy and six laity, including two women. It began discussions back on August 1 and the board had its problems from the start. Off and on some members were almost continuously overseas and then — would you believe — two of its top candidates, Grant and Canon Peter Hollingworth, were board members. Could they vote for themselves?

Leaks also have been a problem. Even the cabinet of the federal Labor Party could not leak with such holy efficiency. Early on, they appeared to be split between Grant and Hollingworth. Grant, 52, was a great administrator, a skilled historian but did he have the charisma — the drive — to electrify the church?

Hollingworth: 48, director of the Brotherhood of St Lawrence, a man with a huge conscience, well-known throughout the nation for care of the poor, but was he an administrator and did he really know what went on in the parishes?

So time went on and on. They looked at Bishop Oliver Heyward, 57, of Bendigo; Archbishop Keith Rayner, 53, of Adelaide; Bishop David Penman, 47, from the western region of the Melbourne diocese. There were even suggestions that they canvassed the possibilities of candidates among America's Episcopalian bishops, maybe from Boston or New York.

Hollingworth found his position impossible and resigned from the board. There has been a suggestion that Grant, increasingly uncomfortable, withdrew his candidature.

Right from the start there was enthusiasm from many quarters for Penman, brilliant, good-looking, a PhD from the University of Karachi, an ex-missionary, a New Zealander and former vicar of All Saints, Palmerston North. But did anyone know him? He was consecrated in Melbourne only in 1982 and — the real rub — he was "evangelical." He would become devoted to Sydney, they'd be delighted to have him. You have to appreciate that you have high church and low and various shades of clerical grey in between.

Here in Melbourne we consider that Sydney is the home of the low and the evangelicals. I've heard a cleric say that the church in Sydney is so low that they've had to remove the stumps. Yet, by tradition, the Melbourne diocese has been the cosmopolitan diocese, capable of absorbing all camps, so there would be no harm in Penman as archbishop as long as he preserved the ethos of the diocese.

According to one leak, the board divided down the centre: for and against Penman. The agony has been that the delay is brutally unfair to every candidate. The final choice will look like the only wombat they could find in the yard.

This year, criticism of the board has come into the open. The Rev John Bayton of Eastern Hill criticised the whole system as outdated.

He was quoted in the Melbourne Sun: "I'm not a stirrer but the people of Melbourne have an expectation that they should have someone as their leader whatever you think about the bumbling church."

There was even a letter in the Anglican newspaper, Church Scene, pointing out that since they seemed to be having great difficulty finding an archbishop among the clergy, why not Alan Bond: "His dedication to causes is well known. He has a jovial face, appears to pay proper attention to his media image, he has exalted connections."

The board must reach a decision by midnight on July 5 or the members have to resign their commission and so the matter would return to synod. However, the betting is that they will get there within the next fortnight and the winning candidate could be Penman.

Meanwhile one has to think of the archbishop's palace, Bishopscourt, up there in East Melbourne. It is a marvellous Victorian mansion, beautiful garden, restored about six years ago at a cost of $330,000.

If they decided not to have an archbishop, it would make one of the most rentable properties in Melbourne. If they decide not to have an archbishop, I do hope they find another delightful use for it.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: Australia's longest-running election, The Bulletin, 22 May 1984. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
