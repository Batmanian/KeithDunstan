How I learned to love my computer

6 min read.

First published 24 Apr 1984

After two months of living with a Tandy TRS80 personal computer, Keith Dunstan reports on his turbulent apprenticeship — the joy of silent 5am keystrokes, the terror of losing whole stories to the electronic void, the global-search miracle, and a 75,000-word spelling dictionary that is making Batman copy pure for the first time in 25 years.

FOR TWO MONTHS I have been living with a personal computer. It hasn't been easy. I have cursed it as if it were a miserable linesman being abused by John McEnroe. There have been times when I was almost prepared to kick in its green, superior, super-confident screen.

Ah, but then there have been times of bliss and triumph. We are living together now on reasonably amicable terms and the thought of going back to my German electric typewriter is just too awful to contemplate.

This is not my first experience with a word processor. For three years in Los Angeles I had a device designed for sending messages over the telephone. It was folksy in the American style. For example, when I was communicating with New York a message would come up on the screen: "Hi, this is your Reuters computer." I never felt like coming back saying: "Hi, this is your eager reporter in LA." Then when transmission was all done it would announce, "So long, that's all, folks."

Frankly, I find friendly computers unbearable. Our software at home is rather more formal. The computer at the office will wish you "Merry Christmas" and "Happy New Year," but most times it is cool and polite, using "please" and "thank you."

I wandered around computer shops for a month, searching. The first thing you learn is that prices are off the screen and money melts like floppy discs on a hot stove. The set up which would have been perfect for my needs came to $11,000.

Eventually I went to Tandy and bought a TRS80 model 4 and the appropriate software, plus printer, plus 15 months insurance, plus 12 hours of personal instruction and that came to just under $5000.

There were many reasons for buying the green-eyed monster. Number one, I am an unbearable lark, married to an owl. The only time my brain will function is about 5am. Any time after nine it is in deep decline. Can you imagine living with a Germanic typewriter which makes noises like a chainsaw at 5am?

The sound of the keys on a word processor is like the clicks of ivory playing mah jong. You can put everything on floppy disc, wait until morning tea time, push a button and then spew out the lot on a printer.

Putting it on floppy disc is the extra marvel. I mean, with double-density floppy discs you can store a book on two or three discs. The situation is coming where no longer will we send the publisher his two or three manuscripts, laboriously typed, weight half a tonne. Instead you will put your three discs in an envelope with the message: "Hi, this is your friendly word processor operator. That's all, folks."

Ah, but say the swine wants you to make some revisions; bits of Chapter Four ought to be in Chapter One and in Chapter Seven you omitted the sexual deviations of Colonel Carruthers. The Tandy has double disc drive — that is, you have two discs in the machine at once and one disc can talk to the other. So just by pressing the right buttons you can switch chunks out of Chapter Four into Chapter One. It would have taken weeks on the old German.

The computer does all that for you. Ask it correctly and your manuscript is made tidy once more, pages all re-numbered as fast as you can bat your eyelid.

Make no mistake, there have been some disasters. Computers are utterly unforgiving. If you are moving copy from one disc to another, if a full stop, a colon, even a blank space is out of position, they will refuse to function. You get a screen message which says: "You have committed error 24." You look up error 24 and you get something confusing such as "wrong syntax."

The most serious aberration, the real prang, is to forget to commit your copy onto a floppy disc. You type all day then you are supposed to push the "save" button. In the first week I forgot to do this twice. Whole stories disappeared into the electronic void and no amount of button pushing would call them back.

But it's a bit like falling under a tram. After you have done it once or twice you don't feel like doing it again.

The computer actually hates destroying copy. If you want to get rid of a story, say, on Joh Bjelke-Petersen you write KILL JOH BJELKE-PETERSEN. Then the computer comes back and asks anxiously: are you sure you really want to kill Joh?

"Yes," you say. Finally, it gives the news that the execution is complete. JOH KILLED, OK.

Then, there's the joy of all the software. Say you have written a 40,000-word piece and you discover to your horror that in your 5am delirium you have called Joh "Jock." You institute "global search." You tell the machine to find every reference to Jock and change it to Joh. Press the button and lo, back comes the message on the screen, reporting 75 changes.

What's more, I bought some software called a dictionary. This dictionary has 75,000 commonly-used words. So after writing a piece I have my disc with the story in one slot, the dictionary disc in the other. Then I type "P," asking it to proofread.

The dictionary counts the number of words, checks each one for typing and spelling errors, then comes back and announces the number of horrors it hasn't found in the dictionary. For the first time in 25 years Batman copy is going close to being copy pure with correct spelling. There was a time when sub-editors used to call me Chaucer because of the originality in spelling.

Some of the costs are fairly awesome. I mean, the printer will do anything. Ask it to double space, triple space, change its type, give you cute margins, give you 15 copies; it will do all that. But you go through paper like the New York Times and a large box of fan-fold paper can cost anything up to $75.

Then you spend, maybe, $45 on a box of floppy discs, which are delicate things and have to be protected. And when you go to buy a floppy disc box made in Switzerland they offer you a choice: $35 small, $55 large.

At this stage I am like Matthew Flinders feeling his way past Van Diemen's Land. Everything is a bit strange but very exciting. There will be more gripping episodes later.
