Cinders – it's back to the ball

6 min read.

First published 08 May 1984

Keith Dunstan travels by K-class steam locomotive to celebrate the centenary of T94 at the North Williamstown Railway Museum — 21 steam locomotives, a magnificent 1890 Norman car used by Victorian governors and royalty, and a coming Steam Age Australia luxury excursion service modelled on the Orient Express.

DEVOTION to steam unquestionably is becoming deeper and more prevalent. Mine was somewhat abated by travelling on troop trains during World War II and by enduring Brisbane suburban railways. There was something about their effluent, the flow of soot and cinders. If you lived in certain areas in Spring Hill, you could come to smell like a steam engine. Yet, of all passionate enthusiasts — of all hobby nuts — the most devoted are the steam people.

You see them at their best on railway excursions. Some dash by car from crossing to crossing so they can lie on their stomachs and take photographs — so that they can get the best angle, say, on the cow-catcher plus bogies, the coupled wheels or the pure beauty of the effluent as the driver blows his whistle. Others go along with tape recorders to capture every delectable puff, grunt, hiss and, of course, the wondrous noises made as the vast machine rattles through points and crossings.

Ah, but the true enthusiast is the one involved in restoration. Others may indulge in lesser pursuits such as golf, fishing or wind-surfing but he leaves his wife and children and spends every weekend painting and restoring 50-year-old dreadnoughts of the rail. Sometimes his wife is a nut, too, and they do it together.

We have just celebrated the centenary of T94, at the North Williamstown Railway Museum.

While 100,000 or more people were demonstrating against the threat of nuclear doom, another fine crowd — mostly members of the Australia Railways Historical Society — went by steam train to pay homage to the T94. We were carted by a very nice K-class (circa 1946) with vintage carriages which had charming photographs of scenes of Daylesford and the road to Apollo Bay. The drivers were Bill Steedman and Norm de Pomeroy, both veteran railwaymen. Norm said that he drove a diesel locomotive during the week. "Tomorrow I'm going to Geelong and the next day I'll be taking a train to Albury."

He was spotless in blue overalls and cap, yet he didn't pause for a second. He was working valves, pounding on the treadle, working the butterfly trapdoor on the firebox, shovelling coal, running an eye over pressure gauges.

Norm explained: "Steam is different. You are working with a live, breathing thing which is infinitely subtle. Yes, steam is like a woman — temperamental — but, treat 'em right, they give you a wonderful lot in return."

Bill and Norm, on their railways day off, were running a shuttle all day. It had begun early. I mean, with a diesel locomotive you can hop in and press a button and you are instantly in business. From cold, though, it takes three to four hours to work up sufficient steam with a K-class.

You start by burning wood and kerosene-soaked lint. Norm says that you can't push the coal in until you have enough steam to work the blower.

So we arrived at North Williamstown. The museum was started 22 years ago. One can remember that day very well indeed. The diesel era began in 1952 and the enthusiasts began to realise with horror by the end of the 50s that, soon, not a steam train would be left in service. Those of the faith could not even say the word "diesel"; they would growl "dis-easel."

The achievement at the museum defies belief. It has 21 steam locomotives and a whole range of vintage rolling stock, all perfectly maintained. The engine to be honored was vintage 1884, one of 18 built by the Phoenix foundry in Ballarat. Her brass dome was shining, she was exquisitely painted in green and gold — the Metropolitan Transit Authority colors. Even with 1,009,738 miles (more than 1.6 million km) on the clock, she looked as if she could start over tomorrow.

They have other very good stuff at Williamstown. Heavy Harry (H220), for example, was built at the Newport workshops in 1941. It was wonderful what we could build in those days. Now, everything seems to come from elsewhere.

Only one Heavy Harry was made and he was the biggest of all engines, a majestic, awesome thing — "260 tons, 92 feet 5 inches long, 14 feet high" — so big that many of the bridges in Victoria couldn't take him.

But I am fascinated by carriages. Williamstown has an old suburban red rattler or dogbox. Whether you could open the door of a dogbox on a cold day was always a magnificent test of strength.

Chairman Gerald Dee and museum manager Neil Pearson showed off with pride the magnificent Norman car. Built at Newport in 1890, it was used by the VR commissioners; for cabinet meetings; for royalty such as the Duke and Duchess of York, Edward Prince of Wales and others. As you admire the gorgeous comfort, the woodwork, you can muse that tough old Tommy Bent used to travel in here and no doubt it provided comfort for Sir Alexander Peacock, the premier who used 6 o'clock closing to help us win the Great War.

But steam is making a comeback in more ways than one. There are vintage trains which run on the first Sunday of every month. Three different groups are working on R-class locomotives — 180-tonners, heavy locos made in Glasgow circa 1953 — the last of their era. One, being restored by the Australian Railways Historical Society and the Society of Railway Enthusiasts, should be running again before the end of the year. Another is being done by railwaymen, members of the AFULE, and a third is part of an adventurous idea by a commercial firm — Steam Age Australia.

Tom Binns, managing director of Steam Age, has taken the R-class loco from the park in Bendigo. He bought 14 1941 era steel carriages in Adelaide and is spending $2 million to create, shall we say, a sort of luxurious vintage Orient Express. It all goes into action early next year, exquisite art deco style, and will run excursions to vintage capitals — Ballarat, Bendigo, Mildura and such. Binns doesn't think it can miss. He will get the nostalgia and the convention trade and, what with all the best in air-conditioned comfort, the patrons won't have to breathe a cinder.
