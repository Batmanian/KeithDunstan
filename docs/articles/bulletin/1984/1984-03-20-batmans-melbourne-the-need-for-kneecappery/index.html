The need for kneecappery

5 min read.

First published 20 Mar 1984

This has been a vintage summer for knees — Peter McNamara's reconstructed cruciate ligament, Graham Yallop's medial ligament, Garry Sidebottom's knee — leading Keith Dunstan to propose an Australian Foundation of Kneecappery and chairs of Cartilology at our universities.

THIS PAGE continues to be amazed by the wonderful medical knowledge one can gain by reading about sporting injuries. Normal humans live, suffer and die in near anonymity. Rarely do we get information on what ails them. But our sporting heroes are reported on in detail. Just in case you haven't noticed, this has been a vintage summer for knees.

There has been the saga of Peter McNamara's knee. He tore the cruciate ligament in his right knee and we were told never would he play tennis again. Recently we have received daily stories on his knee reconstruction, on the 11-kilo plaster cast which is being replaced with smaller casts and how he will make a comeback soon.

Then there is Graham Yallop's knee, a completely different ligament. He damaged his medial ligament while playing cricket against the West Indies. Right up to the moment of the final selection of the touring side we had hourly reports on the swelling and whether Yallop would or would not pass a fitness test. Alas, he did not.

Then there was David Hookes' knee. He injured his while going for a catch in one of those one-day matches. The word is that he is still carrying a limp even while playing over yonder in the Caribbean, so we will be looking forward to many more fascinating medical reports.

Even before the new football season has started we are getting some fantastic knee reports. Garry Sidebottom, Fitzroy's star centre half-forward, has broken down with knee trouble. A surgeon trimmed the knee joint and ligaments and repaired the cruciate ligaments in his right knee last September but it has swollen up again.

But for these cricketers and footballers, few of us would have heard of cruciate and medial ligaments. Then we read that Rene Kink of Essendon, sometimes called the "Abominable Hulk," is suffering from "patella tendonitis" and they are giving him a program of running in water to ease the pressure on his ailing knees.

Unquestionably, this has been the most rewarding knee year since 1973. Peter Hudson was having fearful knee trouble then. That was the year they were flying Hudson every weekend back and forth from Hobart in a Piper Twin Comanche so that he could play his match with Hawthorn. Francis Bourke, the Richmond champion, went down with knee trouble that year and few of us will ever forget the stirring headline in the Herald: TRAGEDY STRIKES RICHMOND.

Des Tuddenham, the captain and coach of Essendon, went the same way. It is said even Raquel Welch's bosom didn't receive as much attention that season as Tuddy's knee. One woman even broke into verse!

Oh yes! Rodney Marsh could be representing us in the West Indies right now if only his creaky knees had held together. It is clear that 90 percent of sportsmen eventually retire because their knees no longer can take it. Cricketers are copping it because of the one-day matches.

I remember in the old days that when batsmen were running up and down the wicket you would allow an extra run for the throw. Not any more. The fieldsmen have what you might call a berserk factor. They go after the ball in a state of frenzy. Hence the injuries.

The truth is that God didn't do a very good job when he designed the knee. It is particularly prone to injury in Australian rules football. If a player leaps high for a mark, receives a good belt on the way down and crashes at an angle, those medial and cruciate ligaments are at terrible risk.

So, if we are losing all our best men, why don't we do something about it? We have an Anti-Cancer Council, a National Heart Foundation, organisations to fight TB, asthma, multiple sclerosis and diabetes. But where is the organisation to look after the knee?

We should have the Australian Foundation of Kneecappery or, say, the Anti-Cartilage Council. Not enough research is going into the problem and maybe it is time our various universities had Kneecappery chairs or chairs of Cartilology. If you think this is a matter of insufficient moment, perhaps you should be reminded that John Coleman — perhaps the greatest full forward of all time — ended his career long ahead of time because of knee troubles.

It is generally agreed that Polly Farmer's knee was the biggest news story of 1962. It was photographed repeatedly. Zoom lenses came within 5mm of it on television. Teams of specialists discussed it. We followed that cartilage through all its stresses and strains until finally, at the end of the season, it was removed.

It was most surprising that the museum did not seize on the opportunity. Farmer's cartilage could have been put on display like Phar Lap's heart.

All sporting organisations should put in money to set up this National Foundation of Kneecappery. They could discover whether it is possible for old sportsmen to be revitalised with knees made of plastic.

Or perhaps they could set up a knee bank with fresh young knees taken from victims of car smashes. We could have the full excitement of knee transplant operations.

The fragile knee cartilage is as disastrous to the footballer or cricketer as the sesamoid bone is to the horse. When a horse breaks its sesamoid bone, the practical racehorse owners don't usually mess around: They shoot the beast where it has fallen.

I often think that we should do the same thing to footballers.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: The need for kneecappery, The Bulletin, 20 March 1984. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
