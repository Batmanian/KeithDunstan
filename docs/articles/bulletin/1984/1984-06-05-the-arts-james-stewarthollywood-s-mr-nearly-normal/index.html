Hollywood's Mr Nearly Normal

5 min read.

First published 05 Jun 1984

With James Stewart arriving in Australia to promote a Hitchcock retrospective, Keith Dunstan recalls his Hollywood encounters — a tourist bus driver shrieking at the sight of Stewart's dog outside his ivy-covered Beverly Hills house, the American Film Institute dinner where Henry Fonda recalled Jimmy's flawless career, and Hitchcock's toast to "the one exception" to his rule that actors should be treated like cattle.

IT IS SAID of James Stewart that he always had a plodding but triumphant virtue. Louella Parsons, the columnist, went even further: "Of all the Hollywood stars he was the one that was most nearly normal."

Back in the dreamtime when I was in boarding school, I kept two pictures of movie stars in my study cubicle — Deanna Durbin and James Stewart. Miss Durbin had dimples, an enchanting soprano voice and I remained deeply in love with her until that love turned to lust for Ava Gardner.

It is not so easy to explain the interest in Stewart; perhaps it was because he didn't seem excessively bright and was so honorable. James Stewart could — remember that filibuster when he was in Washington, coat, tie, voice disintegrating, all to put over some desperately virtuous bill?

My devotion to Stewart gave way eventually to Gary Cooper. When Cooper — as a legionnaire in Beau Geste — picked up a Vickers machine gun in his bare hands and mowed down the enemy, he won me forever. I still think it was a pretty good feat of strength.

Now the news comes through that James Stewart will arrive here in June to boost five classic Alfred Hitchcock movies. It shakes one to learn that the man has just turned 76.

The last time I was close to Stewart (or nearly close) was when working as a correspondent in Los Angeles. Everybody liked him. I think it was that practised, bumbling drawl that did the trick — even Bette Davis could mimic him.

The tourist companies used to run bus tours to the homes of the stars. They would take you up to Westwood and Beverly Hills and virtually would promise that you would see a live movie star. Of course, nobody did but they made it a point of visiting Stewart's place, where he had lived for decades — a large, double-storey house, covered in ivy. The day I went the driver shrieked with excitement: "There's Jimmy Stoo-et's dog!" And my God there was a dog outside Jimmy's gate. The bus almost tipped over with people rushing to inspect.

Soon after, I actually did see Jimmy. The American Film Institute gave him a "life achievement award" in 1980. They have a passion for handing out awards in California and this took the form of a grand dinner at the Beverly Hills Hotel. Present were Charlton Heston, Ernest Borgnine, Dustin Hoffman, Karl Malden, Gene Kelly, Jack Lemmon, Ruth Hussey, Richard Widmark, Walter Matthau and Princess Grace.

Jimmy already was into his 70s and looked it. He was stooped but didn't he always stoop? His hair was white, even his toupee was white. But that wonderful, burbling Stewart voice was unchanged. It came through so slowly, with so many stumbles that it sounded like a tape recorder with failing batteries. Or, as one critic put it, a mixture of "indecisive molasses."

Hitchcock launched the vast swamp of compliments. The old master looked terrible. It was only weeks before he died. He said: "I have been quoted as saying that actors are like cattle. The quotation is incorrect. What I said was 'actors should be treated like cattle.' An exception should be made. I won't keep you in suspense. His name is Jimmy Stewart."

They brought on Henry Fonda. He and Stewart were very old mates. They roomed together in New York and Fonda was waiting at the railway station when Stewart first arrived in Hollywood in 1935.

Fonda said that Stewart came from the town of Indiana in Pennsylvania and his parents were big in the hardware business. It was assumed that young Jimmy would take over the business. He went to Princeton and graduated in architecture. But he became interested in a summer theatre group which included Joshua Logan. Jimmy's parents were terribly shocked. Instead of entering into honest business, he joined Logan in a play on Broadway. Yet his father warmed to the idea. When Jimmy won an academy award for Philadelphia Story, his father asked for the Oscar and it was on display in the store until the old man died.

Fonda with great emphasis said: "Now here's a thought I want to lay on you. I walked the streets for five years looking for a job. Not Jimmy. Everything was a success. He just went from one to another."

Eventually we were blessed with Stewart on stage. Somebody said: "Mr Stewart will never make a great actor until his voice stops changing." He was quite right. But after that voice stopped, in his mumbling voice he said: "I know it's late and I don't want to keep you any later than is necessary. I'll try to talk first but the problem is that I don't know what to say."

He said: "My wife always says that when the phone rings and she picks up the receiver and says 'hello,' if there's a long pause she knows it's me."

It is puzzling that he should be now touring the world flogging old Hitchcock films, even if Hitchcock was a friend. In recent times he has been in hospital for sciatica, skin cancer and for a heart that doesn't beat regularly.

Yet he maintains this reputation for impeccable niceness. I remember one story. He and his wife grow vegetables. He once had such a splendid crop of peas that he decided to give some to his mate, Lucille Ball, who lived next door. The maid answered the door, looked at this old fellow in his gardening clothes and was going to close the door. Jimmy said: "There wasn't much point in arguing. I just took my produce and went away. At least the maid was nice about it."
