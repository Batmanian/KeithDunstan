How the footy is going to the dogs

5 min read.

First published 26 Jun 1984

Weekly VFL attendances have drifted from 9 percent of Melbourne's population in 1930 to 4.45 percent in 1984, five clubs are technically bankrupt, and North Melbourne ruckman Gary Dempsey warns the game is dying. Premier Cain has offered to help enforce player contracts, the VFL president wants a national competition of 16 teams, and international soccer has arrived, Paolo Rossi's dinner seat at the St Kilda Town Hall cost $5000.

WOULD YOU kindly stand by for a deathly shock? Victorian football is not doing very well.

In 1972, after immense study, we worked out a graph. Back in 1930, when football was the cheap entertainment of the masses, 9 percent of Melbourne's population went to the footy every Saturday.

So our graph started high on the left-hand side of the board and, with a few zig and zags, slowly descended to the right. Enthusiasm kept pace with population growth through most of the 30s. It was over 9 percent until 1935 and still 8.5 percent in 1938. During World War II (when we were outraged by the sight of healthy men not in uniform), it fell to 3.3 percent. That was in 1942.

After the war, all was well again. In 1946 the great euphoric love was away with 9.1 percent. Yet things were never quite the same again.

Attendances drifted with the rise of the affluent society. Everybody started to own automobiles and mostly they went in the wrong direction from the MCG and Victoria Park. By 1971-72, only 5.3 percent of the population was going to the football every Saturday.

During 1972, the average weekly parade of devotees was 133,405. In terms of numbers alone, the best year was 1963 when the average attendance was 152,187.

We have done a count on the first 10 rounds for 1984 and it comes to an average attendance of 133,575 or 4.45 percent of the population. You see, it's going down all the time; the game is not winning new adherents the way it should. If our devotion had kept pace with the grand old days, weekly attendance should be 273,000. That's going on an estimated Melbourne population of three million.

The sad truth is this: Victorian football has dreams of glory and it is carrying on as if the weekly attendance were 273,000.

North Melbourne ruckman Gary Dempsey retired recently. Dempsey is as near to the deity as you will get in Melbourne. He is 35 years old, a Brownlow medallist and he played 337 matches — the second-highest total of any player in VFL history.

He announced that VFL football is dying. "If something's not done in the next five years, league football's gone," said he. "You can't have clubs owing $2.5-$3 million when the revenue is not there."

Dempsey said: "We're all to blame. We all ask for more money and keep pushing." There was one interstate player he refused to name who had received $180,000 "in the hand" to join a club. He alleged that a couple of VFL footballers were getting $80,000 a year.

Premier John Cain says he believes that five of the 12 league clubs are technically bankrupt. He didn't name them but the VFL president Dr Allen Aylett last week named four. He says the VFL is guarding the finances of North Melbourne, Footscray, the Sydney Swans and St Kilda.

The VFL is acting as guarantor for loans totalling $1 million to keep Footscray and North Melbourne afloat. The Swans have been kept going only because the league is acting as guarantor for a $500,000 mortgage. Then, only last week, the League directors voted to prop St Kilda with a loan of $195,000.

These clubs have to make monthly budget reports to the league and they have to get written approval for any purchase costing more than $10,000. So they are in a hideous position. If they are broke and at the bottom of the league ladder, how do they get up again? You couldn't buy a pre-loved girl guide for $10,000.

So, what's to be done? This is a matter of such serious moment to the development of Victoria that Cain has offered to help the VFL enforce player contracts and end the heavy traffic of players changing clubs. This means that there could be an official veto on the star full forward or the high-octane ruckman getting his ransom price.

Aylett has said that VFL clubs must show a profit every six months or risk being expelled from the league.

There have been all sorts of suggestions. Herald sports writer Rod Nicholson reported speculation that St Kilda could merge with North Melbourne and Fitzroy pool resources with Melbourne, leaving only 10 teams.

Meanwhile, North Melbourne has called for a national competition of 16 teams from six states — urging a start next year or in 1986 at the latest.

The only flaw in the North Melbourne idea is that it shows an extraordinary devotion to the superiority of the game in Melbourne. There would be nine clubs from Victoria, two each from Western Australia and South Australia and one each from NSW, Queensland and Tasmania. Two Melbourne clubs would either have to disband, merge with other Melbourne clubs or move interstate.

Aylett, too, also has shown enthusiasm for the national competition.

So you see the game which allegedly is dearer to Melbourne than God, Mother and even the meat pie, has to make a move somewhere.

Meanwhile we have had international soccer in town — Juventus from Italy, no less. The star of Juventus, Paolo Rossi, is known as the $5 million man. He is called this because, six years ago, he transferred from Perugia soccer club to Juventus for $5 million.

There was dinner for Juventus at the St Kilda Town Hall, legendary home of a VFL team down on its uppers. The price for sitting next to Rossi at the dinner was $5000. So VFL finance, compared with international sporting cash, is really quite trifling. A million dollars, for example, is needed to get the Saints out of trouble.

Martina Navratilova earns that sort of lolly in one day.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: How the footy is going to the dogs, The Bulletin, 26 June 1984. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
