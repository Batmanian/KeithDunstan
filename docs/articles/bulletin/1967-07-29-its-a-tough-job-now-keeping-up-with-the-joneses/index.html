It's a tough job now keeping up with the Joneses

5 min read.

First published 29 Jul 1967

Melbourne detective Barry Jones becomes the father of quadruplets, prompting reflections on the fertility drug gonadatrophin, the contraceptive pill's impact on Australia's birth rate, and a colonial-era account of a Warrnambool woman who gave birth to sextuplets in 1841.

NOW LOOK HERE. We have Mrs. Jones the margarine lady, we have Susan Jones the air hostess, we have Andrew Jones the politician, we have Barry Jones the quiz king, and we have Barry Jones the Melbourne detective. Never was keeping up with the Joneses more difficult.

Actually keeping up with Barry Jones the detective is more difficult than keeping up with some of the other Joneses, like detective Andrew Jones. Detective Jones is the father of quadruplets, and his happy face has been beaming at us from the front pages of Melburnian newspapers for more than a week past. The successful birth has been Melbourne's triumph, and Mrs. Joanne Jones has given us a description of her four children. Andrea Louise: 'A glamour girl, but big enough to look after herself.' Carolyn Maree: 'A bit of mystery, always sleepy.' Susan Elizabeth: 'A real little fighter, always awake.' Dean Stewart: 'A little placid, but he's very long. He'll be a footballer like his father.'

But the really interesting feature is the publicity gained for the fertility 'pill' gonadatrophin as against the non-fertility pill progesterone.

It is only a few weeks since the 'Current Affairs Bulletin' of the University of Sydney produced a very interesting paper titled 'Pills and Populations.' It gave an estimate that there were 500,000 users of the pill in Australia. That is, about one in every five women of an age to bear children (ages 15–49) used the pill. On a population basis this is about 3.5 times the rate of consumption of the UK and 50 percent higher than that of the USA. According to the CAB figures this would imply that we are one of the leaders of the world. Note the proportion of Roman Catholics, 23 percent of the population.

The CAB also said that our birth rate was down to 19.61 per 1000 for 1965, the lowest for 22 years, and it sank again to 19.1 for the first half of 1966. However, the CAB was sceptical that this was all due to the pill. Probably the birth rate would have declined anyway. The truth is that the fertility pill will not overcome the non-fertility pill. In Melbourne the fertility work is undertaken by a small research team at the Endocrine Clinic at the Royal Women's Hospital. The doctors there say that gonadatrophin can help only about one percent of women. Mrs. Joanne Jones was treated with this drug after she had failed to have children for seven years. The gonadatrophin is produced in the hospital's laboratories from extracts of the pituitary glands. The hospital's tally is now 28. The first was rather spectacular, the Lawson quintuplets, born in New Zealand in 1965. Now there have been 10 single births, three twins, one set of triplets, and the Jones quads.

Several newspapers mentioned that the Jones quads were the fifth set born in Victoria in the past 50 years. But your pioneer is a little saddened that no one mentioned the Ferrers sextuplets. Could one refer you to the 'Port Phillip Gazette' of August 28, 1841: 'A woman of the name of Ferrers, living in the district of Grange (Warrnambool), was brought to bed on 3rd of July last of six children at one birth, presumed to be unique unparalleled in the records of medical history.

'The mother is wife to an industrious and honest man following the labor of splitter and fencer. She is Irish by birth, came out in one of Marshall's vessels, and is 26 years of age.

'The only remarkable symptom she experienced was in the growth of an inordinate appetite, her husband having been obliged to purchase an extra ration for her during pregnancy.

'She was first ill on the 1st of July, had several pains during that and the following day, and was in actual labour 16 hours. The children are, or either were, all girls; one, however, the first, was stillborn, and two died afterwards. Fortunately the poor woman had several female acquaintances who took care of the little strangers and consoled her. Under the prospects of starvation that attended their first appearance, she is now suckling two herself whilst her eldest girl feeds and nurses the third. They are remarkably thriving, and the mother, recovered from the fright and wonder attendant upon so extraordinary a birth, is getting quite proud of her offspring.

'During the period of labour she was fed at intervals with a teaspoonful of hot brandy and water. Living in that distant part of the country she had no other luxury or remedy to resort to.

'We have often heard of the fruitful nature of the climate and its wonderful effects in assisting the disappointed passion of philoprogenitiveness, but could scarcely credit it unless assured from such undeniable sources of truth.'

The old-fashioned philoprogenitive powers of Mr. and Mrs. Ferrers were plainly quite as potent as anything happening now with gonadatrophin.

Source: Batman's Melbourne: It's a tough job now keeping up with the Joneses, The Bulletin, 29 July 1967. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
