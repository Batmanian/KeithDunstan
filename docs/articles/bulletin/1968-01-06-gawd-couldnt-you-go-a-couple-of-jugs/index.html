"Gawd, couldn't you go a couple of jugs!"

6 min read.

First published 06 Jan 1968

A visit to Beechworth, the perfectly preserved gold-rush town in north-east Victoria, where the annual Golden Horseshoes Festival re-enacts an 1873 Chinese procession with elaborate costumes, Tom Mitchell on horseback, and a time capsule buried beneath the town hall steps.

BEECHWORTH is almost perfect. It is the only town in Victoria that is utterly historic. The public buildings, put up at a time when the town was stinking rich with the goldrush, are of a superb local granite and the shops are cast-iron balconied and thoroughly verandaed. The beauty about the town is that nothing much has happened these past 50 years, and if you covered up the petrol bowsers it would look exactly as it was in 1880. Nobody has had the money to ruin the town and even the train arrives now only once a week, mainly to bring in the beer.

Just lately, Beechworth has become tourist-conscious, and once again Mr. Ned Kelly is being called upon to assist. Fortunately, Ned was both in court and in jail at Beechworth. So now you can go on Ned Kelly tours to Beechworth, buy a Ned Kelly helmet for 40 cents, and, for a little more, a Ned Kelly scarf. Tanswells Commercial Hotel is being beautifully restored to exactly what it was in the 19th century in co-operation with the National Trust.

Ned, of course, drank in the bar at Tanswells, so naturally it is being renamed the Ned Kelly bar. You will be able to stand on the very spot where he ordered his pint and look up at a large Kelly print taken from the 'Sketcher.' Yes, everything at Tanswells will be 19th century except the plumbing, which was 19th century before, anyway.

But the great feature at Beechworth is the annual Golden Horseshoes Festival, which is largely inspired by an energetic vicar, Father Bob Hull. Every year they have a procession — and this year they decided to re-enact its one of 1873. It's a marvellous gaudy affair in which the Chinese wore special costumes imported from China worth $1600 ($100,000 in these times), according to Father Hull.

Tom Mitchell led the parade on horseback in the role of a general of the celestial army and he was a sight never to be forgotten. The Chinese Consul, Mr. E. E. Liu, thought he looked like Genghis Khan.

On the other hand, he looked something like a SF gentleman from Mars. Then he had a large flowering yellow shirt, a splendid blue skirt held up by a leather belt. But, wait, that's not all. Backing out from his shoulders were his vast rank insignia. Then out from the middle of his back was actually a dragon's wings. It discovered that a Chinese general cannot lean back in a chair while wearing a dragon's wings.

Beechworth is only a small place, some 3000 population, plus the patients at the local mental hospital. But the work done was immense. There were 300 in the procession all wearing utterly correct period Chinese costumes. Behind our celestial general there were the Chief of the Second Literati, Chief of the Third Literati, representative of the Emperor of China, members of the Imperial Academy, Custodian of the Imperial Jewels, Chief of the Sick and Distressed, and Chief Priest of the Joss No. 1.

The heat during the procession was terrible, and as they marched, bearing sedan chairs, carrying banners and pikes, exploding crackers and what-not, their make-up, which looked like bad bouts of jaundice, began to run. As they passed the post office I distinctly heard a distinguished member of the Imperial Academy say, 'Gawd, couldn't you go a couple of jugs!'

Mr. Liu, representative of the Republic of China, stood at the salute from Tom Mitchell. Students of history might have seen a beautiful irony in all this. The ex-representative of an exiled Chinese Government in Taiwan was there to take the salute from a general of a long-since-deposed Chinese Imperial Army.

Ah, but it was a splendid procession. The celestial general jumped up on the platform to make a speech and almost speared one of the members of the official party with his antennae. When untangled, he said, 'When I was peeling spuds in the Army during the war I never thought that I would end up as a general.'

He revealed later that Tom Mitchell is one of our great characters. He will tell you the Mitchells have been around the Murray since 1838, and as for Tom, he is a Cambridge MA and LLB. Each year the horse was shod with golden horseshoes and he rode into town to buy everyone a glass of champagne. At the declaration of the polls he had a brawl in the middle of the street with the opposing candidate. As a story for a re-enactment it is a little beauty.

Another time Tom played Mr. Kerford, a local member who became Premier of Victoria. 'Yes,' said a beaming Tom Mitchell, 'that day Victoria had perfect government for about 20 minutes.'

The festival features a goldfields cabaret ball and there was a gold-panning competition at Lake Sambell. Out at Beechworth Gorge, for $1.95, one's dish was filled with a pottle of earth that contained one per cent of gold. The man who did the most perfect job of separating earth from gold was declared the winner and won $60. One splendid lady panner worked with such fierce concentration and success that she not only won, but, alas, most of it was lost for ever in Lake Sambell.

The celebration finished with the sealing of a time capsule to be opened on January 26, 2000. Those who contributed their signatures became automatic inheritors on that date, and there were some rather famous ones: Governor-General Lord Delacombe, Premier Bolte and Sir Harold Holt.

The capsule itself was a rather splendid brass cylinder that weighed a ton and inside, God bless Beechworth, was placed various items: promotional brochures from firms in Wangaratta, a brick from a local brickworks, a bottle of beer (not yet specified), and a local newspaper.

The choice hardly seemed representative of our time. The range could have been more interesting — a mini-skirt, a bikini, a Ron Barassi footy sweater, a stubby, an empty can, a pressed flower from a hippy, a charge of LSD, the pill, a surfboard, some souvenir object from the decimal currency changeover, a Melbourne meat pie preserved in formalin, and a lock of hair two feet long from the prettiest young man nearby.

Then all we would need to amuse spectators in AD 2000 would be a copy of Mr. Andrew Jones' autobiography.
