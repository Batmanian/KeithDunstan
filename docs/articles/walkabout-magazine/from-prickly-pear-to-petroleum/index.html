From Prickly Pear to Petroleum

12 min read.

First published 01 Jul 1963

A visit to the Queensland oil boomtowns of Tara and Moonie, tracing the district's turn from a prickly-pear-choked backwater once known as 'The Lost Province' into the birthplace of Australia's oil industry, and meeting the selection farmers whose land the oil was found under.

The road from Tara to the Moonie oilfields was deserted. The sky was as black as the bonnet of the car, and already flashes of lightning were darting down to the earth. Wild life everywhere seemed to be on the alert. Two brolga birds moved gracefully between the trees. An emu came scudding along the fence, his tail wobbling from side to side like a T-model Ford flat out at 60.

Then, lo! a large turtle appeared in the middle of the gravel. It was like steam giving way before sail. One had to pull up and wait for his majesty to waddle slowly across the road. Farther on there was a kangaroo beside the road. The storm would never worry him: he had been hit by a car, and he was dead.

It was more than 50 miles from Tara to Moonie, and there was plenty of time to think of the strange change in fortunes that had come to the district. Tara is 200 miles west of Brisbane. By Queensland standards that is not very far west. In Quilpie or Thargomindah they would talk of it as up on the coast. Yet in the nineteen-twenties it was known as 'The Lost Province.'

From 1920 on, prickly pear spread across Queensland and northern New South Wales at the rate of 2½ million acres a year. At its peak it was on the point of ruining 65 million acres. Many a fine property had to be abandoned. The derelicts were left behind. For years afterwards it was possible to see homesteads not only surrounded by prickly pear but the plants growing through the ruins . . . the ultimate victory.

In 1925, the Prickly Pear Board imported 2750 eggs of the cactoblastis moth from Buenos Aires. The greedy, inch-long grubs did their work with amazing speed, and within five years the prickly pear was near beaten. The cactoblastis cactorum had a fine old time, so much so that they ate themselves out of house and home, and with little cactus left, 3,000 million of them died of starvation.

Yet they weren't entirely forgotten. At Boonarga, near Chinchilla and not far from Tara, the grateful settlers created a monument to the cactoblastis; they built the Cactoblastis Memorial Hall, perhaps the world's only monument to an insect.

Prickly pear was one thing, brigalow was, and is, another. It extends over the low country around Tara, Condamine, Miles and Wandoan. It is a tree of the wattle family. It grows to 30 feet high, and grows quickly. It also suckers and keeps on suckering until it makes a thick, terrible scrub. It is good for practically nothing, except firewood.

Once the brigalow is destroyed, the tired-looking country suddenly comes to life, and it is as good as anything on the Darling Downs. But how do you destroy it? The old method was to ring-bark it, tree by tree, a job that took an eternity. I met one man at Condamine who had a monster machine, an old boiler, loaded down with telegraph poles to a weight of 17 tons. He pulled it behind a tractor and it ground forward like a great chariot, crushing the brigalow into the ground.

The modern method is to string a cable between two bulldozers and simply tear it down. This done, the next step is to wait for a hot, dry, windy day, usually in January or February. Then set a match to the countryside. The result is like the burning of cane on a grand scale. The blaze roaring through the brigalow at high speed, flames boiling up several hundred feet, is a sight you are never likely to forget.

Clearing the brigalow has made all the difference to Tara. For the first time in 50 years the district is enjoying real prosperity. The shire clerk, Sandy Hodge, reports that 98 per cent of the settlers pay their rates on time. That's prosperity. All this gives some idea that that oil country has quite a story.

The Brisbane Courier Mail recently quoted some verse, which is accredited to Mr. Walter Gunn, father of the famous chairman of the Australian Wool Board, Sir William Gunn. It puts the whole situation very movingly in eight lines:

The Weir is a wilderness no one can sell, The Moonie is worse, it is regular hell. The men that live there are nearly dead beat From damper that's soddy, and grey salt meat. High was the brigalow, green was the pear, And sorry was I that I ever went there. There's a scrub out from Tara that hasn't an end, And if you go there, you'll be sorry, my friend.

Tara was the town everybody had been talking about, the nearest civilization to the oilfield. There are many like it in Queensland, a single broad main street with clumps of oleanders down the middle. A thousand people live there. There's a large, rambling, wooden hotel, several cafés, a store for farm machinery, and the important men in town are the bank managers and the representatives of firms like Mactaggarts, Winchcombe and Carson, and Australian Estates.

After more than a year they were taking the discovery of oil very calmly. The outward signs for tourists were the souvenirs. At the local newsagency one could buy bottle-openers, towels, ash trays, butter knives, all stamped with oil rig signs and the slogan 'Tara, the Oil Town.' More exciting, there were four-ounce bottles of honest-to-goodness Australian oil from Moonie No. 2. The Union Oil Development Corporation donated the oil and bottles and they were being sold for five shillings each on behalf of the Tara Olympic Swimming Pool Fund. And what a selling-point they had, The First Australian Oil To Go On Sale!

Another interesting item to be picked up were bits straight from the oil rig. Now an oil-drilling bit is a hefty chunk of metal. It weighs at least 40 pounds, and, new, costs £120. After 12 hours' drilling or so it is finished and ready for the scrap heap. Ah, but what a souvenir! You might ask, what on earth could you do with it? Well, Charlie Hart, the local garageman, is using his as a base for a nice letter box and he is making another for his mother in Brisbane. It will be a never-ending conversation piece.

Charlie said that at least half the people in Tara owned Australia Oil and Gas shares, and very few had sold. The oil strike had brought him a steady stream of business. Others, though, wondered how long the business would last. They feared that a pipeline through to Brisbane would mean by-passing Tara, and life would go on exactly as before. However, one wildcat well has gone down six miles from Tara, so who knows?

I continued on the road to Moonie. The rain began to pour down, so that it was almost impossible to see through the windscreen and the car skidded all over the road. It was flat country with brigalow everywhere, no shops, few houses, and, according to the locals, it was the season for death adders.

Suddenly, in the midst of all this loneliness, I arrived at the Moonie Motel, the finest west of Toowoomba. It seemed as out of place as an espresso bar in the middle of the Sahara. Toowoomba interests, no doubt hoping to cash in on the oil strike, had spent £100,000 on it. At Tara the modern shire hall had the traditional wooden toilet seat and a box of sawdust. Here, everything was fine, a shower and flush toilet to all the 28 rooms, a dining room, living-room space, which could alternate as a dance floor, a self-service store and, glory be, television. The set brought in Channel 10, Toowoomba, and just 20 feet away there was the biggest juke box I've seen in Queensland. Hardened local residents were marvellously capable of taking in a Western on the TV with one ear and a rock tune from the juke box with the other.

That wasn't all. The next day the motel manager Doug Clark, was due to open the swimming pool, a splendid affair, 50 by 30 feet, surrounded by cabanas in the best Los Angeles style. Also there was a 12-foot wading pool for children. Mr. Clark showed the pieces for a magnificent sign to go outside the motel, a model of an oil rig, 35 feet high. It was to be outlined in neon tubing and the blazing neon would simulate oil gushing out the top. At the bottom would be an oil bit, 'the first bit to strike oil at Moonie.' Mr. Clark wasn't prepared to swear it would be the actual bit, but it would be one of the first 20, anyway.

All this was just the start of the brand-new township, Moonie. Future plans called for tennis courts, a bank, a church, a garage, a plumber's shop, and already there was a school just down the road. It all seemed like an astonishing gamble, but Doug Clark did not think so. He said there was a steady through traffic of truck drivers and sightseers coming to the oil field. Besides, he had his permanent residents, wives and families of oil engineers.

That night, two of the owners of the very land where oil was found came to look at TV. I don't know what I expected. I rather felt, perhaps, that they would be drinking champagne and lighting cigars with £5 notes. The reverse was the case. The two men were Harry Green and Ray White, both in their early thirties. They said that the oil was spread over four properties, and the owners were Harry Green, Tim Brown, Ray White and Ron Boland, which led me to speculate whether, like gold, there were colours of oil.

All four had won their selections, just a few years before, in the ballot. They had four to five thousand acres each, and they agreed they were real battlers in the Dad-and-Dave tradition. Only Tim, who was married, lived in a proper house; the other three lived in shacks. They had no money to waste on home comforts.

I asked them how they felt when oil was found on their property. Ray White said: 'We could have wished them to hell.'

He said the seismic crews and the riggers went through, cutting fences and upsetting stock. Now, however, things had improved. They had a better understanding of each other's problems.

But what about the money? Ray White said they owned only the surface. All the mineral rights belonged to the Crown. For each hole that was down they received compensation of £100.

They didn't want to quarrel in any way with the oil people, but they were very confused about the future. After all, their life was farming, not oil. If the oil field was a big success and the engineers put down wells every 50 yards or so, they would do very well. Otherwise . . . who knew whether the inconvenience would be worth it? They were just at the stage of trying to build up their properties. Harry Green said that a month previously they were burning off the brigalow, and the fire on Ron Boland's place had got out of hand. It swept through the entire property, and it gave the oilmen an awful fright. The fire was all around Moonie No. 9. It stopped drilling for 18 hours and they had to fight back the flames with three tractors. Of course, the fire destroyed all the feed on Ron Boland's land. Harry Green said that he and Ray White were taking 1000 of Ron's sheep by turns until the grass started to grow again. You get that type of mateship out on the selection.

The next morning I drove the extra five miles to look at Moonie oil wells, numbers 1 to 10, where they had 10 strikes in 10 holes. It wasn't as dramatic as one would have expected, no oil pouring into the air, no rush of men and trucks. There were just 100 men on the job and it was explained to me that the oil was carefully controlled; it was never, in unseemly fashion, allowed to gush. All the wells ready for production were hardly worth looking at. They were housed in little tin sheds, very suspicious-looking little Queensland sheds, and on top of the well was a 'Christmas Tree.' They go for colourful names in the oil business, and a Christmas Tree is nothing more than a glorified fire hydrant, a series of valves and chokes.

The best sight was the well actually being drilled, at this stage, Moonie No. 10. The rig stood 135 feet high. On the platform were six massive diesel engines, and alongside great pumps for circulating the chemical mud so necessary for drilling. The man in charge at No. 10 was Dave McGarry. He came from Port Augusta, South Australia. He looked almost out on his feet. He had slept for only six hours in the past three days. He was just finishing off No. 10, and he was involved in pumping cement through perforated piping 5000 feet down to seal off water close to the oil level. It was a delicate operation, like baby-sitting. There were a thousand things that could go wrong at any hour of the day or night.

Dave slept in an aluminium caravan, just 50 yards from the rig, which meant that he could be on call round the clock. The only real home comfort at the camp was television. It shakes you a little to see a TV aerial out there among the kangaroos.

Dave was not anxious to answer any questions about costs or the size of the oil reserves. From the wells put down it was obvious that the size of the field was three and a half miles long and pear-shaped. Yet at this stage he said there was no telling how much oil there was; they would keep on putting down more and more wells until they proved to be dry. They are now sinking their 10th. Even the executives like Doyle T. Graves talk little more than Dave McGarry. Mr. Graves is resident manager of the Union Oil Corporation. Union Oil has 40 per cent of Moonie, Kern County Land Company of California 40 per cent and Australian Oil and Gas 20 per cent.

However, the real proof that Moonie is ready to go is the decision to build a £7 million pipeline, 190 miles from Moonie to Brisbane. No one spends that kind of money except on a sure thing. The Queensland Parliament has pushed through the necessary legislation. This means that no farmer will be able to object to the pipeline. Earthmoving equipment will dig the ditch, and the prefabricated pipe will be simply rolled into the ground. It should be in action by the end of this year.

While the Union-Kern A.O.G. people are rather conservative about all this, confident statements come from the Queensland politicians. What with oil from Sunnybank, near Roma, plus Moonie, they are certain that the pipeline will be a common carrier for a number of oilfields. Indeed, judging from some statements, one rather gets the impression that Brisbane is on the way to become a sort of oil-sheikdom, with every Cabinet minister in a gold-plated Cadillac.

Even if that is never likely, one Queensland newspaper full of gusto recently described Brisbane as 'the oil capital of the South Pacific.'
