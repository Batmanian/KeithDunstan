1966 and All That

11 min read.

First published 01 Dec 1966

Years are terribly ephemeral. They elapse with such appalling rapidity that it is difficult to remember what happened in any one of them.

There are several good ones that do stick in the mind: 1066, 1788, 1812, 1925 (I was born that year) and 1956, the year of the Olympic Games.

What on earth happened to all the others? They have just disappeared into the wasteland. Yet 1966 is almost worth adding to one's short list. It was unquestionably above average and vintage in many ways. It was decimal year, Menziean retirement year, Holt year, drought year, mini-skirt year, conscription year, Vietnam year, Prince Charles year and the year, glory be (I belong there), that Victoria at last won 10 o'clock closing.

It started in typical hectic fashion with one of the most violent prison break-outs Victoria has known. Ronald Ryan and Peter Walker broke out of Pentridge Gaol, Melbourne, on December 19 last year, and by the time they were re-captured on January 7, a Pentridge warder and a man at Middle Park had been shot dead, and several families had been held in terror.

They were captured in Sydney, and Ronald Ryan commented: "We should have known better than to try and beat Sydney coppers." It was the sort of remark that went down rather well in Sydney and rather poorly in Melbourne.

On February 1, Victoria introduced 10 o'clock closing for hotel bars, the second last Australian State to take the step. Actually Victorian hours were cut back to six o'clock in 1916, as a temporary measure to boost the war effort. Many felt that it wasn't really a decisive factor in the defeat of the Kaiser; nevertheless it lasted for almost 50 years.

The move was hurtful to a number of New South Wales border towns. Every night after six p.m., there had been a mass exodus from Victoria over the border to the still flowing wells of New South Wales. All this came to an end. Now the last six o'clock closing State was South Australia. So there was a new exodus, the six p.m. exodus across the border from S.A. to the newly flowing Victorian wells.

Decimal currency came to Australia on St. Valentine's Day, February 14, and beforehand all sorts of fearful happenings had been predicted. People would utterly fail to understand the new system of dollars and cents, or be able to convert the old L.S.D. to it. The supplies of coins and new notes would be hopelessly inadequate, and roaring inflation would take over.

None of these things eventuated. Oh yes, there were some price rises, and I think the only items that actually cost less in the new currency were licorice straps, but altogether things went remarkably smoothly.

After the sedate, very British-style banknotes, the new dollar notes seemed gaudy, as if they came out of a monopoly set. The 10-dollar note featured the poet Henry Lawson (who was always desperately short of a dollar), on one side, and on the other, Francis Howson Greenway, the convict architect, who was transported for forgery. The Governor of the Reserve Bank, Dr. H. C. Coombs, said: "I think it's good to have an ex-convict on one of the new bank notes."

The new coins were well-accepted, but even late in the year, Australians had failed utterly to give them satisfactory nicknames. The only successful names were the "Twin Lizzie" for the two-cent piece which had Queen Elizabeth on one side and the frilled-neck lizard on the other, and the "Puss" for the platypus on the 20-cent piece.

Mercifully, Australians never really took to calling their dollars "Bucks." If one had to be with-it in the hotel bar, then one referred to "Oxford Scholars."

Counterfeiters were most efficient. They forged most things from the 10-dollar bill with Messrs Greenway and Lawson to the half-dollar silver piece.

Meanwhile coin collecting continued to be all the rage, and the 1930 penny, of which only very few were ever minted, fetched $810 at auction. However, Australian counterfeiters moved in and made up for the failure of the Royal Mint. In June several fake 1930 pennies were found in Sydney.

The most significant event during the year was the retirement of Sir Robert Menzies and a new age that came in with Mr Harold Holt. An English M.P., a former editor of the Sydney Morning Herald, Angus Maude said: "Menzies was perhaps the first really great man in a country which has shown a remarkable determination to cut great men down to size."

Sir Robert had served Australia as Prime Minister for a record 18½ years, and few people, even his opponents, wanted to cut him down to size.

He bought a house in Haverbrack Avenue, Malvern, and, incredibly, it became Melbourne's top attraction for week-end sightseers. The cars went by in a never-ending stream, and it was put on the route for tourist buses. Sir Robert himself said the street name should be changed to Have-a-look Avenue.

After the farewell dinners and speeches had died he appeared to enjoy himself. He went to England to be inducted as Lord Warden of the Cinque Ports, at Walmer Castle. He looked an extremely self-conscious Lord Warden in his uniform, and he said: "I'm masquerading as a species of admiral. I have not yet learned to control the movement of my epaulettes when attempting to conduct a social conversation. Yet I still hope to pick up these tricks which are known by heart by any true admiral."

Several days later the Hastings Winkle Club made him Gold Winkle No. 2, and presented him with a solid gold winkle. The only other man to receive such a token of esteem was Sir Winston Churchill.

One wondered what would happen to the OUT MENZIES slogans which had been so diligently painted on railway bridges by local Communists for many years. The first HALT sign appeared the first week of the new prime ministerial era.

It wasn't easy for Harold Holt to take over after waiting for so long in the wings. He said later in a press interview: "My biggest problem was getting people to accept and be conscious of me as their national leader."

Yet Mr Holt wasted no time in creating an utterly different image. He was photographed at Portsea in what could only be described as the most sensational picture of the year. There he was in his spear-fishing outfit, accompanied by his three beautiful daughters-in-law in quite disturbingly brief bikinis. The photograph went all round the world, and Mr Holt was branded in London as the "007 Prime Minister".

So the pattern spread. He did almost everything differently from his predecessor. He showed no obvious love for London; no passion for ceremony, for Wimbledon or Lord's. His enthusiasms were much nearer to Washington, where he developed remarkably close relations with President Johnson. The President invited him back from London to spend another day with him, and Mr Holt's words "We're all the way with LBJ" seemed like the most important Australian policy statement in 20 years.

He has been anything but a stay-put Prime Minister. He has already created some kind of history for an Australian P.M. by visiting Australian troops in Vietnam. He has proved himself a keep-fit fanatic. Mrs Holt revealed that he could hold his breath for two-and-a-half minutes. She said: "He even holds his breath in the bath. Sometimes I have seen him in the bath water, and thought he would have drowned. But he's shaken his head to reassure me."

It was the year of the protest. Everybody seemed to want to march. We had protest marches against the bomb, protest marches against the war in Vietnam, basic wage protest marches, education protest marches, even marches by nurses and architects. A new word came into the language, the "teach-in". This was a sort of an interminable protest discussion which tended to go on all night. Then it had its variations, the sleep-in, the eat-in, the work-out.

The brave new Prime Minister achieved with comparative calm what threatened to cleave the country in two world wars; he was the first man to introduce successfully a compulsory call-up of young men for war on foreign soil. Yes, there were protests. The tyres of his car were let down, marbles were thrown at him, young men burned their call-up cards outside his house, but even so the calm was remarkable. Most people accepted the need for the call-up. Our commitment to Vietnam increased to 4,500 men, and the first national service trainees died in action in May.

At the various universities it was possible to buy NO CONSCRIPTS FOR VIETNAM stickers for 50 cents each. Another popular sticker was: Y DY 4 KY? But most national servicemen accepted quietly that it was their duty to give two years to their country. Cricket lovers suffered a terrible shock. Doug Walters, 20, and already the greatest Australian all-rounder since Keith Miller, was called up just after his selection for the tour of South Africa.

Where others had protested Walters said simply: "I've got to go, so I'll try to enjoy it." All this happened just after he won the Golden Peanut Award donated by the peanut industry for Australia's cricketer of the year, but nobody thought of calling Doug Walters a peanut.

Yes, it was an up-and-down year. Joern Utzon, after a dispute with the N.S.W. Minister for Public Works, Davis Hughes, resigned as architect of the far-from-finished Sydney Opera House. The estimated price went up to $50 million and Melburnians enjoyed calling it the Danish Blue. They had suffered several years earlier over their fractured Kings Bridge.

Sir Thomas Playford, leader of the Opposition in the South Australian Legislative Assembly, and the former Premier for 26 years, retired on his 70th birthday, which made it a remarkable year for politicians voluntarily giving up office. Asked how many Federal Prime Ministers he had outlasted he said: "Well, there was Mr Lyons, Sir Robert and, in between, Earle Page, Fadden, Forde, Curtin, Chifley . . . and two British monarchs."

Mr Calwell, leader of the Federal Opposition, survived with a scarred chin after a 19-year-old factory hand blasted at him with a shot gun. The youth's excuse was that he just wanted somebody to take notice of him.

The mini-skirt became the essential fashion for all those between 14 and 22 and an unessential fashion for those over 22 who tried to wear it. The cry went around the land: "The thigh's the limit." There was one consolation, at least it was now possible to tell the boys from the girls. Mia Farrow, Frank Sinatra's young bride, cut off all her hair, to give the boyish look. This, too, became the fashion in Australia and the task was even easier.

The girls were the ones with the short hair. Prince Charles came and went. He spent two terms at Geelong Grammar's mountain outpost, Timbertop. The Australian public saw very little of him. The young Prince was extremely well sheltered from the public gaze. He seemed to have a good time; he went ski-ing at Mt Buller, he visited country properties, he visited New Guinea and the Barrier Reef during school holidays.

However one felt sorry for his Man Friday, the Commonwealth policeman who never left his side, Inspector Derek Sharp. The word went around that the Inspector suffered in keeping up with the Prince on the snow, out on the water, on long, long hikes and cross-country runs all around Timbertop.

But our most resplendent visitor of 1966 was President Lyndon B. Johnson of the United States. Nothing quite like this had happened to Australia before. The tall, lean Texan with his aide and an entourage of several hundred officials, journalists and photographers, covered more of Australia in his four days' visit and was seen by more Australians, in person and on TV, than any previous overseas guest. Those who, like the Prime Minister, went "all the way with L.B.J.", found the pace quite exhausting.

We could go on and on with all that happened in 1966. Once again we didn't quite decide a Test cricket series against England, but we did thrash them at Rugby League. There wasn't a single Australian in the men's or the women's finals at Wimbledon, but Jack Brabham became world champion racing driver as well as world champion manufacturer in his Repco Brabham car.

Actually, as I write this, 1966 is far from finished. Nevertheless I am quite a soothsayer, and I can tell you many of the things that will happen in 1967. Motor mowers will make their biggest noise when you are suffering from a hangover on Sunday morning.

The kids will forget their school lunches 35 times, lose their hats, caps and homework books at 8 a.m. 270 times, moan that other kids in the class can stay up to 9 p.m. to watch TV 365 times, and you'll start off a sentence: "In my day, if . . ." 280 times.
