The Year of Anti-Football Protest

10 min read.

First published 01 Apr 1968

Keith Dunstan's account of founding the Anti-Football League in 1967 with Douglas Wilkie, from the badge sold for the Berry Street Babies Home to the posthumous Wilkie Medal awarded to Prime Minister Harold Holt for never once being seen at a match.

IT WAS THIS TIME LAST YEAR that a strange, almost unbelievable, phenomenon hit Victoria, an Anti-Football League. Now the time has come to issue an annual report.

The actual beginning was on Sunday, April 16. The scene, the reporters' room of the Sun News-Pictorial in Melbourne, was typical. Nobody was working much; all the males, and even the females, were standing in groups discussing what Collingwood had done to Melbourne, what Carlton had done to Fitzroy, or whoever was playing whom.

Just one man stood out, Douglas Wilkie, the foreign affairs commentator. He said: "We get the previews, we get the inquests, we get the post-mortems, we get the predictions, then we get the expert analyses, we get eternal football, talk, talk, talk."

"I know," I said, "we get as much as 23 hours of TV time a week on football. As for radio on Saturday afternoons we are almost completely swamped."

"Well, I don't know that I can stand it much longer," continued Wilkie; "there should be some protection for people who aren't interested in football. There should be some sort of organisation or society that could fight for them."

Next morning I put a paragraph in the Sun, to the effect that a correspondent had suggested that there should be an Anti-Football Society. It was a very little paragraph, for to suggest anti-football in Melbourne, in April, was rather like being anti-clerical at the time of the Spanish Inquisition.

The morning after that there were six letters, not in protest but all virtually demanding an anti-football society. One said: "I will join. All my family will join. Even my dog will join."

By Wednesday, with no more mention of the subject, we had received 32 letters. Obviously we had to go ahead, so I called on the editor for what I felt would be a difficult interview.

The Sun had always proved itself, shall we say, not without interest in football. The editor was a true Geelong supporter. He made visits to Geelong on alternate Saturdays, which is almost extreme devotion.

I said: "How would it strike you if I formed an Anti-Football Society?"

There was a long silence. He put his head on his clenched fists and didn't say anything. This was worse than I had feared. Then he said: "If there are people who buy our newspaper who love football and there are people also who hate it, then we should cater for both. All right, you go ahead."

Immediately plans were made. Douglas Wilkie at once was appointed president of the Anti-Football League. It was important to be a league and not a society. We felt that had to match the Victorian Football League in strength.

I became the honorary secretary and the first move was to get a badge. The idea of the League was not to hate football, but to get relief from the talk about it. With a very simple and discreet badge, one AFL member could recognise another AFL member and here was a chance for an intelligent conversation with someone who was uninterested in how many goals Wade had kicked or how Barassi had performed on Saturday.

What we wanted was something about the size of a Legion of Honour rosette, and red, so that it could be seen quickly. There should be no lettering. The shape would be that of a cube, symbolic of something that would not bounce.

While we were pondering this there was a letter from a commercial artist, Alfred Phillips. He said: "I will design you a badge." He went on to say that he was most experienced. Then I looked in astonishment; the notepaper had the letterhead K. G. Luke Aust. Pty. Ltd. The chairman and managing director of this company is Sir Kenneth Luke, president of the Victorian Football League.

Surely he was joking? But on the telephone Mr. Phillips assured me that Sir Kenneth would not mind. The VFL and K. G. Luke Aust. Ltd. were quite separate organisations and if the AFL gave a definite order then it would be accepted. And, from that moment, the co-operation from the Lukes was marvellous.

But the next problem was how to sell the badge. The only thing to do was to approach one of the country's largest department stores. Interviewed, a director looked startled. He said: "I have been making my own decisions for 32 years, but I don't know about this one. I'll have to go to the board."

Clearly the Anti-Football League was a very dangerous affair. But all was well. He received the "go ahead" and we ordered 600 badges. Yet there was still a snag. It was felt that an Anti-Football League could not exist on merely being anti. It needed a cause, so we decided to sell all the badges in aid of a babies' home. After all, it was a very good cause, and the babies might even grow up to be anti-footballers.

The AFL waited impatiently for six weeks for the 600 badges to arrive. The launching date was Saturday, June 17. The story, to be given front page lead in the Sun's Saturday magazine section, was in proof on the Thursday, when the department store rang. Alas, they could not go ahead. It would get them in dutch with other charities if they established the dangerous precedent of selling badges.

Now we were in trouble. The initial cost of the badges was 18 cents each, to be sold for the babies for a minimum of 40. The only thing to do was to buy the whole 600 myself, and pray.

It was an anxious week-end. At 11 a.m. on Monday the Post Office sent around a sack of mail. There were 264 letters, most of them containing postal notes. And it went on and on. The entire 600 badges were gone in two days, and we put in a crash order for another 5,000.

Here was a typical letter: "I write to ask you for membership of the Hate Football Association, in fact I want to be a foundation member, as high on the list as possible. As a youth I played the odd game, but early became sickened by the religious ritual of those in the congregation. I suggest that in the interests of safety the society be organised along the lines of the classic secret society as members are likely to be regarded as fearful heretics. Yours, etc., John Winckle, Drummond St., Carlton."

One woman sent a donation of $10 for a single badge. She said her life had been ruined by football. Every Saturday her husband went off to a game. At night she watched the replay on TV, as he drank down can after can of beer. Then he went through the same performance on Sunday.

Not all the letters were complimentary. Indeed some were violently otherwise. One said: "If you don't like football, why don't you get out? LEAVE MELBOURNE NOW."

Another said: "Now that you have got your League why don't you get out of Victoria? There are plenty of decent Victorians in Sydney who are pining to get home for the finals. We could work a sort of exchange like between the Egyptians and the Israelis. One decent Victorian in Sydney for 1,000 AFL members."

A surprising number of people accused the AFL of being a Communist-inspired movement. One pointed out his contempt over many years for everything I had said and written. The colour of the badge, too, was very indicative. If one ounce of decency had been shown this badge would have been made red, white and blue in honour of the boys in Vietnam.

There were a few scuffles in bars. One barmaid in a hotel not far from the Sun took particular exception. She made a habit of pulling pin-badges out of lapels, and she claimed that she shot down 15 Anti-Football members in the first week. One member complained that a character came up to him in Collins Street, pulled out his badge, threw it on the ground and jumped on it.

Yet the movement simply grew and grew. Noel Ferrier at Triaca's restaurant and June Bronhill, both there and at the Lido, were auctioning them every night. They received up to $70 for a single badge, all for the babies.

A team of lady 10-pin bowlers, who felt they were football widows, formed themselves into a team, named it the Anti-Football Wives, and bowled with such ferocity that they went on to win the State A Grade title.

The AFL also won a fashion accolade. The badge caught the eye of one of Melbourne's top couturiers. She thought the badges were so neat that she decided to feature them as smart lapel accessories in her spring-summer collection. She ordered a gross.

Seeing that all footy clubs had club songs and records, it was important that we should do the same. The words of our song, written by David Rankine of the Sun, were put to music, sung by folk singer Doug Owen and recorded by W. & G. The first verse went:

Oh, we are the Traitors, The VFL ballers; We're aiming to cut footy's image to size. . . . We think big Barassi Isn't really that classy; To us he's a Little Boy Blue in disguise.

To complete the record we had another idea. The most desperate need in the entertainment industry was for silence. So we felt what a fine thing it would be if this record had four minutes of silence on the other side. Suffering parents who searched for peace on their radiograms could have pure stereophonic silence. True enthusiasts could play it over and over again. It was introduced by Doug Owen, four minutes silence followed and then it finished with a respectful "Thank-you".

There were articles on the AFL published in English, Irish, Scottish and American newspapers. There were several letters from an Indian gentleman in Ceylon. He felt that the philosophy of the AFL was right in line with the teachings of Buddha, and he finished his letter "I love you".

There is a vast occasion in Melbourne which comes at the end of the home games, the awarding of the Brownlow Medal for the "best and fairest" footballer of the season, and it gets great cover in the press, radio and television.

It was important therefore that we should have an award for the best anti-footballer. Our friends K. G. Luke came to our aid again and struck a truly beautiful medal, correctly engraved and in a plush-lined case. We called it the Wilkie Medal in honour of our president.

After much debate, and calling for opinions from the members, it was decided to award it to the Prime Minister, the late Mr. Harold Holt. The reasons were these: Governors and politicians have always and most carefully aligned themselves with football teams. Mr. Calwell is for North Melbourne, Sir Robert Menzies has always been No. 1 at Carlton, the late Sir Dallas Brooks was No. 1 at Richmond and now the present Governor of Victoria, Sir Rohan Delacombe, has taken his place. Even Mr. Whitlam is an honorary member at Collingwood. But Mr. Holt allied himself to any team, rejected all offers to be patron, and, as far as anyone knew, never went to a match; therefore he was a worthy recipient of the medal. The League presented it to him in his office on September 11.

By the end of the season there were 17,012 members in the AFL, double the number of the League's largest club, Collingwood, and the profit to the Berry Street Babies Home was $6,932.50.

Regrettably though, towards the end of the season the AFL began to be treated, not as a grave protest but as a bit of fun. Norm Smith, Lou Richards, and many other leading football figures were seen wearing badges. My worst moment I think was immediately after the Grand Final. Richmond had just won the flag, and it was a moment of euphoria for all Richmond supporters.

Just then Roy Wright, former Richmond captain and Brownlow Medal winner, tapped me on the shoulder. He turned back his lapel to reveal an Anti-Football badge and said: "Aren't you proud of me?"

At that moment I wondered whether the AFL had been defeated and what we should do this year. But you wait, we'll be back.

Source: The Year of Anti-Football Protest, Walkabout Magazine, April 1968. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
