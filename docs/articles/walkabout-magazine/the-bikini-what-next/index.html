The Bikini, What Next?

9 min read.

First published 01 Jan 1968

A history of Australia's long, losing battle to regulate what beachgoers wear and when they may swim, from the segregated sea baths and bathing machines of the 1890s and William Gocher's 1902 defiance of Manly's bathing laws to the 1968 furore over the bikini.

Many of my friends have interesting views on modern bathing costumes. For example, there is Alf Bloggenheimer who says: "The whole thing has been proved and finished. Bikinis have become so brief nobody takes any notice any more. And it would be the same if all girls got around starkers, they wouldn't notice them either."

No doubt there is something to be said for the if-everybody-did-it-you-wouldn't-notice theory, and I have tried to put it into practice.

Take the Victorian beach of Lorne. As bikini beaches go this is a beauty. The young things parade past by the hundred, bikinis unbelievably brief, lovely sun-tanned bodies.

I sit wearing sun glasses with that worthy novel Aunt Hilda gave me for Christmas. Slowly, inexorably, one's eyes rise just above the print and these sun-tanned bodies are moving past, languidly.

Now, according to the Bloggenheimer Law, these interesting scenes should have no effect whatever. But they do, they do. One Thursday afternoon I started the same paragraph 22 times.

And if Lorne had been in Ancient Greece, where they had complete beach freedom without strings, I feel I would have lost grip of the book altogether.

Complete freedom of undress on the beach is almost at hand. There are only five or six square inches to go. But perhaps it is our serious southern demesne, perhaps it is the cold winds blowing up from Mawson causing shorter summers and less time for experiment, but whatever the reason we always seem to have been more easily shockable than our northern neighbours, across the border.

Back in pioneering times we used to bathe in the Yarra. Some sights were so disgraceful that, in 1841, the Victorian Parliament passed a law to protect tender eyes from the sight of bare flesh. Bathing was forbidden between the hours of 6 a.m. and 8 p.m.

There was a little open sea bathing then, in Victoria, but not overmuch. If a lady wished to bathe there was the bathing machine, the box on wheels which trundled right into the water, and the dip was taken with the utmost discretion. Practically all bathing until 1908 or 1909 was done in sea baths, in which, you will be pleased to hear, the sexes were segregated. The male swimming instructor did not get into the water with a young lady. He stood aloof on the boards, rope around her middle in case of disaster.

In open sea swimming we were a long way behind those forward people of Sydney. They had the famous case of Mr. W. H. Gocher, proprietor of a Manly newspaper. In 1902 he objected to the law of the New South Wales Parliament, which forbade citizens from bathing between 6 a.m. and 8 p.m.; so he advertised in his newspaper that the next day he proposed "to force the issue by bathing at noon".

Next day, wearing his neck to knees, he sat on the beach and waited to be arrested. No policeman appeared, either then or on the next day when Gocher reappeared clad for bathing. The battle of the Sydney beaches was won.

Yet Melbourne took another eight years to fight for freedom. The crude sight of flesh was bad enough, but what really shocked people was the thought of males and females in the water together. Heaven knows what libidinous incidents might take place.

So all around Victorian beaches strict regulations were enforced. In some areas bathing was banned altogether, at others bathing was allowed only if there was strict segregation of the sexes, and beaches were divided down the middle, men on one side, women and children on the other. Even in 1912 this was still going on at Williamstown. The girls had to bathe on the western side and the boys on the eastern.

On December 7, TRUTH printed this piece of verse:

Now Williamstown, in Grundy's gown, will segregate the soxes. How with his girl to have a bathe, poor Johnny it perplexes. Upon the sands she lonesome stands, Like Nancy Lee, she waves her hands But may not join her cobber. Chorus: She may not join her cobber.

Reading through the newspapers at this time it is fascinating to note how many decent people were genuinely horrified. Here's what Reverend T. Adamson had to say at the Methodist Conference of 1912, when mixed bathing had begun on some beaches.

"No modest woman can be associated with mixed bathing and no man who respects the opposite sex could take part in it. I know that arguments will be raised against me. Some excellent men have told me they have been associated with mixed bathing, enjoying it in company with their wives and daughters. It is interesting to hear them talk, but another gentleman refuses to be associated with the practices of mixed bathing. Married women ought to take up a strong position in this matter."

Even then bathing had been banned on Sundays, Good Friday, Christmas Day, and utterly forbidden in any way between 10 a.m. and 5 p.m. On reading the regulations and journals I couldn't understand why. If any immorality were to take place, surely it had a better chance in the dark.

Even THE ARGUS, on February 6, 1911, asked why "poor sweltering bodies were kept out of the water during the very hottest hours of the day".

It was the summer of 1912 when that strange term "sun bathing" first made its way into print. Some called it a "new form of worship". It was another practice that had come over from Sydney. It was amazing how many evil practices seemed to start in Sydney.

Even THE ARGUS, which was fairly liberal on most new ideas, was dead against it. On March 8, 1912, it said: "Lolling on the sand, either for individuals or for mixed groups should be regarded as strictly forbidden, not only by law, but by etiquette, which is often more effective than the law."

THE ARGUS held that sun bathing was a new form of idleness.

St. Kilda Council passed a law which directly forbade it. Many people were fined. One man was convicted just for standing on the beach in neck to knees and drying himself with a towel. This "no loitering" law was still on some council books, 30 years later. Most people ignored it.

Just when everything seemed to be getting better, on October 27, 1914, the St. Kilda Council brought out an incredible series of regulations. The beach was divided into areas. In some spots bathing was allowed only between 4 a.m. and 10 a.m. and others between 4 a.m. and 6 p.m., but on Sundays, Christmas Day and Good Friday, it wasn't allowed at all after 10 a.m.

There was a great protest meeting of local citizens at the Methodist school, and they decided that the following Sunday they would have, what we call to-day, a mass bathe-in.

More than 1,000 people turned up that Sunday morning, all defiantly determined to march into the water. Many of the protesters wore bathing togs under their overcoats, but sadly, God seemed to be against them. It was bitterly cold, and it began to rain. Only a few crusaders of vast courage were brave enough to go in.

But St. Kilda Council, disapproving the whole affair, refused to change the regulations. It wasn't until January 18, 1917, that mixed bathing became completely free, with Sunday hours up to 7 p.m.

For 15 years things were relatively quiet. At least we were allowed into the water. The next battle was what we didn't wear. Women started the trouble. The year 1932 saw the backless costume. A Congregational minister, the Reverend S. H. Cox, described it as "the hottest thing ever introduced on the beaches."

He said it was a return to the old paganism of the Epicureans, who lived only for the pleasures of life. The costumes would succeed in attracting the attention of the prurient minded, and would affect the essential modesty of the pure woman. Girls who wore these daring, disgusting, backless beach costumes were not the kind to uplift the moral tone of the community, nor, when married, to teach morals to their children.

Then, in 1933, Cr. C. E. Sheppard, president of Warringa Shire in Sydney, warned us of the possible shape of things to come. "Women soon will want to go into the water 'nude,'" said he. Costumes were backless and almost frontless. They consisted of a small brassiere attached to a pair of trunks by thin cords.

In 1936 the men were causing trouble with "roll or not to roll". The top half of male costumes buttoned on to the bottom half; alternatively, one could roll down the top to display a manly chest.

Anyone who dropped a shoulder strap was liable to a fine of £2.

In 1938 councils were starting to crack, but Cr. Levy of Brighton insisted that beaches were playgrounds for children of tender years, and some men were unsightly, "like hairy monkeys".

And so we progressed. Bikinis first appeared around 1954. The bikini was named after the H-Bomb on the theory that it was the ultimate. One couldn't really go any further. The South Coast Town Council gave them the go ahead for Surfers Paradise on February 19, 1955.

Yet they were still causing trouble in Melbourne as late as 1964. In November the 18,000-strong Victorian Federation of Catholic Mothers' Clubs asked all 19 councils to ban all bikinis on bayside beaches. The Federation's secretary, Mrs. Mary Cullen, said: "We definitely think that bikinis lower the resistance of young people to temptation, and contribute to immorality. I can't see anything attractive about bikinis. In my opinion they expose an ugly part of the body."

The SUN NEWS PICTORIAL ran a poll. Eighty per cent of readers were pro-bikini. Here were some of the antis.

"The bikini is a degrading garment, regardless of what the men on our councils say. Every women's club and society should take this up." — "Modesty", Frankston.

"I am for banning them and also short dresses. What else are they for but to produce undue sex in men." — G. Masel, Melbourne.

"Bikinis are one reason why my girls are molested these days. They also leave temptations open to our sons." — "Labor Mother", Belgrave.

Here were some of the pros:

"Is there an excess of depravity in native communities that mix together almost nude." — "Can't Bare It", Seaford.

"If a girl is young, shapely, and is prepared to exhibit 95 per cent of her body to appreciative male eyes, she is entitled to do so." — "Unashamed", South Melbourne.

"If bikinis expose an ugly part of the body, how can they be over-provocative." — "Amused", Burwood.

Regardless of council by-laws, regardless of warnings from the pulpit and ban-the-bikini requests, young people went right on wearing exactly what they wanted. And as they say on the radio stations, that is the news to this moment.

So I continue to sit on the beach, wearing my dark glasses, waiting to see what will happen next. I even suspect that the bikini is not the ultimate after all, and before this summer is over I might even be holding Aunt Hilda's worthy novel upside down.

Source: The Bikini, What Next?, Walkabout Magazine, January 1968. Accessible via the National Library of Australia's Trove database.
