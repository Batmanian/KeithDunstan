Melbourne's Evergreen Exhibition

11 min read.

First published 01 May 1964

A history of Melbourne's Exhibition Building, from the lavish international exhibitions of 1880 and 1888 through Sidney Myer's Depression-era Christmas dinner for 11,000 unemployed people to its 1963 revival as a trade fair venue.

YOU can have all your 20-storey filing cabinets and glass-houses. To my mind the best thing that has happened to Melbourne is the rebirth of the dear old Exhibition Building. Now in its 85th year, it is being treated with all the respect due to its size and grandeur. You see, the Exhibition was built at least 80 years ahead of its time, and only now has a city comparable with its own proportions. The Exhibition is booked out every week for more than a year ahead, and it is considered one of the six best halls of the kind in the world.

Yet, until recently, the Exhibition was the joke of Melbourne. It was called a monstrosity, a white elephant, and there was a very determined effort of the City Council to have it pulled down. On police rounds used to sit up at Russell Street police station and dream through the long night. Their dream was that the mammoth wooden Exhibition would catch alight. In my day this was considered to be the ultimate of stories, and we used to pray that we would be working the night it happened. It never did. There was even a popular notion that the building should come down for the very reason that it was only temporary in the first place. Temporary? For heaven's sake, it was built on solid limestone foundations, as solid as the Sydney Opera House.

Perhaps the biggest reasons for the resurgence of the Exhibition was the last international trade fair and the spending of half a million pounds on extensions which covered 64,000 square feet. What's more, the original grand main entrance was put back into use, just as it was in the old days. In many ways this trade fair of 1963 was most impressive, but, judging from the records, it didn't touch the great international trade fairs of 1880 and 1888. Just sit deep in your chair for a spell while I tell you all about it.

Back in 1880, we spent £350,000 on an exhibition, when the population of Melbourne was a mere, but very hearty, 200,000. The Exhibition Building, built especially for this show, cost nigh on a quarter of a million. The great nations of the world took part. There were ships from five navies out in the Bay. The fair opened with an enormous cantata specially composed for the occasion, which was sung by a choir of 900, accompanied by an orchestra of 90.

In those days exhibitions were all the rage. Victoria was terribly inspired by the big Paris exhibition of 1878. And besides, Sydney was putting on an international exhibition in 1879. However, the Melbourne Herald was able to point on the opening day, October 1, 1880, that the Sydney affair really was very hurriedly got up, and that its overseas exhibits were few and unimportant, whereas the opening of Victoria's first international exhibition was a spectacle "such as had never been witnessed in the history of the world in a community not 50 years old".

The Herald wasn't far out. Before the big opening procession, crowds spent all night on the Treasury steps. Window seats along Spring Street cost a guinea. The procession was led by the Governors of all the colonies in their carriages, followed by the Victorian Chief Secretary, Mr. Berry, in a carriage pulled by two white horses, 3,500 trade unionists, nine bands, 12 fire brigades, 2,159 troopers in their red jackets and 3,000 sailors from the British, French, Italian, German and Victorian navies. His Excellency the Marquis of Normanby opened the Exhibition. The choir sang its cantata. The sopranos wore blue sashes over white dresses and the contraltos red sashes. The Herald said: "The muslin caps of the ladies in contrast with the pale blue sashes, had the soft, delicate appearance of freshly-fallen snow."

And what of the exhibition? The main hall was the Avenue of Nations, Great Britain, Germany, Italy, Japan, France, U.S.A., Russia, Spain and the rest. Judging from the photographs you never saw such a collection of stuff, bird cages, Turkish baths, railway locomotives, railway carriages, biscuits, top hats, hand-powered washing machines, lawn mowers, pianos, billiard tables, reapers and binders, blunderbusses. But in the French exhibit there was something special, a painting by the celebrated Chevalier Jules Lefebvre, brought out just for the occasion, Chloe, the nude painting which stayed to become the beloved of Melbourne and eventually to occupy the place of honour in the saloon bar at Young and Jackson's hotel in Swanston Street.

The judges (male) seemed to treat her very kindly and awarded her a Gold Medal, their highest honour. Controversy about the frightful effect this picture of a naked woman could have on young minds did not start until Chloe went into the Museum in 1883 and the Sabbatarians started their complaints. Sadly, the exhibition of 1963 did not have an exhibit to equal the Chloe of 1880.

In seven months 1,330,000 people saw the show. It cost 2/6 to get in. In the second-class dining room, you could get a three-course meal for a shilling. Down below in the basement there were the All Nations Beer and Wine Cellars. Wine was 3d. a glass, and there you could sample beers and wines of Victoria, Germany, France, Austro-Hungary, Tasmania and the other colonies. The oyster bars were there, too.

If that exhibition was large, the exhibition of 1888 was tremendous. This was the Centennial Exhibition to mark the 100th anniversary of the first settlement on Australian soil. Sydney was doing nothing worthy of the occasion, so Melbourne decided to take it on. Anyway, Sydney's exhibition building had been burned down. The Victorian land boom was just coming into full flower. Money didn't matter.

Just to give you some idea of the size of the exhibition, the present building with its new extensions covers about 11 acres. For the 1888 exhibition they put up buildings, and fairly solid at that, which covered THIRTY-FIVE-AND-A-HALF acres. From the fountain north, they covered the entire Carlton gardens. Then they lit all this by electric light, something which was tremendously novel. The Argus said it cost £60,000. It was the largest single installation of arc lighting that had been attempted anywhere in the world, all run by steam engines that put the fear of God into the local fire brigades. Apart from the electric light, do you know what the real crowd-pleasers were? Typewriters, real American chewing gum and Mr. Edison's phonograph.

There were fireworks, and 30,000 went to Sandridge Beach to see the illuminations on the naval ships. And, of course, there was another cantata with another huge choir. This time, the exhibition commissioners brought out the conductor of the London Philharmonic, Mr. Frederick H. Cowen, engaged at a fee of £5000. That would be at least £15,000 to-day.

It was hard to keep up the standard. In 1889 at the Exhibition, 700 babies took part in the Grand National Baby Show. Regrettably, it was not a great success: 699 mothers did not agree with the choice of the judges, and there was such a riot that the 700th mother and baby had to be rescued by the police.

There were no more international exhibitions, but on January 18, 1892, the Exhibition had the World Championship Pull. There were tug-o'-war teams from Spain, Germany, Italy, Sweden, Wales, Scotland, Denmark, England, Australia, Norway, France, Ireland, New Zealand and the United States. Over 10,000 people packed into the building, and those who had bought tickets and couldn't get in tried to bash down the doors. The show went on for six nights and there were some terrible, agonising, moustache-twitching pulls that lasted for 45 minutes.

Ireland beat Australia in the final, and it was reported in the Argus that the local Irish patriots went mad. They threw their bowlers, umbrellas, everything into the air, and more than 1,000 stormed the stage.

There was another international pull in 1899, and Ireland again defeated Australia in the final. The idols of the period were 20-stone "Big John" Noonan of Ireland, "the strongest man in the world", and "Mighty Tom" Herlihy, also 20-stone, a Melbourne sugar carter. The story goes that Mighty Tom used to train by pulling single-handed against a draught horse.

Naturally, it had to be decided who indeed was the strongest man in the world, so an individual contest was organised between the two. Hundreds of pounds were laid in side wagers. The two men, Big John and Mighty Tom, took up their positions on wooden cleats, which were like a heavy ladder laid flat on the ground. Noonan had so much power in his legs that he often broke the rungs. It was said to be one of the longest and most agonising pulls in the history of the sport, but eventually Noonan outlasted Herlihy and retained the title. Noonan settled in Australia, and became the anchor man for the Carlton Brewery team. The story goes that "the strongest man in the world" had the easiest job in the brewery. This was to go round with a small can and top up the casks. After all, a strong man had to look after himself.

The Exhibition never recaptured those great days of the 'eighties. Melbourne was then a gay, unbelievably high-spirited city, but the crash of the land-boom was such a shock, such a disastrous lesson, that Melbourne acquired a mood of solid, dull respectability from which she has never quite recovered. At the Exhibition Oval there were balloon ascents, wheel races, but nothing on the grand scale. The Exhibition Building was too big for anything that Melbourne could turn on. Even the costs of painting it were crippling. One estimate 10 years ago was £50,000.

In 1919 it became an emergency hospital for influenza patients, but for my money Christmas Day, 1930, was the most remarkable in the history of the building. This was a depression year. "Susso," for the sustenance worker, was a new word in the language; the unemployed had reached a peak of 30 per cent of the work force.

It was wet and miserable, but this was the day when Sidney Myer, founder of Melbourne's biggest retail store, gave the greatest Christmas dinner the city had ever seen. He fed 11,000 of the poor and unemployed at the Exhibition Building. At first he planned to limit the dinner to 10,000, and he issued tickets to them, but when it was almost over he walked outside and found 1,000 people waiting there silently. So he invited them inside.

It was all highly organised. There was free travel on the trams for the ticket holders. Preparation for the great meal started at 7.30 a.m. The staff and executive of Myers worked voluntarily, waiting on the tables. The first sitting went through at 10 a.m. After that they came in hourly batches of 2,000. Many of the people had not eaten a decent meal for days. Each of them had a plate of ham and corned beef, part of a lettuce, three pickled onions, two bread rolls, two apples, one pound of Christmas cake, 1½ ounces of butter, peaches and ice cream, coffee and two glasses of beer or orange juice.

Altogether, 300 members of the Myer staff were on the job, and Mr. Myer himself constantly walked from table to table, making sure that everybody was fed. They ate, among other things, three-quarters of a ton of ham, half a ton of corned beef, half a ton of butter, and they drank 1,000 gallons of beer. One newspaper said: "Mr. Myer seemed disconcerted by the continuous gratitude shown him. At first he fled from the concerted cheering, but then he remained and stood with his head bowed."

During the war the Exhibition was taken over by the services, but my strongest memory of the old place goes back to public examinations. It was here that one sat for school leaving examinations. Whenever the paper was impossibly hard, there was time to examine the immense, lofty ceiling. This was rewarding, for it was decorated with luscious, mid-Victorian maidens. You must have seen the type. Such maidens used to decorate the dining rooms of our more ancient country hotels and Greek cafés. They had long hair, they were very bosomy, and they were plumply loaded with calories. They wore long negligées, sometimes superbly off the shoulder, and they appeared to be prancing through the clouds.

They are still on the Exhibition ceiling and better than ever, for they have been polished and restored. What's more, they have been defended by Mr. Eric Westbrook, director of the National Gallery, not so much for their artistic merit as for their curiosity value. Anyway, it would be like murder to get rid of those nice girls.

So, you see, the Exhibition Building has quite a history. Now it is always occupied, motor shows, ideal home shows, garden displays, industrial shows, electrical goods and balls; and for the World Power Conference, in 1962, it was the scene of the biggest sit-down dinner ever, in this country. There were 1,600 guests, 224 waiters and 45,000 pieces of crockery and cutlery. All the food came by convoys of trucks in hot boxes. There were carpets on the floor, long loose drapes from the ceiling and the old walls were softly illuminated by coloured lights. The old girl had never looked lovelier.

No longer now is there any possibility of the Exhibition Building being turned over to the wreckers. There is one excellent reason for this. She is making money.

In Melbourne we are a hard, practical people.
