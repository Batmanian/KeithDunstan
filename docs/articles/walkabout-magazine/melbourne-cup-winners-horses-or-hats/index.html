Melbourne Cup Winners: Horses or Hats?

13 min read.

First published 01 Oct 1966

A survey of the Melbourne Cup as spectacle as much as sport, from Jean Shrimpton's mini-skirted 1965 Derby appearance and the ritual of Cup Day toppers and fashion parades to the race's history, Carbine's 1890 record run, and Archer's 550-mile walk to Flemington in 1861.

I HAVE HEARD IT SAID that nobody can remember the name of the horse that won the 1965 Melbourne Cup. Actually it was a beautiful mare named Light Fingers.

However there are many who insist that the real winner was a London model named Jean Shrimpton, "the most beautiful girl in the world". Miss Shrimpton, you will remember, came out to be a judge in the "Fashions in the Field contest". At the Melbourne Cup, these times, we have a contest both for the horses and the ladies.

The advance publicity for Miss Shrimpton was most gratifying. She was to bring with her a superb wardrobe featuring the finest in Continental design.

Oh, horror! Miss Shrimpton made her first appearance at the Derby, three days ahead of the Cup, sans stockings, sans gloves, sans hat and with plain white shift four inches above the knee. As far as we were concerned this was the beginning of the mod look, and the deep shock in the members' stand at Flemington reverberated around the world. Nine out of 10 females present thought Miss Shrimpton looked utterly atrocious. Nine of 10 males thought she looked utterly gorgeous.

The news hit the front pages of every newspaper in Australia, and one London daily even put a man on the first aircraft, so that he could file a hem by hem report.

The following Tuesday, the "Shrimp" was a little more subdued. She appeared at the Cup in a neat suit, bag, hat and all, but with skirt still four inches above the knee. As she walked proudly down the lawn every head turned. She caused the sort of ripple or wake that a battleship would leave on Melbourne's Albert Park Lake. That's why some contended that the Shrimp won the '65 Cup.

This I refuse to concede. I still believe, hopefully, that horses (equinus) are more important than fillies (humanus) on the first Tuesday in November. After all, the stakes are still higher for the horses. First prize is $60,000, compared with $24,000 worth of prizes offered to ladies who win the various fashion contests.

Furthermore, Cup Day is almost a sacred day. We in Melbourne are one of few peoples who feel it perfectly proper that an official public holiday should be created for a horse race. I know people who chart their lives entirely by Melbourne Cups. For example, they wouldn't say: "My boy was born in 1925." They would say: "When was Jimmy born? Oh yes, Windbag's year." Then, are they saying now: "My little grandson was born in the Shrimp's year"? Certainly not. The year 1965 belongs entirely to Light Fingers . . . I hope.

But if Melbourne holds day-long holiday from most serious activities, the whole nation, as horses line up for the Big Race, stops dead for 10 minutes, just as if the film had been stopped in a silent movie. Stock exchanges cease to function, courts adjourn, schoolchildren in class suddenly produce transistors, taxis pull into the kerb if it is not already occupied, cities everywhere die so that people can listen to their radios or watch a set that is doing a direct telecast. Outback, diggers and drovers, shearers and swaggies stop to hear the fate of their five-bob wagers.

I know of only one set of related places where life goes on in the normal fashion. The first week in November is the deadly serious time of public examinations. On that Tuesday afternoon inevitably students are working through three-hour papers, dealing with such subjects as Metallurgy I or Biochemistry II. Once the doors of the examination halls are closed nobody goes in or out, and certainly cold-hearted university examiners would allow no interruptions.

Yet, although there is no radio, no announcement, every year it's the same. Somehow, by sheer magic, the result gets around to the students. By 2.45 p.m. everybody knows. Thoughts are back on metallurgy again and pens write more happily or despondently, as the case may be.

The betting is the thing. Very few of the population of 11 million do not have a stake in this horse race. They may not bet on any other day of the year, but it is essential to have a bet on the first Tuesday in November. Even in those days when there was no off-course tote, there was always an illegal off-course bookie just around the corner. It was said that no matter where you were, in the chamber of the Supreme Court, on the highest mountain, 600 feet down a mine, or even in gaol, it was possible to lay a bet on Melbourne Cup day.

Now all this behaviour may seem peculiar. Why are we so odd? Maurice Cavanough, historian of the Victoria Racing Club, has recorded that English racecourses have improved very little since the days of Charles II. The English like horses better than people, so they provide the best running surfaces in the world.

The Americans do things the other way round. They like people better than horses.

Their horses know nothing of beautiful springy turf, the racing is mostly on dirt tracks. However, the people are catered for superbly. One can hire a box for the day, be waited on with every kind of food and drink, and be given an attendant to spare one the effort of walking to the totalisator.

On Australian racetracks both horses and people are treated with kindness. The turf is springy and the grandstands are splendid; more than that, comforts are for everybody.

Let us dip into history. Although the Melbourne Cup is rather older, 1964 was the centenary of the Victoria Racing Club. In the 1860's this was still a pioneer country. Everybody was passionately interested in the horse. Even bushrangers, the Australian highwaymen, insisted only on the very best of horseflesh.

Much of the success of the Melbourne Cup is due to the first secretary of the Victoria Racing Club. He was an Irishman, and he had the unique idea, for 1864, that racing was for the working man. He built a bluestone stand for the cash customers, that was, for heaven's sake, better than the timber affair which had been provided for the members.

Furthermore he drained the marsh in the centre of the course, and threw that open to the public free. All Melbourne could come there for a picnic, a picnic that was a real carnival. All Melbourne? That isn't greatly exaggerated; there was never an entertainment for the masses like this. For the Melbourne Cup of 1880, 100,000 people were at Flemington racetrack. The entire population was only 282,000. So you see, better than one in every three people were at the races on that day. Over 85 years later, we have to struggle to make that figure with a population of more than two million.

Flemington was, and still is, a magnificent racecourse. Best of all, it is handy to the city. In the old days you could go by train, tram, public wagonette, drag, dogcart, trap or omnibus. This year, there are no public wagonettes, but there will be helicopters from the city every three minutes. They land right beside the second furlong post.

There is another reason for this adoration of the Melbourne Cup, and racing in general. The classic races in England have been weight-for-age affairs, for three-year-olds which all carry the same weight. The colt, therefore, which wins the Derby, and the filly that takes the Oaks, clearly, are the finest in the field.

So after these races they are retired to preserve the line, to propagate further champions. This was no good at all to Melbourne's working man. In the 1860's the city was full of Irishmen, bred out of the gold rush. They knew what poverty was like, and there was hunger everywhere to get rich quick. What odds could you get out of a weight-for-age race? You just had to look for the best horse, and the bookmakers knew that better than anybody.

Ah, but the Melbourne Cup, spread over an agonising two miles, was a completely unpredictable race. It still is an unpredictable race. Colts and fillies, three-year-olds, four-year-olds, seven-year-olds no end, have won it. The favourite has come home less than once in four times, and the odds are splendid.

So Australians adore it. We are the only people who put a handicap race on a pedestal above all others and, if you must have a greatest, we rate the Melbourne Cup as the greatest handicap horse race in the world.

As mentioned earlier, the prize money is $60,000, plus a gold three-handled loving cup worth $1,500. It is not a big sum by world standards, and you couldn't compare it with value in the grand old days of the Melbourne Cup. Take Carbine's year, 1890, when Melbourne was riding a land boom. The Victoria Racing Club put up the prize money to £10,000, and racing men in other parts of the working world could scarcely believe it. The working man then earned about £2 a week. To equal Carbine's year the V.R.C. would now have to put up the prize money to more than $200,000.

But 1890 really was a Cup year. Not only did Carbine carry a weight that has never been equalled, 10 stone five pounds, but he won in record time (3 min. 38½ secs.) and out of a huge field of 39. As Carbine returned, the owner, Donald Wallace, threw his arms around the horse's neck and hugged him. It was a most suitably emotional gesture, because not only had Carbine provided him with the 10,000 sovereigns first prize, but Mr. Wallace had won 30,000 in wagers as well. The trainer was so excited that he plucked hairs from around Carbine's tail and presented them to the ladies. No doubt many Melburnians still possess one of these valuable trophies, suitably mounted in a silver frame.

That was Carbine's year, but what about this year? Even if you cannot stand horses is it a lovely way to spend the day. No other sporting event is conducted in such beautiful surroundings. There are acres upon acres of flowers, four rows of them over half a mile long, all growing exactly as they are told to by the V.R.C., roses, poppies, pansies, ranunculi, cinerarias . . .

From the grandstand the view goes out for miles, across to the lofty glass towers of Melbourne, to the dockland, over the great green acreage of the course and down to the river, taking in the flags, the green umbrellas of the betting ring, the swirling colour of the crowd.

It all gets under way down the straight followed by more than 100 pipers, a tremendous sight. The members' car-park, once filled with fine carriages, now has a lush sprinkling of Rolls-Royces. It is always de rigueur in the car-park to serve a chicken-and-champagne lunch. A charming sight, of which one never tires, is to see a uniformed chauffeur, salad of ribbons on his chest, quaffing champagne.

As for the ladies, this is the fashion climax of the year, and the Cup brew becomes rather heady. One can always get away with rather more on Cup Day, if not quite as much, or little, as the Shrimp. Hats are not expected to be believable. One looks for absurdities built upon the head like threatening cumulo-nimbus, hats with a spinnaker like an America's Cup challenger, hats inspired by the wagon wheel and just as large, with feathers for spokes.

The desperately important thing is to be photographed, and every lady knows that between 11 a.m. and noon is the really serious time. No time can be wasted in the Champagne Bar; the thing to do is to walk up and down the Lawn, and keep walking. The tension gets tighter. Photographers, each with a cold, calculating social writer at his elbow, approach the lawn.

Oh, surely, surely, this time it's going to be me?

Then Flemington is one racetrack in Australia where men take their fashions seriously. And accent is also on the hat. To be a gentleman at Flemington on Cup Day it is rather essential to wear a grey top hat; the equally important accessories being a black or grey morning-suit with tails and striped trousers. And don't be mistaken, the lofty grey topper is not an easy hat to wear. It takes breeding and style to carry off a topper with the right flourish.

For the 1963 Cup, Gatum Gatum's year, the Soviet Ambassador, Mr. Loginov, was there complete with morning suit. Now was a gentleman who knew how to handle a topper. He had a particular charm as he raised it to the ladies; one would have suspected that he possessed an earldom at the very least.

So you place your topper at a slightly rakish, but certainly not fully rakish, angle, and parade across the lawn to the betting ring. There's a choice. You run your eye up and down the prices to see what the bookmakers are offering or you may place a bet on the tote. Or maybe you wander into the birdcage, where the stars of the occasion are being prepared for the big race. It is here that the racegoer discovers afresh that there is nothing in the world so beautiful as a thoroughbred horse.

Actually, the Melbourne Cup is the star attraction of a Spring Racing Carnival which gives away nigh on a million dollars in prize money. Melbourne looks to it as a moneyspinner, a magnificent bait for tourists. For several weeks there is not an empty bed to be had. There are Cup parties, Cup dinners, Cup shows, even a Cup ballet. The Elizabethan Theatre company turns on a Melbourne Cup ballet, where ballerinas play the rôles of Melbourne Cup horses. A restaurant in Collins Street gives its customers horseshoes taken from famous Cup horses. In a continental restaurant this might be regarded as a delicate suggestion that horse was well served for dinner. But here in Melbourne we know differently, a horseshoe is practically a religious symbol.

The town really does go gay. The streets are decorated with boxes of flowers and there is a floral competition between the major buildings. The big brewery in Swanston Street always puts on a very good display, and with pansies in its window boxes it looks a very kindly brewery. Yet, maybe, the banks of Collins Street are the keenest competitors; they fight out the prestige battle, pelargonium for pelargonium, orchid for orchid. The winners receive a small replica of the Melbourne Cup.

We mentioned that it is possible to put on a bet anywhere. The off-course system has been in operation in Victoria for five years. It is run by the Totalisator Agency Board. In every suburb, in every shopping centre, one sees the sign TAB. The object has been to make off-course betting infinitely respectable. TAB buildings, or many of them, now are as plentiful as churches. No seats are provided, but this is so as to discourage loitering. The turnover from the TAB is expected to be $140 million by 1967, and the profits are enormous. These are shared between hospitals and race clubs, so both sport and conscience are satisfied.

The racing share of profits from the TAB are being poured back into the Flemington racetrack, and, as the years go on, the stakes for the Melbourne Cup and other races in the Spring Carnival will become richer and richer. However it is not so much the money but the tradition that makes the Melbourne Cup.

For example, every schoolboy knows that the Sydney horse Archer was the first Cup winner in 1861. Indeed Archer trained in a park which was known then as St. Kilda Park. He was stabled at the Botanical Hotel. What's more, the Botanical Hotel is still there, admittedly much grander than it was in 1861. But this is the important point: Archer's stable is still there also, and unquestionably it will be preserved for ever by horse lovers, as a shrine.

And so it should be. In 1861 horses inevitably came from Sydney by boat, but Archer was different. With his strapper, Dave Power, he set out from Nowra on the New South Wales south coast and walked more than 550 miles of winding bullock track to Melbourne. He averaged about 26 miles a day through the bush, starting out from a small, prosperous sheep property called Braidwood. The story goes that both Archer and Power looked rather scraggy when they arrived in town, that October of 1861.

An unusual preparation; but one, we feel, that Percy Cerutty, the trainer of the mile champion Herb Elliott, would approve highly. So if we discover that any Sydney horse this year has walked the entire distance along the highway to Melbourne, we'll back him with every cent we can muster and our shirt as well.

But wait on. Isn't the Baroness von Thyssen, exponent of le dernier cri, coming to judge Cup fashions? In a way, isn't she the logical successor of the Shrimp, on a loftier plane?

Couldn't she win, too?
