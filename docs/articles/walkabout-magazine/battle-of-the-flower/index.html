Battle of the Flower

10 min read.

First published 01 May 1965

A tour of Australia's competitive gardening culture and its flower festivals, from Toowoomba's Carnival of Flowers and Ballarat's begonias to the Sydney Morning Herald's statewide garden contest and Western Australia's wildflower season.

THERE'S AN INSIDE STORY about Australians that has hardly ever been told. Thousands upon thousands of us are competitive gardeners, and there's nobody quite so devoted as a competitive gardener.

One sees evidence of this in almost any city suburb. One sees a fictional street; we'll call it Grandview Terrace. In the front garden of 22 Grandview Terrace there's a row of poinsettias (Euphorbia pulcherrima). Look next door in Number 20, and you can bet he is trying to out-pulcher his neighbour with an even better row of poinsettias.

If Number 12 has Magnolia grandiflora, undoubtedly Number 10 is doing his darndest trying to out-grandiflor him with a bigger and better tree. Smart newspapers and councils were quick to cash in on this, and they organised all kinds of garden competitions and flower festivals. Sometimes they worked on a natural local enthusiasm. At other times, let's be honest, they started the competitions because of a lack of local pride. In some areas you have to hold out big rewards to drag the Australian male back from the beach, the football, the races or the golf course.

Whatever the motives, the results have been extraordinary. In the past 10 years this country has become riddled with garden competitions and flower festivals.

My favourite flower town is Toowoomba, the home of the Carnival of Flowers, a city of astonishing local pride. After all, this is the place that first had the nerve to hawk to tourists cans labelled "Toowoomba Mountain Air". Just the stuff to take home for breathing in smoggy Melbourne or Sydney.

Toowoomba is the capital of Queensland's rich Darling Downs. It is 2,000 feet above sea level and everything, including the climate, is perfect for gardening. The soil there is a gorgeous brown, just like chocolate cake. You can keep your fancy fertilisers, Toowoomba doesn't need any.

The Carnival of Flowers takes place from September 18 to September 25. Here almost everyone has a beautiful garden, and the gardens competition is one of the most deadly serious in the country. The winning entries are nigh on perfection, not a weed in sight, poppies, stocks, tulips, everything growing precisely according to instructions, almost as good as the pictures on the seed packets.

However, this is not just a domestic thing. The shops, hotels and business houses are in it too. They have a window competition, so that all the shops along Ruthven St. are filled with the most elaborate displays, complete with working waterfalls and banked masses of flowers. So they fight out this competition poppy for poppy, ranunculus for ranunculus.

My next favourite flower town is Ballarat, in Victoria, also high up, 1,437 feet. Ballarat can't quite match Toowoomba with its domestic gardens, but its public gardens are superb. Late February and early March, Ballarat has its Begonia Festival. Nobody grows begonias quite like Ballarat. If you have seen them anywhere else, they are perhaps like saucers. In Ballarat they are big as dinner plates, and there are over 100 varieties of them, deep reds, pure whites, fragile pinks, all with a soft, luminous quality.

They are grown in glasshouses and they require precisely the correct method of ventilation and watering. Come the Begonia Festival, they are put in great banks, and every one of the heavy blooms is wired up to give the most perfect appearance.

Actually you could say they were organised like a display of so many Paris hats. I don't think there is a more beautiful bloom. If ever I am tempted to midnight larceny of somebody else's flowers, it's at the Begonia Festival. Of course, Toowoomba, too, has its garden competitions, all divided into various sections, amateur, novice, industrial, and so on. A regular winner of the industrial section is the Ballarat Water Commissioners' Case Factory, out at Kirk's Reservoir.

There you have the plain factory shed, but outside there is a slope down to a creek, a rustic bridge, water lilies and great masses of dahlias, marigolds, cannas, petunias, red-hot pokers, all presented with the tidiness of England's Kew Gardens. Tourists are out there all day.

Another good festival is the Grafton Jacaranda Festival. If ever you are in northern New South Wales between October 30 and November 7, move heaven and earth to see this one. Just possibly, the jacaranda is the most spectacular of all flowering trees, but it seems to be even happier and bigger around sub-tropical Grafton than anywhere else.

The jacaranda loses its leaves in late winter. However, just before the new leaves appear, out come these clouds of bell-shaped purple flowers. In Grafton they are in people's gardens and in every street. The place to go is Jacaranda Avenue. There the trees meet overhead and, if nature is dutifully falling in with the timing of the Festival Committee, there's the astonishing effect of a complete purple tunnel, purple overhead and purple all over the road where flowers have fallen. The effect is so lovely on the ground that you feel you ought to walk through on your hands, so as not to disturb anything.

Of course, it is not only Grafton. New South Wales, more than any other State, is festival happy. Altogether they have 57 annual festivals, and of these 27 are floral. One could almost go on a year-round walkabout or flowerabout and barely get a week off. Bellingen has its Azalea Festival, Batlow an Apple Blossom Festival, Blackheath a Rhododendron Festival, Bowral its tulips, Bargo wattle blossom, Glen Innes roses. At Goulburn it is the Lilac Time Festival, at Byron oleanders, Orange for cherry blossom and in Broken Hill they have the Festival of the Sturt Pea.

We could go on and on, to Port Macquarie for the Carnival of Pines, to Tumut for the Festival of the Falling Leaf, to Tenterfield for the Festival of the Willows, and Sydney would be terribly hurt if we failed to call in at the Waratah Festival, October 3 to October 10. The big deep-red waratah is the State emblem.

One always gets into trouble on these occasions, so for all those numerous floral festivals unmentioned here, I do pray forgiveness.

Of course, if you prefer your flowers au naturel, then Western Australia is the place. The number appears to go up every time one hears about it, but they do boast of between 6,000 and 7,000 species. The south-west, according to some geologists, was one of the first parts of the earth's crust to become solid, 1,500 million years ago. Maybe this has something to do with it, but there are few parts of the world with such a range of wild flowers.

Just to name-drop, there's the red kangaroo paw with its strange plumage, a completely fantastic flower and the State emblem. There's the cowslip orchid, a delicate, lovely thing of pure yellow, or the famous blue leschenaultia, which covers the gravels of the Darling Range... and they are blue, an astonishing wonderful blue. Wildflower time is mid-August to mid-November and there are one-day, three-day and even six-day wildflower tours organised. You can go to places like the Yanchep and National Parks, Mundaring Weir, Canning Dam, Serpentine Dam and Lake Leschenaultia. The colour film companies think it's all very wonderful.

Of course, part of the enthusiasm for festivals is due to the newspaper garden competitions. The Courier Mail runs a big competition in Brisbane, so does the Chronicle in Toowoomba, the Advertiser in Adelaide, the Courier in Ballarat, and many others, but the biggest competition of all is run by the Sydney Morning Herald. It covers the whole State, divided into 11 zones. So the judges are on the go all year round, travelling thousands of miles by car and chartered aircraft, often landing on the rough paddock aerodromes of the outback.

And that's an intriguing point about the competition, the variety. Often the judges are impressed and inspired by beautiful gardens they find in arid, drought-stricken country, literally "back o'Bourke", by bore-water and the devoted attention of the woman of the house. On the other hand the judges have come across some little gems of pocket-handkerchief gardens in the backyards of Sydney industrial suburbs like Redfern and Leichhardt.

The competition has been designed, craftily, to bring in everybody, with sections for big and little gardens, for the champion gardeners and the novice gardeners. There's even a "Good Neighbour" section for three neighbours co-operating together in a fenceless setting. The prizes range from 100 guineas down to three guineas.

The Sydney Morning Herald started its competition in 1957, expecting to get, maybe, 500 entries. There were 1,000, and the interest and rivalry among gardeners was almost frightening.

The winner of the only garden section that year was Mr. W. W. Kirwan, of Wahroonga. He opened his garden for charity and 16,000 visitors went through in one week-end. That has been the pattern ever since. As soon as the prizes are announced, week-end motorists by the thousand head in that direction, and the charities do very nicely indeed. The organisers say that, despite the inevitable stiletto heels, surprisingly little damage is done.

The pattern of flower enthusiasm doesn't change much. In South Australia, come early April, there's the Aldgate Autumn Leaves Festival in the Adelaide Hills, and it's always the custom to illuminate the trees at night with electric light bulbs among the leaves. At Port Augusta, in May, there is the Poinsettia Festival, but the big show is the Flower Day at the Adelaide Festival of Arts. This comes in alternate years and the next will be in March, 1966.

Then the whole city is turned out in flower, in window-boxes, in the great sweep of King William St. and North Terrace. There's the floral carpet in front of the War Memorial, and one can always enjoy the spectacle of elaborately-hatted ladies on their hands and knees in the city streets, arranging artistic dolls' houses and Australian flags entirely constructed from blossoms and other wires.

With all this rash of festivals and garden competitions, what does it do for the plain, old-fashioned, humdrum gardener? I have found that there is nothing quite so embarrassing as to be caught in the midst of a flower festival, or to discover that the fellow three doors up has just carried off the Grand Domestic Gardens Championship.

For those trapped in this fearful plight I can only suggest two solutions. The first is to concrete the garden right over. This need not be as harsh as it sounds; there are very attractive shades of concrete available.

Then you put tubs containing shrubs, hydrangeas and such in charming positions all round your concrete garden. Tubs can be tiresome, you say. True enough, but there are nurseries that do a special trade in this sort of thing, just like nurseries that look after indoor plants in office glasshouse buildings. They visit once a fortnight, say, on alternate Mondays and swab the tubs about. They might change your hydrangeas for azaleas, making sure that something is always in bloom. Your garden space can be one of those services brought to you like the milk and the daily paper.

The second solution is rather more attractive. I have a friend who put forward the idea that gardens, as we know them, didn't really come until the Reformation. When a man grows a handsome hedge, put down a handsome flower bed or a glorious formal garden, here was a sign that he was wealthy enough to afford unproductive labour. So my friend merely planted his garden with Australian trees, ferns and shrubs and let them rip. Eventually his garden became a genuine piece of Australian bush, with no watering, no supervision. After long periods of inactivity I have explained to my wife that this is my plan, but rarely is it received with much enthusiasm.

But there's no doubt that the general interest in gardening here, particularly in other people's gardens, is an astonishing thing. We had the case two years ago when Ripponlea, one of Melbourne's last formal gardens, was thrown open to the public for the first time. Here were 10 acres with little waterfalls, lily ponds, ornamental bridges, rose gardens, grottoes, the lot. Over 30,000 visited it in one afternoon. The crowd was so great that the garden was practically trampled under. Ambulance men treated 12, and one gentleman had a heart attack. Yes, our interest in gardens is growing.
