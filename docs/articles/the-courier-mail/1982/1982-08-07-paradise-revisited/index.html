Paradise revisited

6 min read.

First published 07 Aug 1982

Returning to Surfers Paradise after nearly 30 years, Keith Dunstan finds the fibro weekenders of the 1950s replaced by white high-rise towers, 'Inspect Now' signs and unsold apartments, and weighs whether the Gold Coast is better for it.

THERE should be a gentle orientation period before making a visit to the Gold Coast. The cultural shock was too much for an old hand.

It was nigh on 30 years ago that we used to visit our mates at Surfers. They had an utterly classic weekender right on Main Beach, a yabbie [?] whiff from Dick Cavill's Surfers Paradise Hotel.

It was a combination of fibro, wooden lattice, glass louvres, tired galvanised iron, and a view out front of the greatest beach on earth.

You could just walk across the dirt road to the water. The old house — call it Lean-To-Towers — always had a quarter inch of sand on the floor and a dozen people sleeping around.

You'd never lock it. Funny, it was an insult to lock anything in those days, you even left your car key in the ignition.

Suddenly the boom came. An enthusiast offered 4500 pounds for Lean-To Towers. FORTY-FIVE HUNDRED POUNDS! Did you hear that everybody? Ye Gods, it was an offer no sane person could refuse.

A few months later Lean-To-Towers went for 12,000 pounds, then 25,000. Now there's not a trace, a towering white thing is on the spot. The underground garage has electronic security gates and you can get in only if you get the OK from up aloft.

I think it was some time around 1973 we started making gags that Surfers was beginning to look like Miami. Friends, it is not a joke any more. It IS Miami, even more imposing.

I came over the Southport Bridge, round the bend on to Main Beach and that's when it hit. It always was a special moment, a sight of astonishing beauty, and it still is. Now you see all those towers, all white, all like ice trays out of a refrigerator.

You get the terrible feeling that someone might pull a lever and you will get showered with a hundred million ice blocks.

They have beautiful names, Aegean, Aquarius, Talisman, Biarritz — they should call Biarritz 'Hamburger House' — it looks like a pile of 200 whopper burgers.

Then there's Focus, like the Tower of Pisa, only three times the size. Actually it does seem to have a lean to it. Push it a little harder and it will become a tourist attraction.

Peninsular, still a crane job, right in the heart of Paradise, more than 40 floors up, is terrifying and reminds one of pictures of Moscow University.

Then I found Sun Tower. That almost brought a tear to the eye . . . funny little Sun Tower, only eight floors. Was it 10 or 12 years ago that they built Sun Tower? It was a sensation at the time. They'd never go higher than that.

In mid-afternoon we called on an old friend, a Victorian probate refugee. He is almost the top ice block in one of the ice block trays. He was in a state of euphoria, watching the Victorian football match-of-the-day.

'Where else,' he said, 'can you take in a view like that and watch the VFL match-of-the-day at the same time?'

Indeed the set did appear to have merit, but one said: 'What is happening here?'

'Inspect Now,' is the theme message of the Gold Coast. All the towers have the label 'Inspect Now.' There even seems a special urgency about it, 'For God sake — come in NOW — if not sooner'.

My probate reffo conceded that the real estate scene was shocking. He thought there ought to be a stronger word than shocking but he didn't have his Roget Thesaurus to look it up.

He pointed out tower after tower after tower that was empty, no buyers in sight. 'And look,' he said, 'that's Contessa, a hole in the ground, supposed to be 32 floors.

'I reckon they've spent a million already, now it's on hold.'

Contessa, another charming name, one thought. Obviously it implied the bare-footed Contessa, but I bet they'd allow no bare feet in that foyer.

But even if the Gold Coast has a 1000 unsold apartments strangely Paradise still has an atmosphere of wet concrete and even on Saturday afternoon chundering concrete waggons were on the job, as if not a second was to be lost before they produced another 1000 unsold apartments.

Inspecting Now, is the perfect weekend pastime and the better buildings have an exquisite 'Display Unit' on show. The 'interior decor' usually has been done by some fancy Melbourne firm.

There are the vases of dried flower arrangements, the refined prints which so delicately marry with the color of the curtains and always there are copies of Architectural Digest left casually on the occasional tables.

Not to be missed is the display in the Peninsular. This building is not even slightly finished. They take you on a labyrinthine obstacle course of wet concrete, dripping walls, and suddenly you are in this anti-probate Eldorado.

One apartment was decorated in passion pink, pink walls, pink carpet with those refined prints to match.

Another was in the most delicate duck egg blue. One couldn't help but speculate what a noble job the hands of five grandchildren could do in half an hour.

However the agent on the job, Ken Bandy [?], insisted all was going well — 245 units, 216 sold and 12 actually sold twice.

However if you have any spare money I'm sure you can still pick up a penthouse for $700,000.

This is the vital question: Is the Gold Coast better now than it was 25 years ago? Oh yes, it has an undeniable throbbing vitality. One says hesitantly that ice block towers have to be better than our sandy, tumbledown fibro weekenders.

And yet . . . and yet, what happens now? Doesn't this council ever know when to stop? Victor Ferris [?], an expert on the environment, says the problem is not so much the buildings, but the traffic.

The area is fast strangling itself. Even in early August it moves past in a never-ending tide of Mercedes, Jaguars, Commodores, Campers, Caravans, Toyotas, Datsuns, Fairlanes, Rolls-Royces and you near die of starvation waiting to get across the road.

So the thought comes, how it will be when the boom once again clicks back into automatic and all the 'Inspect Nows' are filled.

Yet I love Surfers. There is always something superbly vulgar to gaze upon. I'd tone it down a bit.

Is it really necessary to make it so besotted with signs? Do we have to have advertising on the backs of seats, real estate men telling us to buy even on the sides of bus shelters?

But wait, look at that astonishing punk blonde, her hair like a peroxide dish brush and that 50-year-old mama in the floral matador suit. Her behind is so starved for room it looks like a split pumpkin. Then that chap over there in the beautifully cut pale cream slacks, the white shoes, the red shirt with the alligator on the left breast, the Cartier watch — he has to come from Hunter's Hill, Sydney.

On the beach this morning, before the wind became a little chilly, there were all those girls, their bare nipples brazenly pointing up to the sun. Absolutely not tolerated in California.

If you did that at Malibu, Santa Monica or Santa Barbara you would shock them out of their minds. All right to sniff coke, pump heroin, carry a Saturday night special, mug your auntie, but steady on, dropping your bra on the beach — that's going too far.
