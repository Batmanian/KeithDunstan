The Australian Stage

46 min read.

First published 1972

Traces the knocking of the Australian stage from Lola Montez's 1855 Spider Dance controversy and Harry Seekamp's Ballarat horsewhipping through The Triad's savage music and theatre criticism, Nellie Melba's battles with John Norton, Frank Thring's career as a drama critic, the hostile reception given to Australian plays including Summer of the Seventeenth Doll and The One Day of the Year, the Katherine Brisbane libel case, and David Williamson's The Removalists, ending with a collection of ABC-era music criticism.

One of the first to suffer was the lovely Lola Montez. Lola was born in Limerick in 1819 of an Irish mother and a Spanish father. She was christened Marie Gilbert, but during her baby days her mother called her Dolores. This was shortened to Lola.

After an unsuccessful early marriage Lola toured Europe to become one of the most celebrated courtesans of her day. She was the lover of Liszt in Poland, mistress of Louis of Bavaria; a whip-carrying, cigar-smoking beauty, famed for her fiery temper.

She came to Melbourne in 1855 and did her celebrated Spider Dance at the Theatre Royal. None of the contemporary accounts describe precisely what Lola did during her Spider Dance, or how much bare flesh she actually put on view. The newspapers, apparently, were so shocked, they had no pure words which could possibly describe what they saw.

William Kelly in Life in Victoria has described how the diggers adored her. The Montez Spider Dance was always sold out and she would fill every corner of all the houses where she played. He wrote that before the show every face was radiant with anxiety, pinned in watchfulness, every ear at full cock to catch any sound denoting the approach of the longed-for moment. They would wait so long until finally a digger would rise in the house and call out: 'Come on, Lola; damn it, come on, ole girl, before the moon goes down.'

The diggers loved her, but the very formal Argus did not. After her opening night, 20 September 1855, at the Theatre Royal it printed this notice:

Why the stage of the Theatre Royal should have been selected for a public exhibition of the kind which took place last evening we cannot imagine. Although far from admiring the entertainments with which the public of this city have for the past [several months] been nightly treated at what we once hoped to call our national theatre, we have hitherto abstained from commenting on them; but after the exhibition of last evening it would be criminal on our part to continue to be silent, when such a course of conduct might be construed into a merely negative objection to such scenes as that presented by Madame Montez. We feel called upon to denounce in terms of unmeasured reprobation, the performance in which that lady last night figured. We do not intend to enter into details. The crowded attendance at the theatre will understand our motives for not doing so. The conclusion, however, at which we arrive is this: by such performances, they have no right to impose on respectable ladies by inviting their attendance; and if scenes of the kind are ever to be repeated, we consider that the interference of the authorities is imperatively called for to put it down as a nuisance, and as utterly subversive of all decency and public morality.1

Next day Lola replied. She said the 'piety and ultra-piety' of the Argus would prevent them from even taking her letter about the Spider Dance, so she sent it to the Herald:

The Dance in question is essentially a national one, witnessed with delight in Spain by all classes and both sexes, from the Queen to the peasant. I am not the inventor or originator of the dance in question, but simply the exponent of a great national spectacle.

I do not perform the dance to minister to any morbid taste or for immoral representations. I have always looked upon it on strictly artistic grounds. I have performed it before the most distinguished audiences in the world; and I throw back with scorn the insinuation of the Argus, that I come forward to pander to a morbid taste for indelicate representations. Theirs is the indelicacy who look at my art with an arrière pensée in their minds; let them remember that the symbol of innocence is the naked Eve, and that since the days when Eve surveyed herself in the fountain, works of art, however portrayed, have by their very character been sacred from the ribaldry of the licentious or uninitiated, for example, the Venus de Medici, and Power's Greek Slave.

I shall be back at my post tomorrow night and shall there take a course which will test the value of the opinion advanced in the paragraph of the Argus above alluded to. LOLA MONTEZ, Imperial Hotel.2

But it did not stop there. Several days later a Dr Milton went to the City Court and demanded of the Mayor that a warrant be issued against all repetition of the performances of Madame Lola Montez at the Theatre Royal 'in the name of an outraged community'. He announced that her Spider Dance reached 'the extreme of indecency' and was 'too disgraceful to be tolerated in a civilized community'.

The sensitive doctor had been outraged by the mere thought of Lola reaching the extreme of indecency, for he had not seen the performance. So the Bench decided that it could not issue a warrant, and his information was not properly supported enough to take any action, for her last performance was that night. After all the fine publicity the Theatre Royal was packed. At the finish of her Spider Dance she came forward, thanked the crowd for their support and asked them if they had noted any indecency in her performance. There was an uproarious cheer.

According to the Argus she had toned the act down and she did not look quite so Eve-like that night as on the first night. The crowd loved it when she referred to an 'Antony and Cleopatra' farce, and she referred to Dr Milton as her future [husband].3

She unquestionably evoked powerful feelings in one Thomas Callaghan whom the Argus described as a frightful-looking old man, who was caught chasing Lola across the stage at the Theatre Royal. After his arrest he told the police he was so violently in love with Lola he couldn't resist the opportunity of throwing himself at her feet and declaring his passion.4

From Melbourne Lola went on tour, first to Bendigo and then to Ballarat. Her appearance at the Victoria Theatre, Main Road, Ballarat, on 16 February 1856, caused a sensation. After the opening night a letter in the Ballarat Times, under the fictitious signature of 'Civis', condemned the Spider Dance and said Lola was notorious for her immorality.

Lola in reply made a speech from the stage of the Victoria Hotel Theatre in which she attacked the editor of the Ballarat Times, Mr Harry Seekamp. She said: 'The great gentleman attacks poor little innocent me. I am called notorious for my immorality. While he was at my house the sherry and port and champagne were never off the table. You all know Mr Seekamp is a little fond of drinking. I was a ten-pound note out of pocket by Mr Seekamp but I was a little green at the time. Mr Seekamp told me that the miners were a set of ---- hogs. He told me that he was a great man in Ballarat; and could do as he liked. He said: "I am going to be made a member of the Legislature."'5

Melbourne Punch commented:6

Up at Ballarat, the good
Montes went to battle,
And, in course, she rais'd a tempest
With her gusto tact.
For a certain Mr Seekamp,
(so she told her friends),
Had aboosed her spotless name, and
Had done so for his selfish ends.
He had been her guest in Melbourne,
He had drunk no end of wine,
He's a little fond of drinking,
Said his green and genial lady.

'And of the champagne he swallow'd
Ten pounds, at the least, the cost is,
When he's in a state of licker—
This here good for nothing man—
Then his wife performs his duty
Well as any woman can.'
'He's to be a legislator,
This great man at Ballarat,
And he called you hogs, and
Diggers, what d'ye think of that!'

The evening following Lola's speech Harry Seekamp called at the United States Hotel, where she was staying, next door to the Victoria Theatre. As soon as she heard Mr Seekamp was in the building she came downstairs with a riding whip in her hand. She had drawn it as a prize at a 'Fancy Bazaar' only a few hours before.

She laid into Harry Seekamp with 'hearty goodwill', with heavy blows across the shoulders. But he had come prepared with his own horsewhip. When Lola attacked him he hit her back. This was too much for the Diggers in the bar and they drew their revolvers. Harry Seekamp might have been killed if the police had not arrived. The Geelong Advertiser correspondent commented: 'I need not say much more on the transaction, the more so as the parties engaged in the affair are both of them rather eccentric and but little desirous to be judged by ordinary rules.'7

All this was first-rate publicity. The Victoria Theatre from then on was crowded and the applause for Lola was deafening. Every night the Diggers showered the stage with small nuggets. A new street was built at the corner of Eureka Street; it was named in her honour. Harry Seekamp had no such streets built for him.

Yet the Advertiser correspondent was not all censure. He said the Spider Dance was 'unexceptionable', the kind of thing that took place on the stage in England and never caused any fuss.

Lola had Harry Seekamp committed for trial, to be held in Geelong on 10 March. Bail was fixed at £300 and two sureties of £150. She also laid information against him for publishing an unregistered journal.

Seekamp was arrested and put in jail. An editorial, written by Harry himself, appeared in the Ballarat Times. As an example of editorial blast with all organ stops out it was magnificent.

Seekamp was arrested at midnight on 25 February on a warrant signed by J. Daly Esq., P.M., Chairman of the Local Court, on the information of Lola Montez. Said the editorial:

Let but the practice of J. Daly Esq. be affirmed and sanctioned, and no man is secure from being torn from his home at midnight, and compelled to herd with felons and sleep with vermin in the abominable [log jail] of Ballarat and thus the first principles of the British constitution are wantonly outraged. The boast and glory of Britons that they are free men until they have been proved and found guilty by a jury of their peers are utterly gone, so far as this part of the world is concerned.8

Lola had left the country before this action could be brought to court, but the fuss was still not over, for there were a series of libel actions between Lola's lawyer, Edward John Lewis, and Harry Seekamp.

The magistrate dismissed one action brought by Lewis as 'frivolous and vexatious'. Seekamp was delighted and at the bottom of his report in the Times on 1 April he added '(serves the dirty, petty-fogging Jew informer, perfectly right Ed.T.)' This started yet another action until finally the Circuit Court, before Mr Justice Barry, found Seekamp guilty and he was fined £100, to be imprisoned until the fine was paid.

One has to look hard for equally spectacular cases of the face-whipping of critics, although some critics have had their faces slapped. Perhaps almost as fiery as Lola Montez was Ana Raquel Satre, who came to Australia in 1962. Some newspapers called her 'a raven-haired thirty-one-year-old beauty from Uruguay'. Woman's Weekly called her 'the youthful combination of Sophia Loren and Maria Callas, an opera producer's dream'.

The Courier Mail in Brisbane on 19 February gave a special criticism of a production at the Brisbane Theatre. The critic, Dr V. Lovelock, said: 'The part of Alfredo was taken by Franco Bianchi who gave an interpretation of considerable promise. At his stage, however, one does sense some immaturity and lack of experience. ... He tends not infrequently to sing flat (in a weakness Madame Satre as Violetta set him the example) and to produce hard tone in forte.'9

That night Madame Satre chose her moment during the interval of 'Ariadne on Naxos' at Her Majesty's. She did not appear in the opera, but she sat in the stalls during the first act, wearing dark glasses. At the interval she left her husband, Patrick Bashford, and went upstairs to the foyer. As she told reporters later: 'I sent a message to Dr Lovelock to meet me in a private ante-room. I did not want to make too much of a scene. I wanted to fix him in private. When he joined me I used some words which I have learnt in English and I chose them deliberately.

'Then I took my glove and hit him hard on each side of his face. I had planned to punch him on the nose with my heavy turquoise dress ring, which I wear on my right fist, but I decided to spare him that. I did not want to make him bleed. I did it because he infuriated me with the insults he included in his criticisms of my singing.

'And,' she said, 'that tenor wasn't even singing flat. He was sharp if anything. It is too ridiculous to be subjected to criticisms in Brisbane, where young people are still guided by [limited standards].'

Reporters asked her if she had any regrets about slapping Dr Lovelock.

'None whatsoever,' she said. 'I did it because I genuinely believe it is worth while writing letters to these critics, objecting to their insults. Dr Lovelock is the second one I have hit. I hope it won't be necessary for there to be a third.'

As for Dr Lovelock, at the Queensland Conservatorium, he said: 'I've had my face slapped. It's the first time I've heard such language. I accepted it as gracefully as I could under the circumstances.'10

Others, rather than horse-whipping or face-slapping, sought retribution against their critics in the courts. One such man was the renowned tenor Philip Newbury. The Triad magazine in its issue of 10 July 1916 said that Newbury repels and attracts, and attacked his individual voice and style of performance. It was contended that the Triad, by its criticisms, had held Philip Newbury up to ridicule and contempt. Newbury was a singer of great reputation, and his livelihood depended on his being able to gain employment. If he were held up to contempt and ridicule, management would hesitate to employ him. The jury decided that this had happened. The Triad had to pay £500 plus costs.11

The Triad, over a long period, was, apart from the Bulletin, the only journal which gave criticisms at length of art, music, books and plays. It was consistently merciless. 'The Maid of the Mountains' with Gladys Moncrieff was one of the great stage successes of the century. Here is The Triad's criticism for 10 March 1921:

With its youthful jigs, and shudders of Carmen on Savoy operatic lines, 'The Maid of the Mountains' suggests Bizet re-written by Gilbert and O'Sullivan. The music sets coryphees stepping to trim measures, but it is always vaguely echoic of things that one shouldn't have heard before. Superiorly one may say that the orchestra isn't over-whelmed by the virtuosities of the composer, Mr Fraser-Simon, though Mr Andrew MacCunn is there with his white gloves and his gliding baton to create the hallucination of unparalleled business in hand. Whenever J. C. Williamson Ltd. is stirred by the ambition of providing a musical evening, it usually installs Mr MacCunn, whose very presence is a lullaby to one's critical feelings. He sits there in his seat, with his clothes almost painted upon him, his face set in a rock-like rigour that is born of Scotch righteousness, and an unreasonable youth of oatmeal whilst his back, reeking with the pride of clan and the witchery of tone he beckons into existence, is considered sufficient to present the performance with a certificate of character. Mr MacCunn has a perfect back for a musical director.

But to return to the 'Maid'. What a figure she is, in that high mountain path leading to the robbers in the manufactured moonlight of Mr Oscar Asche's! Likewise packed with all the colours that she has been educated — or crammed — Gladys Moncrieff, playing the Maid, deceives one here. Her bobbed hair and lady-like behaviour serve to thicken the ambush from which she springs the sad tidings of a half-hearted conversion to a Spanish voluptuousness and temper. Miss Moncrieff sings both richly and well, but there is no dark enchantment or flexibility about her acting. She is not graphic but oleographic. Indeed her impersonation may be only a producer's invention. It is unfortunate for Miss Moncrieff that she possesses in the stage-lover, Baldassare, so bald an interpreter as Mr Stage Freeman. The actor is convicted of gross impersonation as soon as he opens his mouth. He is only the shadow of a bandit's shadow.12

Another time, on 10 June 1924, Triad described her singing the old 'honey and butter ballad', 'I was Dreaming':

She sang it badly. There was about as much tenderness in her rendering as there is in a musical box wound up to perform nightly, and at slightly reduced rates for matinées. Miss Moncrieff might just as well have been singing 'I was Playing Bridge one Evening'.13

Most of the great names took a hiding. Here was a criticism of Peter Dawson. It seems remarkable he went on to such a great career.

On the 12th of November [1915] I sat out the first part of Mr Peter Dawson's Concert in the Sydney Town Hall. A second hearing of this vocalist merely served to corroborate the impression I formed last month — of his voice; his method, his interpretation. Furthermore at this concert there was an extraordinary epidemic of out-of-tuneness on the part of the performers. ... Mr Dawson sang the Buffo Song 'La! Factotum' from Il Barbiere (Rossini). In this there was the usual muscle-tightening and forcing of the voice, which results in the baritone quite frequently singing sharp. He also missed much of the humour of this number, suffering the while from a stiff inelastic accompaniment. He then announced he would sing an excerpt from Rigoletto, in which he sang sharp almost throughout, and his phrasing was grossly inartistic. His tones were forced until his face became a flaming red. As a second recall he gave Purcell's 'Passing By', which was sung with an entire lack of refinement and with coarse phrasing. 'Till I die' is not an improvement on 'till I die'.

As I pay for my seats, refusing to be the uncomfortable guest of my entertainers, I availed myself during the interval of the open door and somehow forgot to return to the Town Hall.14

That review was by the editor of The Triad, Charles Nalder Baeyertz. Yet even Dame Clara Butt did not fare any better. She must have found interest in this 1921 criticism:

Dame Clara Butt must be a problem to herself and an even more complicated problem to the musical critic. Ernest Newman, that most discriminating of English critics, has never completely solved the riddle constructively although he had probably written more destructive things about her than most of the scribes. He admitted one thing however, in this cleverly insulting phrase, that she did sing. The same criticism holds good for all time. Dame Clara sings mostly rubbish, shop ballads of the worst type which must bring her in a good fat royalty so fearful are they. She sometimes sings without any taste and sometimes with great violent, vulgar outbursts of vocal noise which is noisome to the musician and balm to the British public. She mis-manages the wonderful organ of hers in such a manner that makes one shudder at its potential or occasional ugliness.15

Allan Wilkie enchanted Australian audiences for more than fifteen years with the consistent quality of his Shakespearean productions, but month after month he received his mauling from Triad. In 1910 there was a column headed 'Allan Wilkie and — er — Shakespeare'. It pointed out that the Princess Theatre was severely frost-bitten and the Shakespeare provided by Mr Wilkie was being consistently neglected by the general public and deserted by the intellectuals. He was 'portentous' and 'arid' as Shylock in The Merchant of Venice. He was 'blundering' as Malvolio in Twelfth Night, and 'not very impressive' as Jacques in As You Like It. As Falstaff in The Merry Wives of Windsor he showed 'the quality of expansion'. One critic dismisses his fat knight as merely 'the masterpiece of a bicycle pump'.16

One actor who exploded in fury against the criticism was Macdougall. In 1928 Hugh McCrae said Macdougall was a producer of talent, but he was not a good actor; and there was no especial reason to justify his continuance on the stage. 'Of too short a stature, with a laboured delivery of his lines, and a set of gestures stereotyped to a degree, we would wonder at the proximate cause for putting himself at the head of a company otherwise well chosen for the presentation in hand. Is it vanity or stress of circumstance?'

McCrae replied: 'I think artists of the theatre in Sydney should demand comment worthy of the name of criticism from those who make a profession of writing about them. Artists ought to claim from them something more than the mere passing opinion that the man in the street might hazard; something of the consideration given by, say, sporting journalists to our athletes. A sporting writer would dare to suggest in print that a player of his standing be warned off the field without bringing to the subject keen reflection and a mastered technical equipment. Nor would I think such short shrift would be given to any other artist, or casual writer, save to the player of plays.'

Macdougall was particularly upset over the slur on his height. 'I am told of my shortness. I knew that before. What I have never been told is why that should force me to abandon my career. A manager in New York asked me my height. I replied, "Two and seven-eighth inches taller than Garrick, Edmund Kean and Caruso".'17

Of course, if the poppies grew particularly tall then they could expect to receive more knocks to bring them back to a respectable levelling size. Even the great Nellie Melba did not escape. Her voice was such a perfect instrument it was difficult to attach any grounds of quality. There was the criticism, for example, that she sang without soul.

In 1903 the Bulletin pointed out that local musical comment was usually limited by the commentators' incapacity and by their desire to ingratiate themselves with singers and managers. Instead of judgement and discrimination, the public received banalities or pretentious nonsense.18

So it made no apologies for reprinting a review from the Triad of a Melba concert in Christchurch. Triad admitted she had the best voice ever to come to New Zealand, but that the tears in her song are not reproduced in the voice, but is rather the result of indomitable energy and perseverance. A superb voice is like a perfect piano or a magnificent Strad; each should be the vehicle of emotion and thought. It is the mind and spirit behind the voice, the piano, the violin, that is of supreme importance.

Nature has endowed Madame Melba with the full physical equipment of the vocalist, but while giving the voice the stage presence, she withheld the temperament. Madame is not a genius; but I could hear her again with pleasure at — strictly reasonable prices.

In other words Melba is a remarkable mechanical nightingale, destitute of temperament; and all her artifice cannot supply the hot passionate heart of melody.19

Yes, that was the trouble, no hot-blooded passion. Selwyn Rider was saying much the same thing sixteen years later. He said: 'I have listened anew to the characteristic squeaks of the lady's many worshippers and toadies', so now he decided to have a go himself.

Did I enjoy Melba's singing? Why, of course. Did I enjoy the concert so greatly that the night will stand in my memory as something altogether exceptional and wonderful? No. Melba's voice stirs me to almost passionate admiration, but admiration isn't joy; admiration doesn't satisfy; there is no ecstasy in admiration. Melba's voice reached my brain without stirring my blood or in any way touching my heart. It is beautiful, even strikingly beautiful, as beautiful as a white camellia might be beautiful.

I never could get thrills from camellias.20

Then there was the sighing of relief in England in 1919. They reported on the amount of space she was receiving in the daily and weekly press, with Melba comments on everything: 'Melba's conception of the peace terms; how Melba would deal with Bolshevism; Melba's idea of a short way with wharf-labourers; Melba's thoughts about her daughter Helen.'21

Ultimately the comments were about her fading voice. While her voice waned 'with the encroaching years', her prices continued to rise. In 1921 her voice 'dwindled in range, steadiness and power'.22

And this was the review for her Sydney farewell:

It is to be hoped that Melba's 'absolute' farewell to Sydney will prove to be her last. I, for one, hope these performances will not be followed by more, leading in the remote future to a season of absolutely absolute final, sad, fond farewell. Even singing here again for charity, a thing Melba has done, should be abandoned. Charity may cover many things which does not, of course, enter into consideration as regards the soprano. But charity will not hide the decay and sad remnants of a once-peerless voice; which is not the point. The public should be allowed to remember her for the unique beauty of tone and [extraordinary] art which together made her the greatest light soprano of her time. We have assisted at the obsequies of her voice. Let us pray that there be no resurrection.23

The most appalling knock of all came from John Norton, the half-brilliant, wholly unscrupulous proprietor of the Sydney and Melbourne Truth. It was he who spread the story that Melba was a drinker. Norton loved to become involved in legal battles with famous people. He would couch his articles in such terms that a libel action could almost be guaranteed. Not only did he get a flavoursome story from the original articles, but a whole series of others as the action unfolded in the courts. Usually he put his libel in the form of an open letter, starting not 'Dear Sir', but a cold 'Sir.' In this case it was 'Madame.' In Truth of 28 March 1903 there were large headlines:

AN OPEN LETTER TO
MADAME MELBA
Concerning her
CHAMPAGNE 'CAPERS'
BREACHES OF PUBLIC FAITH
OUTRAGES AGAINST GOOD MANNERS
INSULTS TO AUSTRALIAN CITIZENS!

Mellifluous Melba, Supreme and Sovereign Monarch of Matchless Song.

Madame. Your public and private conduct during your short six months' sojourn in Australasia makes it compulsory that you be told the truth. Genius is mostly eccentric; the eccentricities of genius are condoned up to a certain point. The eccentricity of genius which is only excelled by your eccentricity. You have so far heard too little of the first and a great deal of the last. The turpitude of a talented termagant can be borne, a ten times ten, but there is a limit of licence which cannot be condoned. You have so often transgressed that limit that the public has at last become tired of your talented tricks and vicious vagaries. Your scandalous breaches of public faith, and private propriety are no longer to be borne without protest. That protest I now make; and if you resent it, I invite you to vindicate yourself by civil or criminal process in a court of law.

You began your career in Australia by a gross breach of faith to the people of Melbourne and Victoria, by postponing your first announced series of concerts in the Town Hall. ... Your non-appearance at the time was attributed to the sudden illness of your father. That may or may not have been the true cause, though I believe it was NOT, but that you yourself were the cause, and that you were really 'indisposed' as Sairy Gamp would say, and that your own conduct was the cause of the 'indisposition', especially as stories were current in Melbourne, and believed by those 'in the know', of your gay goings-on at sumptuous champagne suppers at or about the time when you should have been singing to thousands of your enthusiastic admirers in the Town Hall. What has since come to light concerning your champagne capers on the 'Continong', in gay 'Paree', in the 'Rue de Revelry' and elsewhere; in London, at the hotel in Frisco, in Sydney and Launceston, and last but not least, Menzies Hotel in Melbourne, lends colour to the suspicion that your first and subsequent breaches of faith with the Australian people were as much, if not more, due to champagne than real pain. Moet and Chandon have a lot of embarrassment and chagrin to answer for, especially among prima donnas.

Norton then quoted a message from his correspondent who had the honour of describing for Truth the first appearance and enthusiastic reception in the Town Hall. Every place she has visited has experienced her imperious pretensions, combined with her eccentricities, so much so that Society, with a big S, has been compelled to say she is 'really not nice'. Notwithstanding this reputation she has had one lordling so continuously in tow that he can almost be labelled as part of her baggage. Some say she intends to marry him, but as there is 'just cause and impediment' to it, unless it has really been removed by an American divorce, the surmise is very likely of only shimmery substance. One night she was due at a theatre. She arrived an hour late, and dropped nervously into the dress circle, too nervous to risk a grand tour around to the manager's box. That haven might have involved a too stormy and disastrous passage. The way to it is strewn with steps, at any of which a star might have fallen. Imagine so august a personage coming a champagne cropper in a public place! So she wisely stuck where she sat first, a sorry spectacle, pale and palsied. There might have been sympathy displayed towards this weak woman but did amiability accompany her human frailties. But she has done everything to estrange solicitude. She has bossed, bounced, bullied and buncoed to right and left of her. Many a stage star has shot an erratic course but none has done it with such vicious vulgarity, such bourgeois bumptiousness and such indiscriminate cruelty.

Norton then went on with a sample of Madame Melba's outrageous behaviour. Tasmanians had flocked to Launceston from all over Tasmania, among them the Governor, Sir Henry Havelock, and his lady. Their train had to be stopped at Paratta, and there they received the news: 'Melba will not sing tonight'.

In his open letter to Madame Melba, Norton said: 'On your behalf a belated medical bulletin was issued to the effect that you could not sing in consequence of a "strain". This was looked upon as a deliberately concocted canard, intended to gull the Tasmanians who, having in a sense been "taken down", looked upon this as adding insult to injury.'

According to Norton when she left Launceston her train was hooted by the crowd. The letter then continued with a report on Melba's stay at the Brisbane Hotel in Launceston:

You stormed, scolded and sneered like a drunken crab. Finding only a cold collation awaiting you, instead of a hot dinner, you kicked up hell's delight. You couldn't leave the poor waitresses alone. Here's a cameo cut out of a hunk of your choice conduct:

Narked Nellie: Where's your cap!

Terrified Waitress: We don't wear caps, Madame.

Narked Nellie (yelling fortissimo): Get out and get one at once.

Here's another:

Melba (studying menu in disgust): Is this a sixpenny restaurant or a common hash-house? Order me a steak and be quick about it!

When the steak is brought Melba is too mad to munch, but rails and raves at everything and everybody, and tears off to her room in a towering tantrum, where she remains, after her manner on such occasions, giving ground for the impression that she is either half-mad or wholly drunk. Is it any wonder that the curly-headed Quigley, the beatified 'boots' boss of the Brisbane Hotel, should have torn his hair and cursed his luck that he ever sent Melba along to Launceston.

There was much more, attacking her for meanness, attacking her for ill-treatment of staff and using such vintage Norton language as 'It is altogether too bad to add to the truculence of the termagant, the vagaries of the virago, and the proclivities of the voluble pocharde those of a miserable miser, who, while revelling in wealth and swigging champagne, has balanced and buncoed, and kept better faith with her than she has kept with the public.' This was his final fusillade:

Madame, I've done with you for the meantime. Perhaps now that I've done with you you'll think it about time to begin with me. Be it so, but be sure you count the cost before commencing; think who will gain most by a public investigation and cross-examination in the courts — you or your legal counsel.

I tell you frankly that I court such a contest, and feel confident that if it is commenced that I will come off more than a conqueror. Maintenant il faut que je vous dise mes adieux, en chantant to the classic air of 'Dolly Gray':

Good-bye Nellie, I must leave you;
Give up swigging dry champagne;
Else your friends will surely leave you
In disgust and poignant pain.
I remain, Madame,
Your admiring admonitor,
JOHN NORTON.
Melbourne, March 27, 1903.24

What could Melba do? She had engagements back in Europe. It would have been impossible for her to stick around in Melbourne fighting a protracted libel action against John Norton. She did the only thing possible. She ignored John Norton and his Truth. Yet the smear stuck and still sticks. In any publication or discussion on Melba someone raises the lingering thought, was Melba really a drunkard? If this were true, then how did she manage to remain the top soprano in the world for such a remarkably long period?

Did Dame Nellie Melba in return knock Australia? Reportedly she said as advice to a visiting artist, 'Give 'em muck,' but she wasn't the first Australian to despair of being heard in her own country.

Australian theatricals and artists have railed constantly at the indifferent, knocking Australian public, and how it has always preferred the artist from overseas. This is true. Few shows at top professional theatres are produced without at least one imported star in the leading role. It is possible at times to use an Australian in the lead but only if the name has already gained approval from London and international audiences. You are only any good if first you have won fame overseas.

One of the great tirades against the Australian public came from Alfred [Jones], who had been billed as 'the first Australian tenor to return to his country as principal in a Grand Opera Company'. He did not stay for long. This was his farewell message when he left again:

In Australia I find that audiences at present demand emotional insincerity, cheap dramatic effects before technical accuracy, and the vocal tricks of charlatans before pure bel canto. Such a statement probably brands me as an egoist, but there is in Australia the best material in the world upon which to work. Unfortunately the average Australian is not yet sophisticated enough, nor yet un-insular enough, to be able to judge whether the music he hears is of real value, or is merely 'dope'. By music of 'real value' I mean music that adds to the mental and emotional stature of the hearers. All other music is opium.

To my fellow-Australians I say — You have not arrived at the point of appreciating your own artists — the artists who have genuinely made good in a big way. ... The old gibe that a foreign name carries more weight than a local one still holds good.25

Much the same thing was being said in 1953 when Anthony Quayle, director of the Shakespeare Memorial Company, came to Australia. He said:

'Australia has a wealth of artistic talent. But the heartbreaking thing about it is that these people get no recognition in their own country and are driven overseas to find it. It is pathetic that Sydney, the second biggest white city of the British Commonwealth, has only three professional theatres. Yet Australia is producing more theatrical talent on a population basis than any other Dominion.'26

The Australian actor, Frank Thring, backed him up. He said Australia ought to snap out of its indifference to the theatre or hand the place back to the aborigines. After all, under the influence of their white successors the culture of the aborigines was deteriorating almost to the point of complete disappearance. He said:

'It is a damning indictment of the level of Australian mentality and judgement that throughout the world there are our own top-ranking artists in every art, while at present there is not one of the first calibre in Australia. And why? Because they have been driven out by low standards and ignorance. Those who have not left already are certain to do so in the next few years unless either the Government or the public takes moves to support them. The public's personality is split — into one element that supports cheap farces like the Folies Bergère and an invisible audience that fails to attend plays in which they might have to think.

'In the theatre they believe that every imported has-been and incompetent is infinitely better than the local product; that anyone who doesn't make a beeline for the West End can't possibly have talent.'27

Yet Frank Thring proved himself to be one of the keenest drama critics. He produced what surely must be the shortest dramatic criticism seen in Melbourne. He was reviewing the pantomime Jack and Jill. The Argus reported that he sat down to his typewriter, sighed, and said: 'Good grief, this is it.' The result:

THE THEATRE: The Tivoli.
THE SHOW: Jack and Jill.
THE CAST: Roy Barbour, Nina Cooke, John Bluthal, Ivor Bromley.
THE CRITIC: The End!

Another time he wrote of a Shakespearean production: 'Last night Cleopatra barged up the Nile and sank.'

In 1955 he wrote of Britain's 'Heart-throb' David Hughes, 'Like the H-bomb Mr Hughes is one of the menaces of modern civilization. With the nauseating charm of a precocious child he alternately bellows and moans through a mass of sticky sentiment, a mentality culminating in a terrifying example of commercialised religion.'

He found Googie Withers's acting 'shallow' and of a flamenco dancer on the stage at the Tivoli he said 'she had the agonized twistings of a run-over cat'.

And this review caused a disturbance. It appeared in the Argus on 24 February 1955:

[The Australian National Theatre Movement inaugurated its] Shakespearean Festival with a diabolical performance of [A Midsummer Night's Dream].

This presentation (one hesitates to use the word production) plumbed the depths of incompetent amateurism. To list the players would be sheer cruelty as they have been so handicapped not only on the production side, but also by the most appalling stage-management it has been my misfortune to witness, either here or abroad.

Lights flashed on and off, scenery quivered, and a rather tired cypress tree, which for some reason had been leaning against the sky, fell over with such monotonous regularity that eventually one thought it must be part of the plot.

This idea, however, was finally discounted by the fact that whenever it slipped, the person who happened to be passing at the time became so enmeshed that one felt the need to rush on the stage and hack them out with a small axe.

The Australian accent was at all times nasally apparent, and on occasions one scanned the tottering cypresses for an itinerant koala.

It is typical of the debilitated state of the theatre that even the prompter's diction was completely indecipherable, and although prompts were bellowed from the wings with the power of a bull elephant at mating time, the struggling players fumbled in a mass of inaudibility.

The swift changes that Miss Anne Fraser's excellent composite set facilitated were marred by what seemed to be rather an ugly brawl in the wings, and the costumes appeared to have come partly from Bourke Street and partly from a bankrupt opera company of the late 1920s.

The curtain advertised to rise at eight o'clock finally ascended at 8.12.

It shouldn't have.28

After this review the National Theatre in Melbourne banned him as a critic. It was after 8 p.m. and he had taken his seat for the opening night of The Merchant of Venice. Miss Gertrude Johnson, founder and honorary director of the National Theatre, approached Thring and said: 'I must ask you to leave. We do not want criticisms. You may stay provided you agree not to write anything, otherwise you must leave, or the curtain does not go up. We have not invited any of the newspaper critics here, and though you have bought your own ticket, the curtain will not go up unless you leave.'

William Carr, the producer, said: 'I have nothing against Frank personally. It is just that I don't like his sort of criticisms. I don't mind if he criticizes professional theatre, but what I find objectionable is his sort of criticism of these young amateurs.'

Frank Thring said he was 'bewildered'. He wrote: 'The cast of this student production at the University included Malcolm Phillips, Harry Starling, Reg Gillam and others, who have surely not passed their first year. I consider there is no production so good to serve as an introduction for these children, and it is small wonder that they are growing up to be a nation of filmgoers and radio listeners if they are subjected to badly performed Shakespeare, which appears to be the theatre's specialty.'

When Frank Thring received a request to leave the theatre, Michael Fitzgerald and Seymour Porter took his place. He described the play as setting him back fifteen to twenty years: 'Heaven help me, and I was a schoolboy again listening to the elocution teacher: "The quality of mercy is not strained."'

Oddly enough Frank Thring received a gush of telegrams the next day, all inviting him to come and criticize.

One secretary of a young theatre group wrote: 'Dear, Cruel, Mr Thring. Just write SOMETHING, we don't care a hang WHAT'S said.' Another said: 'Request please, your presence. Guaranteed no throw-outs Wednesday, Thursday, next.'

Thring said: 'I will do as many of these shows as I can. But on 12 April I am opening at the Arrow Theatre — much I am sure to the delight of my critics — in René Mitchell's production of The Prisoner.'

His comments on the role of the critic were these:

'It has been suggested that my criticisms are too acid and too personal. I would like to point out that an actor sells not only his body but also his voice, his movements, and his mind to the general public for the price of a theatre ticket. If any of these personal qualities fall below the expected standard then it is the critic's job to point it out and the public's job to reject the artist as it would any other shoddy article.'29

Whether the critics were at fault or not there was little encouragement to Australian writers. In 1926 there was a play called Kangaroo Flat. Maybe it was bad, but one wonders whether it deserved this criticism in The Triad:

Kangaroo Flat, the so-called new Australian play by Mr Talma Ordell, must be one of the most extraordinary productions in the world. I defy anyone to describe it. It is simply incredible. There have been some stunningly bad Australian plays from Robbery Under Arms to The Squatter's Daughter, and On Our Selection, but for glorious inconsequence Mr Ordell's effort surpasses them all.

The dialogue is mainly the patter of antediluvian vaudeville, relieved unexpectedly by a few scattered gems of maudlin melodramatic sentiment. But why should this mixture be called an Australian play! There is not one scene, not a solitary line, that has any touch of character or reality. Perhaps there are some people who may think Kangaroo Flat a contribution to Australian drama, but it seems to me a waste of props and lines and grease-paint.30

A generation grew up who, if they had ever seen an Australian play, would have had great difficulty in naming even one or two. In 1955 Ray Lawler, a professional actor, won a £100 prize in a competition for a play titled Summer of the Seventeenth Doll. Niall Brennan was director of the Union Theatre at the time and Ray Lawler was director of the Repertory Theatre. Recalling this time Niall Brennan has written:

The theatre in those days was an imported thing; Australian plays in commercial terms were virtually non-existent, and Australian actors played subordinate roles to imported stars of little or no literary consequence. Even the Union Theatre, the temple of non-commercial dramatic art, had presented only [two] Australian plays in its twenty years of life.31

According to Brennan, Ray Lawler was producing a play a fortnight for the Repertory Theatre and later, as a change of pace, he invited John Sumner, a former Union director, to come to Melbourne from Sydney to produce one play for his old company. Sumner came, but it was difficult to find a suitable play. He wanted a different play, something that would provide a real challenge. Why not Lawler's one prize-winning play, Summer of the Seventeenth Doll?

But John Sumner, an Englishman, knew of the play and the interesting reaction was that of the Australians. Niall Brennan said: 'Ray Lawler's response to this was little short of hostile. He had never dreamed of doing The Doll. As a short Australian producer charged with a budget at the University, he could hardly dare take the responsibility of staging the type of play which had failed so often.'

It took several weeks before Lawler's reluctance and misgivings were overcome and he gave permission for the play to be staged. Even the Australian writer himself had been conditioned by the Australian attitude.

Brennan said: 'I held his hand in a metaphorical way on many occasions as the day of opening drew near. He came stumbling into my office one day, muttering: "Those actors out there, do you know what they're doing? They're putting on an Australian accent."'

The play, of course, was an astonishing success, receiving praise from almost all the critics. It went to London and the United States; the film rights were sold and Ray Lawler must have grossed better than $160,000, all for a play which he was reluctant to put on.32

The Doll still received criticism in Australia because it presented Australia in a sordid manner. Instead of a beautiful vision of gum trees, golden peaches, koalas and kangaroos, here was a story about a couple of rough cane-cutters sojourning in a wretched Carlton slum with such lines as 'I'm looking at the reflection of the sunset in the gutter.' Many felt that if Australian plays were to be successful then they should do a public relations job for their country. Such a man was Mr S. Leverton, who had the responsibility of directing 'Australia's food publicity in Britain'. As director of P.L.A.N. (International) Ltd. he said it would be a patriot who amended the film script of The Doll. He said:

'It should be shown against a background of Australia's lovely surf beaches, bronzed young men and sweet young things. I'm sure it could be arranged dramatically.'33

Alan Seymour had similar problems with his play The One Day of the Year, again not good public relations for Australia. This was the story of the conflict between a father and his son over Anzac Day and it contained such lines as:

The son:

'It's just one long grog-up. We're sick of all the muck that's talked about this day. ... It was doomed from the start, it was a waste. Every year you still march down that street with that stupid, proud expression on your face, you glorify the wastefulness of that day.'

The father:

'You'd take away everything. You'd take away the ordinary bloke's right to feel a bit proud of himself, for once.

Every city, every little town in this country puts on its service and its march on that day. Every day for forty years they done it and they always will do it. You don't get out there to show what a great soldier you was; you're there as mates; you're there to say it was a job you had to do and you done it — together.'

Seymour's play was knocked for being a 'dig at the Digger'.

It won a prize at the 1960 Adelaide Festival of the Arts, but it remained unperformed at that Festival. Seymour said he was never able to understand why they turned it down, but clearly the committee thought it was a slur on returned soldiers.

The Federal Minister for Territories, Mr C. E. Barnes, even attacked the play in Parliament. He said it was part of a scheme to lessen the achievements of Australian soldiers in two World Wars. 'Fortunately we went to the polls on the national service legislation and we have the approval of the people of Australia.'

And he said darkly: there were many influences in Australia to lessen its war effort.

Robin Lovejoy, producer of The One Day of the Year, said:

'I don't know whether Mr Barnes has seen or read the play. I suggest he may well re-read it. Admittedly there is a strong criticism of Anzac Day by the university student but the sympathy [the play] generates is for the older man. If anything the play begins with a genuine Anzac Day sympathy.'

Those who are criticized and drag their critics into the courts. In October [1966] Peter O'Shaughnessy directed and performed in an Othello at the Sydney Conservatorium. Katherine Brisbane, critic for The Australian, wrote:

'Of all the Othellos I have seen, from the Old Vic to the Royal Shakespeare, never had one so [completely] lost contact with the audience and so entirely bereft of ideas.

Stupidity and lack of talent are forgivable; brave failures are deserving of praise — these are everyday human failings. But this shoddy and schematic production, or rather recitation, makes me very angry indeed.'

Other comments from Miss Brisbane were:

'My companion had a point when, listening to one of the bursts of noise off stage from time to time, she remarked that there was obviously a better play going on back stage.'

Then this was the final paragraph:

'In short the performance is a disaster which has all the makings of a fine production. All it needs is a producer with a little humanity who understands that the actors on stage are people and the audience are people too. As the only fellow-feeling I had with the stage on Tuesday was with Miss Thody's expression at curtain-call. She looked as if she had had enough for one evening.'34

So Peter O'Shaughnessy sued Mirror Newspapers Ltd. for libel. The case came before Mr Justice Taylor and a twelve-man jury in the Supreme Court, Sydney. The action did not come on until more than a year after the review appeared. It was a heavyweight bout; both parties employed Senior Counsel and it ran for five days.

[Clive] Evatt, Q.C., appeared for Peter O'Shaughnessy. He told the court that the article held Mr O'Shaughnessy up to ridicule as a man so bereft of honour and integrity that he would resort to dishonesty in presenting the play. He said part of the review implied that Mr O'Shaughnessy had emphasized his own role in Othello at the expense of the other actors.

O'Shaughnessy said in the witness-box that he had been associated with drama and acting for twenty-five years. The review in The Australian had affected his nerves a great deal. 'It affronted me, and disgusted me, I felt rather ill,' he said.

Cross-examining, Mr Woodward, Q.C., reminded Peter O'Shaughnessy that occasionally he too had been a critic. And asked him if he remembered a critique that he wrote of a performance by Katharine Hepburn and Sir Robert Helpmann in The Merchant of Venice at Her Majesty's Theatre in Melbourne in 1953.

'Do you remember writing "Perhaps Miss Hepburn has dwelt in the exalted region of stardom so long that she is convinced that acting is a kind of glorious confidence trick and that there are no limits to the excesses an audience can put up with"?'

Mr O'Shaughnessy: Yes, I think I wrote that.

Mr Woodward: Fair comment in your view?

Mr O'Shaughnessy: I'm not sure that it is.

Mr Woodward: Do you remember saying 'Sir Robert Helpmann's Shylock is ageless, emotionless, [but] I like Miss Hepburn's Portia, it is magnificently, almost frighteningly unabashed at its failure to resemble a living human being.'

Fair comment?

Mr O'Shaughnessy: Certainly.

Mr Woodward: 'It is the producer and the two stars who must bear the brunt of the blame for the massacre of Shakespeare's play, but very few of the rest of the cast behave as though they have any blood in their veins. All of the cast scrupulously avoid the beauty of Shakespeare's verse. We never hear a missed sound, only heavy of foot prose.'

Still fair comment?

Mr O'Shaughnessy: Yes.

Mr Woodward then went to the end of the O'Shaughnessy [review, reading: 'Miss Brisbane's voice] is so flat and monotonous that it is still hard to believe she wasn't doing it on purpose.'

Mr O'Shaughnessy: I think that's a bit tough.

Mr Woodward: You seem to be objecting to being tough on her.

Peter O'Shaughnessy: I have never objected to anyone being tough on me but I object to being called a dishonest man.35

Mr Justice Taylor told the jury about the job of a critic. He said a critic was entitled to express himself in ironical, bitter or even extravagant language. In the words of a famous Chief Justice he was entitled 'to dip his pen in gall'. The plaintiff's case was that the writer dipped her pen in a much more corrosive substance and had used the word dishonest, which showed that it was not criticism of the production, but an attack on him. In deciding the issue, the jury had to apply the test of whether the opinion was honestly held by the writer.

The jury was out for three hours and twenty minutes. It decided in favour of Mirror Newspapers. The verdict was this — Katherine Brisbane's review was fair comment on a matter of public interest.

Peter O'Shaughnessy appealed and the action was settled out of court in September 1971. He left Australia for Britain that week saying: 'I would never settle here again. Things have run out for me completely, and I have been out of work for the past year, and every application I have made to the Council for the Arts has been knocked back.'36

So Peter O'Shaughnessy, in the classic phrase, 'returned overseas where everything is always better.'

In the 1970s even if overseas actors continued to be imported for top roles at Australian theatres, Australian playwrights were getting Australian plays on Australian stages, but not always. One interesting case was that of David Williamson, aged thirty, a lecturer in thermodynamics at Swinburne Institute of Technology in Melbourne, well-known for his play Stork. He wrote a play called The Removalists. It was the first outside Australia to receive the George Devine Award for writing. The judges included Sir Laurence Olivier and Harold Pinter.

According to Williamson he gave it to an Australian Repertory Company a year before and it was left to languish in a drawer. He said to The Australian: 'I'm not being nasty about the repertory company — I'm not paranoid about it. It's just that established Australian theatre has been convinced until recently that nothing worth showing will ever originate in Australia.'

But now The Removalists has received the official blessing from London, confidence in Australia is once again restored. Critical judgement is reinforced and it can appear on an Australian stage.37

Modern music critics, particularly with the Australian Broadcasting Commission, have a remarkable reputation for knocking. Their knocking usually is fearless — they are unnerved by the reputations of either artists or composers. Yet they do it with skill and often a fascinating turn of phrase.

Here is a collection of knocks gathered at random from Melbourne and national newspapers over four years:

Even the usually fluent Moldau froze to an immovable sound-mass under his baton of disenchantment.
Felix Werder, The Age, 3 July 1969.

It is many a day since this orchestra played Bach. Indeed those who listened carefully to last night's performance in the Suite No. 1 in C major might have decided that much for themselves.
John Sinclair, The Herald, 24 July 1969.

The slow movement dragged its wary way, suffering from the hardening of the musical arteries, through acres of desolated harmonies scented with magnolia blossoms.
Felix Werder, The Age, 12 August 1969.

By an unwritten but apparently absolute law, any conductor who wants to succeed in Melbourne must not live here.
John Sinclair, The Herald, 15 September 1969.

The absurd centrepiece of Saturday night's concert by the Melbourne Symphony Orchestra, under Peter Eros in the Town Hall, was Beethoven's transcription for piano and orchestra of his own violin concerto. Beethoven produced this curious monstrosity simply to 'make a quick quid' and there is no excuse or justification for including it in the programme of a serious concert.
John Sinclair, The Herald, 13 April 1970.

Yesterday afternoon the final concert of the ABC subscription series for 1969 passed away boringly, with fewer than [two hundred] people in the audience. It is obvious that the programme was selected by any principle except as a feast — but [provided] all orchestral members [were] gainfully employed as baby-sitters for some four or five hundred children.
Felix Werder, The Age, 23 September 1969.

This year the civilized world is celebrating the 200th anniversary of Beethoven's birth. And that perhaps might partly explain why this badly-decomposed oratorio 'Christ on the Mount of Olives' was exhumed and made the major feature of the ABC subscription concert at the Town Hall.
John Sinclair, The Herald, 11 June 1970.

The sound was dull, sodden, completely without crisp attack or articulation. ... Easily the most distinguished [contribution to the] whole performance was given by the audience — schoolchildren reading the Sporting Globe, kids yarning over their ice-cream cones and fat little girls eating their play lunches, one of whom was a real virtuoso with the paper bag.
Kenneth Hince, The Australian, 10 October 1970.

If we cared as much for the realities of music as we do for cricket, boxing and Australian rules, anyone who smeared the inside of a dogs' dinner over K467 (Mozart) would get compulsory treatment inside of a week. Justifiably viricide.
Kenneth Hince, The Australian, 2 April 1971.

Mahler for Sydney; mud and a muddle of pottage for Melbourne — it seems to be the ABC's schedule for 1971. Melbourne has always been the great Brahmsopolis, the city that liked to look at itself, the capital whose eyes were set on the past and who walk resolutely with the heels in front and the toes backwards. Denes Zsigmondy's attempt at the Brahms Violin Concerto sweetened the cup very little. My lonely heart warmed to his old-fashioned mannerisms but not to much else. The light of his hair undulated gracefully around a brilliant tonsure. His knees flexed and sprang up again to energize his bow-arm. The tone that came through was thin and appealing, but not much of it survived the throttling encounter with the orchestra. And even then the intonation was very, very weak.
Kenneth Hince, The Australian, 26 April 1971.

Yet there was one celebrated occasion, perhaps the only time a Melbourne critic made the posters. On 5 May 1955 The Herald had the poster:

OPERA STAR
SUES
MUSIC CRITIC

The star was the beautiful and famous Italian singer, Rina Malatrasi. On 29 April John Sinclair had this to say:

Perhaps the best thing to do with last night's performance of 'Madame Butterfly' in Her Majesty's Theatre would be to make it into a film — a silent film. The result would not be a best-seller, but being silent it would have an immediate advantage over the version of the Puccini opera we heard last night. ... As Lieutenant Pinkerton, Agoto Pedroni revealed a loud and biting tenor voice, while Rina Malatrasi seized every opportunity to force her tone to glaring brilliance with complete disregard for the shape of the music or the character of Cho-Cho-San.

Needless to say, her voice has lost the freshness it had seven years ago, and just as sadly she no longer seems capable of conveying an impression of either innocence or sincerity.

After her aria in the second act (which was encored), many members of the audience who shared my opinion, but lacked my manners, cried 'Boo'.38

Rina Malatrasi objected in particular to the lines that the audience had booed her. She claimed that she had been received with great enthusiasm. The Herald settled out of court for £500. Madame Malatrasi insisted on being paid not by cheque, but in cash. The story goes that that night during the performance she left the money stuffed in her handbag. When she returned the money had gone.

Which, undoubtedly, was the greatest knock of all.

Traces the knocking of the Australian stage from Lola Montez's 1855 Spider Dance controversy and Harry Seekamp's Ballarat horsewhipping through The Triad's savage music and theatre criticism, Nellie Melba's battles with John Norton, Frank Thring's career as a drama critic, the hostile reception given to Australian plays including Summer of the Seventeenth Doll and The One Day of the Year, the Katherine Brisbane libel case, and David Williamson's The Removalists, ending with a collection of ABC-era music criticism.
