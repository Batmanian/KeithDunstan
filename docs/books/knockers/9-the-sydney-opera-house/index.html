The Sydney Opera House

11 min read.

First published 1972

A chronological compendium of knocks aimed at the Sydney Opera House from 1962 to 1971, from Noel Coward's 'Danish Blue' to Sir Tyrone Guthrie's 'mish-mash of buildings', tracing the battles with Joern Utzon, the steadily ballooning cost, and the running comparisons with Melbourne's own architectural failures.

The Sydney Opera House or the pyramids — the Pharaohs did not have the benefit of a Lottery. The continuing story of the $100,000 Opera House Lottery made the extraction of the money relatively painless.

It all began with the Cahill Labor Government which wanted to prove to all the world that the Australian Labor Party was not without a feeling for culture. There is little recorded history to explain what agonies took place with the construction of the Cheops Pyramid, but the Sydney Opera House certainly caused more agonies, more architectural problems than any building in modern history.

The battles and the infighting with Joern Utzon went on for ten years. The Opposition in the State Parliament found the Opera House a heaven-sent implement with which to taunt the Government. When the Liberals came to power in 1965 Utzon had to deal with a new tough Minister for Public Works, Mr David Hughes. Hughes was appalled at the steadily rising costs and was convinced that all the problems of this thing, that was threatening to envelop everything like an amoeba, could be pinned onto Joern Utzon. In February 1966 he made a move to downgrade him from controlling architect to design consultant.

This, of course, Utzon would not accept. There was a protest march through the city of a thousand architects, artists, and Utzon supporters. In the Royal Australian Institute of Architects there were rival groups — the 'Utzon in Charge' group and the 'Utzon Go Home' group. Seventy-five of the eighty-five members of the government architects' office signed a statement that nobody but Utzon should finish the building.

The Sydney University Architects' Club printed Michelangelo's unhappy letters over the problems he had with St Peter's over sixteenth-century knockers. A Sydney sculptor went on a three-day hunger strike and one protester kept sending telegrams to the Premier with the message 'Do Not Crucify Utzon This Easter'.

On 28 April 1966, Joern Utzon, a sadly disappointed man, left Australia under an assumed name with his wife and family. Other architects were appointed to complete his Opera House. He had left it for ever. The Opera House, which some enthusiasts described as the building of the century, became a running joke.

Some did not approve of the entrance with the water running down the plate glass. Barry Humphries said it reminded him of old-fashioned butcher shops, which always used running water on their windows to help to cool the meat. He also said: 'The Cultural Centre and King's Bridge are Melbourne's own independent deterrents — we can only hope these terrible weapons will not be used.'

There was high praise from distinguished visitors, but those who did not approve received excellent news coverage. Mr Norman Bromelle, keeper of the department of conservation at London's Victoria and Albert Museum and secretary-general of the International Institute for the Conservation of Historic and Artistic Works, said: 'I didn't like it at all. The whole place seemed to me to be pretty poor, dismal and depressing. In the interior there are deficiencies in both lighting and background. One fault may have been, I think, that the people in charge have not consulted enough and seriously enough with all kinds of people on the gallery staff.' He said a museum should be a bright, lively place, where mini-skirts were welcomed, full of the colour and beauty of the young.

Another distinguished visitor was Mr J. M. Richards, Britain's leading architectural critic, and editor of the Architectural Review. He was merciless about the leading buildings in Sydney, Canberra and Melbourne, but on his first day in Melbourne he neglected to see the Arts Centre. However, enthusiasts for his judgement made sure that he went there. He described it as 'Australia's answer to Coventry Cathedral and similarly independent of the main stream of modern architecture, giving no impression of leading Australian architecture in any constructive new direction.' He found the gallery visually restless, with so many changes of material and conflicts of shape. His summing-up: 'Too many ideas seem to be chasing too few convictions.'

There was controversy over a 20-cent admission charge. 'What other civilised gallery would impose a charge like this?' There was controversy over the decision to put the Victorian coat of arms over the entrance. Norma Redpath was commissioned to do the job, but Patrick McCaughey of the Age thought it a wilful misuse of talent.

He said: 'What other new museum in the world would seriously contemplate putting a crest or a coat of arms up on its walls in an uneasy compromise between State piety and artistic patronage?'

There was much praise, at the opening. Dr A. van Schendel, director of Amsterdam's Rijksmuseum, described the gallery as an extraordinary achievement, one of the wonders of the world.

Some of the sharper knocks came later, and the sharpest came from one of Australia's most imaginative photographers, Gordon De'Lisle. It appeared in the Australian under the heading 'A Nice Little Art Gallery':

Reading the popular outpourings on Melbourne's Arts Centre, one feels that one is drowning in parochial, marshmallow self-congratulation . . . and it seems high time that some debunking was done.

When I first met the architect, Roy Grounds, some twenty years ago, he had just completed his Toorak home, a grey stone house with a court in the middle; a house literally turned inside out, such as was popular in the United States at that time. What Grounds, innocent of new notions, has now perpetrated, is simply three such houses, laid end-to-end in a unit.

The building squats, featureless, like an obscure grey telephone exchange, floating in already scungy moats floored with lolly papers on St Kilda Road, and hovering like a bleak penitentiary suspended over an acre or two of ferro-concrete to Sturt Street.

The facade is indeed so self-effacing as to be without pride in itself. The Great Hall is a bitter, terrible disappointment. French's ceiling is indeed superb, but its support is a veritable forest of black poles, gaunt as a monk's chorus, littering the airspace and thudding enormous, powerful values back at the whole thing, which is rendered an aesthetically emasculated cumbersomeness.

Great cathedrals with twenty times this airspace were built clean-floored in the thirteenth century; with all of today's technology at his command, Grounds has been able to come up with nothing better than an elegant-ceilinged hayshed.

The galleries themselves are pleasant enough, and well-lit on the hanging areas . . . but, one should perceive with downcast eyes; a glance upward distracts with endless vistas of angle-iron, plastic and gimerack plywood.
