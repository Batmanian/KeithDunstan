The Australian Female

12 min read.

First published 1972

Surveys a century of knockers on the Australian woman, from R. E. Twopeny's 1883 portrait of the bored, overdressed colonial hostess to Alan Whicker's 1970 television programme in which Australian women lined up to condemn themselves.

So the theory developed that we could not really stand females. Women, after all, were a different species. They rarely talked about sensible things like football, racing and the quality of the beer. It was better if they were separated at schools from a very early age. Pure bliss was the all-male haven like the RSL and even at parties it was better if the niner were placed at a strategic point so that males could gather around it and the women elsewhere. Indeed beer helped. It was always considered rather 'not done' for women to drink beer. So we created our all-male havens. Apart from the RSL clubs, there were the working men's clubs, the immensely popular lodges, Apex, Lions, Rotary, Legacy, all of which are almost more popular than anywhere else on earth, giving male escape one or two nights a week; and then there has been that haven of havens, the Australian pub.

Only in recent times have females been allowed into Australian public bars. We did not even like barmaids in hotels and South Australia banned them by law until 1967.

Through the ages our pubs had a grim monastic quality, so monastic they didn't have female lavatories. In the Victoria Royal Commission on Employees in Shops of 1883 this was actually cited as an excuse for the abolition of barmaids. A girl of twenty had died of 'haemorrhage of the stomach' brought on by constipation and long working hours. There was no lavatory for women in the hotel. Slowly this stain disappeared and we acquired the 'ladies' lounge'. Here, a lady, properly escorted, could be taken for a drink, which would be paid for at a higher price than in the public bar; a further discouragement, intended to keep the girls out of the place. Even as late as 1969 the unescorted lady was something to be feared. When granting a licence for a new St Kilda cabaret, Judge O'Driscoll warned the nominee: 'The commission will not tolerate women attending without an escort.'

The sexual apartheid spreads to many places. The most famous of them is still the Members' Stand at Flemington Racecourse, of Melbourne. There is a line on the pavement beyond which women must not pass. It is like the famous wartime cry of the French — Ils ne passeront pas. It is always something to show the tourists. Visiting feature writers are fascinated.

Masculine activities have always had the prime position in Australia. Our passion for sport and outdoor activities always has been stronger than anywhere else in the world. In the early days when women were scarce sport was an escape from boredom and an escape from intellectual activity. We had and still do have more race meetings per head of population than even countries like France and England. The crowds that gather at our football are large almost beyond belief. The girl who wants to see her male has to drag herself along to some sporting activity on a Saturday afternoon. As Max Harris has pointed out, the Australian finds in sport a substitute for sex.

So in this tradition the Australian female gets a constant knocking. John Pilger, an Australian writing in Nova magazine, gave her a special blast. He pointed out that the Australian female had a brief period of equality, even superiority, before marriage. After that she learns pretty quickly. The male takes over. At a party she finds it is vital that he must cradle 'his pewter mug of beer beneath his mighty left pectoral muscle and talk of sport and cars' with his mates. Pilger says a mate in Australia is simply that which a bloke must have around him. Mates do not necessarily want to know you, to understand your problems, ideas, hopes; they just want to have a beer with you, to be reassured they themselves have a mate.

So this is the pattern for Pilger's Australian female:

For her the progression from bikini girl to slipper slob is inevitable. The years pass slowly in the great tracts of geometrical gloom that are Australian suburbs, in which there is no song and little happiness: it shows in her hardening face, a boredom gut; it is, perhaps, relieved by a child or two, or by a monthly card night, or a holiday in Surfers Paradise, or by the Dinner Party. Otherwise, the boredom is final.

Max Harris said almost the same thing in The Australian Civilisation. Once married, the beloved became 'the wife' rather than 'my wife'. Sex experience was not endowed with either idealism or romanticism; it was utilitarian and appetitive. There was the old cliché portrait of the blokes around the keg on the veranda while the women segregated in the living-room, but few attempted to discover the psychological factors behind it. 'There is a residuum of sexual shyness,' according to Max Harris. 'The Australian male feels, "You're more at home among the blokes — they can only talk about babies and washing-machines."'

Donald Horne thinks it is all changing. The Australian 'male lord' is starting to disappear. In The Next Australia he writes:

Now the greater entry of women into public pleasures, the much greater number of married women who go to work, and the diminishing of social awkwardness seems to be producing a curious situation. Among many of the young and middle-aged family life is now more a shared experience than two separate spheres of influence . . . he is losing his old practice of exclusive male pleasures. And he still pays the price of being the general useful and relief nursemaid at home.

Not many book writers have turned their attention specially on the Australian female. R. E. Twopeny in 1883 found the women, particularly the wealthy, both uncultured and bored. She didn't know how to dress; she had no idea how to handle servants.

When you tremble before your butler, and have to learn how to behave at table from your housekeeper, wealth cannot be unalloyed pleasure. Without education and taste, the parvenue has small means of enjoying herself except by making a display which costs her even more anxiety and trouble than it does money. . . . What can she do with herself all day long? She has not gone through that long education up to doing nothing which enables English ladies of means to pass their time without positive boredom. She has no tastes except those which she does not dare to gratify, and becomes a slave to the very wealth whose badge she loves to flaunt.

Twopeny did not think much of Australian women but he detested their children even more:

I have a holy horror of babies, to whatever nationality they may belong; but for general objectionableness I believe there are none to compare with the Australian baby. It is not only that the summer heat and sudden changes of climate make him worse-behaved than his confrères over the ocean, but the little brute is omnipresent, and I might also add omnipotent. Nurses are more expensive and mothers less fastidious than in England. Consequently, baby lives in the family circle almost from the time of its birth. Nurseries are few and far between. He is lashed into a chair by his mother's side at meals; he accompanies her when she is attending to her household duties, and of course even when she is receiving her visitors . . . the middle and lower classes of Australian are not content with the baby's presence in the household. Wherever his mother goes, baby is also taken. He fills railway carriages and omnibuses, obstructs the pavements, is perambulated, and is sucked cara populo in the Exhibition. There is no getting away from him unless you shut yourself up altogether . . . You have to kiss him when visiting his house. Having once made the household bow down before him, he is slow to relinquish the reins of office. Possession is nine-tenths of the law. If the child no longer cries or has to be kissed, he makes up for it in other ways. He has breathed the free air of Australian independence too early to have much regard for the fifth commandment.

Sidney and Beatrice Webb didn't like the children either. When they arrived in Sydney in 1898 they commented on all the squalling colonial babies. Beatrice almost echoed Mr Twopeny. She said:

The most depressing feature of New South Wales is the absence of education among the rich, and the bad manners of all classes. The well-to-do women too are uncivilised; they are dressy, snobbish and idle, and whilst they are willing enough to companion the men on the race-course, they apparently think it 'unwomanly' to take an active part in public affairs.

It is a frequent ploy of visitors upon arriving off a ship or an aircraft immediately to praise the beautiful girls. Sometimes it works, but only rarely. The way to get news space in Australia is to announce how ugly the girls are. In 1958 Professor Denis Brogan, a political scientist and one of Cambridge's leading wits, visited Australia. He was here for five weeks, then wrote an article for Harper's magazine. He said:

It is rare indeed to see a smart woman in the streets of Melbourne or Sydney — and I am not asking for the standards of Paris or New York or San Francisco, but will settle for those of Glasgow or Amsterdam. The advertising, the arcane underworld to be found on display in the big stores, reveal all too plainly the acceptance of a non-competitive mediocrity. Nobody should look smarter than anybody else. Nobody does.

The Professor did well. Lady editors gave him pages of space with much indignant comment that Australian females were really more exquisitely attired than any other creatures on earth. But it is the comments on physical appearance that cause the greatest anger. Roy McGregor-Hastie in a book titled The Compleat Migrant said in 1963 that because of over-indulgence in sport 'Australian females had legs like Clydesdales and arms that would not disgrace the discus throwers of the Ukraine'. Mr McGregor-Hastie, as a London-based Russian-speaking authority on Communist affairs, no doubt was an expert on Ukrainian discus-throwing arms.

Mr Peter Michaels in a book called Australia written in 1965 said that when Australian girls lost their first bloom of beauty there was little but desolation. And when they tried to aid nature what they did have became sadly unstuck. 'Faces are crudely made up in great streaks, coarsening features and ruining complexions.'

Between 1920 and 1940 there were maybe half a dozen critical books on Australia. In the 1950s and 1960s they came in squadrons. Australians yearning to be told about themselves, printed and reprinted them. There were books by Dutchmen, Indians, Frenchmen, Italians and Americans. Mulaika Corben was the wife of a college dean at the University of Melbourne. After her husband returned to California she wrote a book saying that Australia was a hard, dirty land peopled mostly by ignorant, vulgar morons.

Of the Australian female she said: 'After marriage her good looks quickly fade; practically all she retains from her youth is her shrewd sense of humor. In that climate her beautiful complexion coarsens, her teeth are soon removed and she is forced to work like a drudge.'

The Melbourne Herald was so upset it devoted twice the space originally given to Mrs Corben, to handling all the replies. Miss Myra Roper, head of the Women's College, Melbourne University, the Dean of Trinity College Melbourne, president of the National Council of Women, local Americans resident in Melbourne were all invited to assure Melbourne females they were as lovely as could be.

John Pringle in The Australian Accent almost for a moment sounded as if he were going to be kind.

Walk down Castlereagh Street or Martin Place on a summer morning and you will see more pretty girls than in any other city in the world . . . pretty, healthy, with splendid strong bodies, the result of good food, sunshine and hours of swimming in the Pacific surf.

Then he said what he really believed:

Personally I do not find their faces beautiful in repose, though many are pretty and some handsome, and they are certainly not wistful. Indeed, their characteristic expression is hard, suspicious and slightly contemptuous. They look, and often are 'tough'. They add to this effect by using far too much make-up, especially the older women, who often appear quite grotesque. It is only fair to say, however, that the climate is unkind to a woman's skin. Their voices are almost unbelievably ugly, and one never quite gets over the shock of hearing some lovely girl speak for the first time. This is not merely a matter of the Australian accent, for many men have pleasant rough voices. Too many women talk with a strident, saw-like whine that is indescribable.

Yet in the long run we are best at knocking ourselves. In 1970 Alan Whicker the English television commentator came to Australia to make another series of what the Sunday Australian called Australphobes. He titled one programme 'This Is The Problem — Women Are Not People.'

It would have been easy to make the regular comments about the trodden-under Oz female, but Mr Whicker barely had to say a word. Australian females were only too eager to come on the show and make the comments themselves. These were a few selected quotes from the programmes.

Andrea: 'It's more important for the Australian man to have a good relationship with his mates than women. It seems to me there are shades of homosexuality. The place is roaring with it.'

Diana Fisher: 'If you go to a country race meeting you will find a row of chairs. . . . All the chaps in another room. I won't put up with it. They like the image of the great big muscular Australian. How many beers can you drink mate? But the girls take it. They acquiesce. They are stupid. The men procreate all right, but they do that and get down to the next game of footy.'

Elsa Jacoby: 'We are very second class citizens. We take it all for granted. It is only when you go away that you discover women are treated differently in other countries. It is a rude shock.'

Maggie Tabberer: 'Do you know in this country girls still put embroidered doilies in their glory box? When a girl should be getting ten bucks worth of make up she's buying a rotten old pair of saucepans so that one of these slobs will come along and marry her. Isn't it terrible?'

So Mr Whicker's conclusion was this: 'Boys always seem to find more time for their mates than the mating game.'

But knocking of this kind is popular. Sue K. Langley, better known as Sue Rhodes, exploited this theme into one of the best-sellers of Australian publishing history with her book Now You'll Think I'm Awful. She said:

Nobody, but nobody gets treated as badly as the Aussie girl. She and only she would put up with men who slap her heartily on her dainty back and expect her to slurp schooners with fellas, and only she would put up with Australian males' bull-in-a-china-shop method of love-making. Me, I want to be seduced tenderly.

In two books she attacked the beer-slurping, back-slapping, all-muscle male and dismissed men as mother-mad, sex-hungry fools.

In Now You'll Think I'm Awful she wrote: 'Yes, dear readers, as soon as I raise the necessary ante, I'm departing these shores for greener and more promising fields.'

In 1971 she married Rory Calhoun, ex-logger, miner, cow-puncher film star. In 1969 Mr Calhoun went through a stormy divorce from Lita Baron, who filed a complaint of 79 infidelities, including Betty Grable, a fifty-two-year-old grandmother. Calhoun denied them all.

But one hopes Susan was seduced tenderly.
