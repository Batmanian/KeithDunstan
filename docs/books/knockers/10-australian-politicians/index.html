Australian Politicians

42 min read.

First published 1972

Traces the long Australian tradition of despising, mocking and vigorously knocking politicians, from the nineteenth-century campaigns against Sir Henry Parkes and Graham Berry through the conscription battles of Billy Hughes, the de Groot bridge-cutting incident, and the unsparing treatment of Menzies, Gorton and McMahon.

Rupert Charlett, as a pressman in Canberra, once told a very revealing story about the attitude of Australians to politicians. The year was 1946 and a Canberra newspaperman asked his hotel maid for an extra lump of sugar with his tea.

'It's hard to get, but I can take one from another tray,' she said.

This fellow had just read a book on ethics so he said: 'I don't want to deprive a fellow human being of a lump of sugar.'

'Oh, it won't be a fellow human being,' she assured him. 'It'll be from one of the senators' trays.'

Australians treat politicians with a terrible contempt, derision, sometimes even loathing. Although our members of Parliament are rarely exposed in public scandal; unlike the leaders in some Asian countries, they are not forced to indulge in graft or make a living by accepting bribes, and for their services their financial reward has never been over-high. Yet they are unloved.

In 1972 the Sun News-Pictorial conducted a competition to find a name for a $250 million project over the Flinders Street railway yards. Nigh on a thousand names came in. The entrants bestowed their love on only one politician. Many wanted to call it Menzies Place. Sir Robert Menzies, then long retired in hospital with a stroke, was a special case, his image in retrospect improving year by year. There were no suggestions for Gorton Place, McMahon Towers, or Whitlamville. Yet they showed their passion for explorers, navigators, and particularly sportsmen and women. Many wanted to call the project after Evonne Goolagong, after Shane Gould, the swimmer, after Johnny Famechon the boxer, after Des Tuddenham the footballer. One personality outstripped all these — Ned Kelly. He polled almost on par with Sir Robert Menzies.

One lady asked that in the main square of the project there be 'tastefully wrought statues of Gladys Moncrieff, Dame Nellie Melba, Banjo Paterson, Ray Lawler, Sir Donald Bradman and Evonne Goolagong. . . .' Two singers, a poet, a playwright, a cricketer, a boxer and a tennis player, but certainly no politicians.

Furthermore, political cartoonists seem more ruthless than anywhere. They go beyond the point of political satire. They take a particular relish in making the politician look absurd. Mr McMahon is always knee-high to his wife, his ears are like yacht and his bald head shines like the deep. They adore to go to work on Sir Henry Bolte. Sometimes he is a wizened, unapproachable, wooden figure — more often he is a totem pole. There was a famous portrait of Mr Clifton Pugh which won the Archibald Prize — one which showed Sir Henry Bolte. The rate indignities are also popular. One bought the form of candles of Sir Henry Bolte and Mr McMahon. There was said to be a particular satisfaction in watching the flame burn down through their skulls and observing their power disintegrate as their idiot shapes.

James Cameron of the Sunday Times, London, thought the Australian attitude to politicians a world phenomenon. He said: 'Nowhere have I seen politics simply as an activity, so reviled, mocked, jumped on and derided as in sunny Australia, land of the free and home of the free. It far exceeds the usual babblers. . . . There is a credibility gap and it is very hard for anyone to see the lunatics inside the cockpit from the lunatics outside.' He applauded those who could provide into two hostile factions — the photographers were paid They sat upon opposite sides of the same room and when the minor became the major faction they changed places. The lunatics outside had to pay into a building called the Treasury, the enormous sum of four million sterling, in return for the privilege of a system of Government by babble. Not only so, but the gabblers — some of whom were persons no one would think of lending a £5 note — were allowed to expend this immense amount of money in any way they chose to determine. As may be supposed, they did not forget themselves. Each windbag pocketed £300 per annum.

The contempt for politicians, the dislike for those in power has always been there. Melbourne Punch in 1874 wrote a fanciful satirical piece. It was produced as a clipping from the Otago, New Zealand, Daily Times for 20 August 1996. A distinguished scientist, Dr Miller, from the Archaeological Institute of Dunedin, had been conducting excavations in Melbourne, which had been destroyed many years before by volcanic action.

The doctor in his report described how the people had filled the fertile land until it was exhausted, how they had ravaged the hills for timber, and polluted the atmosphere. The comments on the politicians were most interesting:

But crowning madness of these people was displayed in their form of Government. Its absurdity literally transcends belief. The Victorians hit upon a scheme more laughable than anything imagined by Swift in Lilliput or Laputa. They appear to have got together about a hundred men specially distinguished by their power to gabble. They shut them up in two rooms, and encouraged them to 'jaw' for eight hours at a stretch. They applauded those who could jaw the longest, and caused them to divide into two hostile factions. They even paid three stenographers £500 per annum each to make a verbatim report of the inutterably foolish and frothy nonsense which the flatulent babblers vented from their mouths.

They sat upon opposite sides of the same room and when the minor became the major faction they changed places. The lunatics inside the cockpit were empowered to compel the lunatics outside to pay into a building called the Treasury, the enormous sum of four million sterling, in return for the privilege of a system of Government by babble. Not only so, but the gabblers — some of whom were persons no one would think of lending a £5 note — were allowed to expend this immense amount of money in any way they chose to determine. As may be supposed, they did not forget themselves. Each windbag pocketed £300 per annum.

Eighty-six years later Malcolm Muggeridge wrote something like somewhat similar. He too was on the gabbling line and his remarks were produced with great enthusiasm by newspapers all round Australia.

The Australian Parliament is, I think, unique in having its proceedings broadcast. As a peak listening period approaches, quips get livelier, interjections get more vivacious, oratory more fervid.

Might it not be possible, I wondered, to carry this further, and turn Parliament into a television show? An element of sex appeal would of course be required, but this could be introduced by bringing in some Sabrina-type members. A couple of good professional comedians on both sides of the house would ensure that viewer interest was sustained, and tone could be added by introducing occasionally a visiting peer or some dignitary wearing robes and covered with orders and decorations. Having thus disposed of Parliament by giving it a useful role in the field of light entertainment, Ministers would be free to deal with grave problems which confront all Governments today.

Instead of exerting themselves to prepare long speeches, which few listen to and none heed, they would be able to address themselves to more urgent questions — for instance the rush to atomic extinction on which mankind seems bent.

A few weeks later he was even more exact in his criticism. 'When they become politicians Australians are pretty odious — small-eyed men with quick glances and often a bottle of Scotch in the desk cupboard. They have practically no political ideas as they are extremely old-fashioned, an antique Whiggism which finds expression in Mr Menzies' relentless platitudes washing like breakers against the harsh, rocky shores of the mid-twentieth century; an almost equally antique brand of Robert Owen — William Morris socialism filtered through Dr Evatt's curious and convoluted mind, or dredged up from the inner consciousness of his simpler associates.'

The dislike of politicians can be traced through a national reluctance to give them money. Members of the House of Commons in Britain did not receive regular payment until 1911. But attitudes in Australia were tougher. The distances members had to travel were far greater, so without payment parliament could be a special club for the privileged and the wealthy.

The members for Claremont and Rockhampton were the first Parliamentarians in Australia to receive a salary. This paid them £300 a year, but the Victorian Parliament was tougher — to actually legislate for payment was generally considered an outrageous idea. Twenty years went by before it was finally settled. The Legislative Council rejected the Bill in 1861, 1865, 1869, and made further attempts in 1874 and 1877.

Agitation began in 1859 and continued right through the 1860s. The big fear was that if you paid people to do this sort of thing, then one would get the commonest, the most crude type of person in Parliament. The Honourable W. Degraves said in the Legislative Council on 4 September 1867:

I intend to vote against the Bill and if I have a seat in the House for the next ten years I shall always vote against it. I look upon it as nothing more nor less than an encouragement to a parcel of political loafers to come here and do as they please. If we had payment for members I have no doubt we should have political loafers introduced in larger numbers and that they would keep the country in a state of uproar from one year's end to another and I think it's a downright attempt to loot the Treasury. It is a disgrace to the country. If I found any man voting for a measure like this I would not take his bill for five and twenty pounds.

Mr G. P. Smith said in the Assembly: 'I believe it would have the effect of creating a class of professional politicians. Of all classes of men, I believe that the most despicable is a class of professional politicians — a class of men who trade on politics, and live by politics.'

So it went on. Melbourne Punch said M.L.A. were the letters a member of Parliament was entitled to put after his name meaning Multus Loquens Asinus. Mr McCaw, speaking in 1869 on the proposed £300 a year for members, said: 'A greater objection is the low estimate in which Members of Parliament hold one another, and the constant bandying of charges of falsehood, and dishonesty in which they indulge. This will be remedied only if we conduct ourselves like men — if we abstain from dirty insinuations. I have had twenty years' experience among horse-dealers, and during that period I never heard such insinuations of falsehood made by them against one another as I have heard in this House.'

Mr Wrixon, later Sir Henry Wrixon, said: 'Give money to politicians and you get the poor and meanest, who have not succeeded in prosecuting any business successfully.'

Parliament at last in introduced salaries of £300 a year. This caused Queensland introduced salaries in 1886, New South Wales in 1889, Tasmania in 1890, Western Australia in 1900 and South Australia in 1887.

The South Australian members received only £200 a year and in 1919 they looked for a modest increase to £300 which Victorian members had been receiving for four decades. They decided to go to the country with a referendum. This would be a clear indication of the popularity of members of Parliament. The plea was crushingly defeated 89,042 votes to 42,943. So they did not get their rise until after the First World War, in 1921.

The idea of Parliament asking the public's approval for a rise in their salaries was never repeated in Australia.

Federal members in 1901 started off with £400, but there were complaints about the agony of trying to live on the low wage. In July 1904 Mr King O'Malley M.H.R. applied for permission to erect a tent in the grounds attached to Parliament House. He said that by the time he had paid living expenses, assisted impecunious constituents, and subscribed to public objects to which a member of Parliament was expected to subscribe, his savings had reached vanishing point.

In 1907 Federal salaries went up to £600 and there was a passionate public outcry. In 1952 Sir Robert Menzies told the House of Representatives the history of public and Press opposition to salary increases, and how the language had rarely varied. Using the vast Menzian sarcasm he said: 'I think it was at that time [1907] that some talented writer coined the great phrase about a salary grab to which, of course, people of greater genius added expressions of raiding the Treasury and so on.' He was wrong, of course. A talented writer first thought of 'salary grab' back in the 1850s.

In his survey Sir Robert said that in 1932, during the Depression, Parliament actually reduced Parliamentary salaries from £1,000 to £750 and he added: 'I go on record that this was the first and time that the great newspaper interests approved what Parliament had done.'

In 1947 the citizens were hostile to the rise of to £1,500. One newspaper proprietor said: 'All I can regard this £500 increase as is smash-and-grab practice.'

In 1955 and 1959 committees under Sir Frank Richards reported on salaries. This caused the richest furore, A Gallup Poll found 62 per cent were against any rises. Only 3 per cent thought that the politicians should get all the pay increases and allowances recommended.

Though to get it and keep it we wriggle and lie And flummox our friends with a humbugging cry, What honour, or truth or a character clear, When weighed in the scales with 300 a year.

Over the years there have been some interesting knocks. In 1853 William Charles Wentworth had the idea that there should be an Australian aristocracy. As chairman of a select committee on a new constitution for New South Wales he recommended that the colony should have its own House of Lords and this would be a great improvement on the Legislative Council.

His committee recommended that the Crown should create hereditary titles. Upon receiving the title one would have a seat for life in the House. However this would not apply to the nobleman's descendants, the inheritors of the title. The Lords would elect representatives to the upper house from among their own number.

Mr Wentworth said that these gentlemen because of 'their birth, leisure and superior education' would lay the foundation of an aristocracy and would lay the foundation for an 'Upper House which would be a close imitation of the House of Lords'.

Furthermore, it would serve as 'an inducement to respectable families to stay in the colony and for upper classes of the United Kingdom to emigrate'.

The suggestion caused both amusement and anger. One letter wanted to know whether the ageing Mr Wentworth had finally gone out of his mind — a House of Lords, an imitation of the Parliament of Kamehameha? . . . It is a painful image of the dotage into which the lunatic intellect may sink.

There was much good fun to be had. In Sydney, leading dignitaries boast in the Royal Hotel in Hunter Street. Mr James Piddington said he would have a Lord Camden The Bill for the new constitution was read for the first time on 9 August and the following week there was a popular meeting in Pitt Street. He has no notion that happiness of human beings is worth a straw. Men and women in his esteem are degraded below even the slaves of America, for these are only associated with cattle, but in Australia we are actually to be subservient to cattle.

James Macarthur, educated privately in England, was the fourth son of the celebrated Captain John Macarthur. He had managed his father's estates at Camden and he had been elected as Camden representative in the Legislative Council. He was rich and distinguished and no doubt with the thought of becoming Lord Macarthur he spoke during the Council debate on the subject. The Empire 'knock' at his speech could have been a model for political knocking:

We shall retain for at least a week to come a painful sensation of still seeing and hearing Mr James Macarthur. As many of our readers as have witnessed the chain pump of a dredge boat, slowly and laboriously jerking out deliberate buckets full of slush, may conceive some idea of the manner and matter of his speech, and fancy the martyrdom of human patience, in having to sit for about four hours under the dreadful infliction of this oratorical chain pump.

There was yet another protest meeting. This was described as a 'monster gathering'. It took place in the 'old Government grounds' near Circular Quay. Estimates of the crowd ranged from five to nine thousand. It was a very determined meeting and over the whole aristocracy movement quietly died. Mr Wentworth withdrew his plans.

Those who had moved in Council held a celebratory dinner. It was called the 'Constitution Minority Dinner'. Some sumptuous and festive gentlemen sat down the rarest dinners ever seen in the colony.

The colonial Press in the early days was extraordinary. The Press was the only outlet, very often the only job available for clever and intellectual immigrants. Not all the immigrants in the 1840s and 1850s were simple labourers. For various reasons, perhaps unable to find jobs in England, clever and intelligent men arrived. They found their newspapers and the standard of writing, often seasoned by savage irony and allusion, was very high.

A good example of this is the long campaign against Charles La Trobe in the Argus. Charles Joseph La Trobe was Victoria's first Governor. Historians have found him a man of sensitivity who felt a great deal about the interests of the colony, always endeavoured to be just, and succeeded admirably under difficult circumstances. The Argus Age campaign came to its head in 1848 when there was a public move to petition the Queen. The first salvo came in a motion to the Town Council, which was passed by nine votes to five. The Argus commented: 'The only wonder is that the people could be satisfied that the would fail once.

The Argus was always very polite. Even when it was calling Mr La Trobe a thief, a Incubus and a snake, it continued to refer to 'His Honour'. Incubus was defined in the dictionary as a supposed being, especially in the Middle Ages, to lie with men and women at night, credited with the power of producing supernatural births. In brief, an incubus — and that was how the Argus found the reign of La Trobe.

The Argus made appeal after appeal for citizens to go to the public meeting to get rid of La Trobe. It published one letter which said:

Let me earnestly implore my fellow colonists to be up and doing . . . let them be at their post . . . and with one united cry of indignation denounce the traitor. . . . It must not be said of the inhabitants of Port Phillip that they tamely permitted their privileges to be invaded and the fair land of their adoption devastated by such a paltry pedagogue as Charles Joseph La Trobe.

The meeting took place outside in cold weather in what was described as 'a pitiless rain storm', yet 1,500 to 2,000 people turned up 'in one of the most numerous, most orderly and most unanimous meetings we have seen in Port Phillip'.

There were some stirring speeches. Mr Johnston said: 'Why not with one consent pitch the faithless traitor overboard? He must surely have feathered his nest comfortably by this time — and, bird of ill-omen that he is, let him fly to some less favoured land, where unheeded and unknown he may pride himself in ignoble obscurity from the merited obloquy and scorn of an injured people.'

John Pascoe Fawkner chided him for keeping back funds from Victoria, for building roads to the properties of his friends, when the money should have been used for building lighthouses, for building the road to Sydney. He was a lick-spittle who 'licked the dish publicly to Sir George Gipps' (Governor of New South Wales 1838–46).

A vote of no confidence in La Trobe asking for his recall was carried unanimously and the Argus exulted: 'The deathknell of Mr La Trobe's administration of the government may now be looked upon as struck . . . the result of our appeal to Her Majesty's gracious consideration may be regarded as perfectly certain.'

The Argus, however, was a little over-confident. The motion was carried, but that was as far as it got — Her Gracious Majesty ignored the whole thing.

It told how of walking in this sunny land, one would come across a snake causing a recoiling, curdling of the blood. It would cut it with a spade. Dead. However, the dead snake would come to mind. What if one put it in water — would it react like an eel? Instead of being a malignant serpent? But no, there was no hope. A serpent does always remain a serpent. This sort of writing continued through most of the nineteenth century. Politics filled the newspapers and the weekly magazines. The debates were recorded ad nauseam, and every little doing of a politician went down on paper. At the same time they were attacked with a savagery which must have been very good for circulation. The public loved to read what rapacious villains they had for rulers.

Sir Graham Berry, many times premier of Victoria, was always a beautiful target. His reigns in power received the title 'The Berry Blight' with the result that often he was depicted in cartoon form as a snake, a monkey, or as a disgusting insect.

Thy high-flown words are sham — thy virtue's worse Thy rule is ruin, and thy name's a curse.

So said Melbourne Punch in 1876.

Berry, an ex-grocer, was a radical. He fought for protection, payment of members, a land tax for monopolists and most particularly he fought to break down the privilege and exclusive power of the Legislative Council.

One of the most startling leading articles against him appeared in the Age on 24 January 1870. It called him a notorious office-seeker, a traitor who 'lost Collingwood through his tergiversation and his deposit in South Grant through his treachery'.

That potent verb 'to tergiversate' does not get into leading articles so often these days. It means to practise evasions or subterfuges, to equivocate. The Age also found space in the leader to call him 'a rat, a bad bargain' and a 'slanderer'. It went on to hope that so untrustworthy a politician as Mr Berry would not ever be allowed 'to have in his hands the public purse'.

The Age continued to compliment Mr Berry in the same vein for several months. On 8 February they had him having 'boldly and unblushingly debauched the whole constituency'.

In his fight to get payment for members in 1878 he included the payment in the Appropriations Bill and the Legislative Council refused payment. Sir Graham, instead of resigning or calling for an election, took other measures. On the night of 8 January, to save money, he sacked 278 public servants.

Most of them did not find out until they arrived at work on a Wednesday morning, 8 January. The newspapers called this 'Black Wednesday', a bitter parody on the famous Black Thursday, 6 February 1851, when Victoria was set aflame by bushfires and by 11 a.m. the temperature in Melbourne had reached 117 degrees.

Among those he sacked were departmental heads, police magistrates, county court judges, coroner and crown prosecutor, architects, gold-field wardens, etc.

All this caused tremendous hardship, even poverty for those sacked. The Governor, Sir George Bowen, accused of weakness in consenting to Berry's action, was recalled and removed to Mauritius.

Berry won his point. The Legislative Council backed down, passed the Bill and most of the public servants received their jobs back. Even so Berry found it a convenient time to get rid of many of his enemies.

The leading articles again were blistering. He was called 'Mad Jerry Berry' and 'Jerry Bunkum'. There were also consistent references to insanity and megalomania. He was also called Robespierre. Melbourne Punch ran this line:

Graham Berry: 'As our political head — An Elder-berry. In character — A Black-berry. In the eyes of civil servants — A Rasp-berry. In the estimation of the Press — A Straw-berry. By the impartial , he is thought — A Goose-berry. By his idiotic acts of retrenchment he had become — A Mul-berry.'

Sir Henry Parkes was Australia's most famous statesman of the nineteenth century. He fared even more poorly than Graham Berry. He took part in the political scene for forty years, and he was constantly in and out as Premier. He fought passionately and eloquently to bring a reluctant New South Wales into Federation and, although he did not live to see it, he is called by some the Father of Federation.

The knocks against him had a cruel personal line. Francis Adams, a poet born in Malta of English parents, came to Australia in 1885. He made these comments:

Sir Henry Parkes — Australia's only statesman . . . large and gross in build with a mass of white hair running all over his head and face, he rises full of a self-conscious pomposity, and startles one's ears with a piping treble. The inane and tedious vulgarity of the rhetoric, the appalling aspirates dropped and added in every sentence . . . seems the fitting expression of an egotism as empty as it is oppressive. A risen counter-jumper in a Sydney toy shop, he is an absolute adventurer, the Ned Kelly of Australian politics, the unregenerate Gladstone of New South Wales.

In 1890 the Bulletin ran a page on the savage comments about Sir Henry ranging from a line by Sir Charles Dilke that even the banal qualities of Parkes's poetry couldn't sink him in the electorate. Sir Henry did actually write highly knockable verse.

Then there were these words from Mr John Haynes, M.P.:

We don't believe the cause of humanity ever found a true response in his heart. On his cedar chair in the old Government offices by the fig-tree he was a Napoleon snob. He was a perfect Caesar in wax. An emperor without a subject even to punish.

He would have reporters and officials crawl at his feet, and his great feat seemed to be to distend his nostrils as he deigned to speak to the officers of the people.

Nasty, but true as the Gospel, said the Bulletin.

Parkes had many jobs. Apart from working in a toy shop, he was also a turner of ivory. In 1850 he started a very good newspaper the Empire Its scathing all the rights But we are convinced the anomaly.

Henry Parkes was so upset he resigned that day. There was no pay for members of Parliament at this time, and as an old journalist he knew exactly how to lay on public sympathy. In the Herald he said:

I have neither decoration nor empty title — no honorary post nor compliment of power. No son of mine is eating the bread of the people which he cannot earn. I leave something behind me which cannot be undone or taken from me, and which men will hold in remembrance and honour. For the rest I seek no sympathy, and am prepared to bear the burden alone.

Newspapers and weeklies attacked him constantly over his public debts. In 1887 his friends took up a public subscription to get him out of trouble which raised £9,000. This invested was enough to give him £500 a year. The Bulletin was very contemptuous. It wondered whether this would be enough to pay off all Sir Henry's many creditors. 'It is more than doubtful, considering Parkes's lofty contempt for the dull, commonplace virtue of solvency . . . the sordid sentiment which leads stupid plodding citizens to pay their debts in full.'

It went on to say that he was the champion 'disher' of his opponents, 'the monumental plagiarist of Australian politics. He has alternately supported and denounced every principle. The private insolvent Parkes is the public Parkes, the wholesale appropriator of other men's ideas. . . . Parkes is such a stupendous egotist that, large enough though his frame may be, there is no room in his body but himself — himself, the cold-blooded, ungrateful vendor of mortgaged allotments.'

Yet no one could ever say Sir Henry Parkes was fortunate out of politics. Come the Depression of 1893 the income received on his £9,000 shrank to £250. He had to sell his possessions to provide for his family. He married three times. In 1895 there was a move to provide him with a public grant, but it was too late. Before anything could be done he became ill and he died in poverty on 27 April 1896 at the age of eighty-one.

Of course Truth newspaper as usual was the most consistent knocker of them all. No office was too high for the treatment. Sir George Reid, often called 'Yes-No Reid', was Prime Minister in 1905. Truth originally dubbed him with the title 'Reid the Wriggler.'

'It would seem advisable to recall some of the more salient features of this cheapjack charlatan's career if only to discover how richly the dirty dodger and high falutin' humbug, this fat, flatulent and frothy fellow deserves the degrading name of Reid the Wriggler.'

Sir Edmund Barton was Australia's first Prime Minister and even Truth, it seemed, would have found him a difficult man to knock. But no, its editor Mr Norton found an angle.

In 1902 he wrote:

It is perhaps useless for Truth to attempt to describe adequately the Bacchanalian and gastronomical feats in London of the amazing expert in the deadly sin of gluttony, Barton the benighted betrayer. There lived but one man that could have done justice to Barton's gourmandizing, guzzling, gustatory, gulping gut game — and that was the immortal man of genius François Rabelais. Rabelais would have forsaken friends, family and fireside in order to follow our great Australian bounder, and, in England, that land of huge banquets and stupendous stuffings, watch the ease with which Toby could paralyse turtle soup, flabbergast luxurious and expensive meats, and decimate stores of expensive wine.

William Morris Hughes had more popularity with the public than any Prime Minister in Australia's history, but the fury he received when the party split in 1916 now makes astonishing reading.

After pledging Australia to conscription Hughes lost the referendum on 28 October. There were 1,160,033 votes for NO and only 1,087,557 for YES. On 14 November in Caucus the anti-conscriptionists set out to get him. A motion was put 'that the Prime Minister no longer possesses the confidence of the party as leader, and the office of chairman be, and is hereby declared, vacant'.

Hughes was a man of action. He didn't wait for the situation to stew. W. Farmer Whyte in his book William Morris Hughes records that Hughes sat motionless listening to all the argument, then he thought, 'Why postpone the inevitable?' Suddenly he got up from his chair, gathered his papers and said, 'Let all those who support me, follow me.' Twenty-three of the sixty-five members of the party rose and followed.

And so Mr Hughes, still Prime Minister, went off to form the Nationalist Party.

The fury against Hughes came out in the party journal Labor Call, which before the fatal Caucus meeting was demanding his head. 'He must go. All his crew of hailing retrogrades couldn't bring him through the shoals of the people's scorn. Now will he have the effrontery to keep hold of a helm that has been severed from the ship? He has been bold and contumaceous.'

(Contumaceous is another word that leader writers have almost forgotten. It means 'Perverse, obstinate, wilfully disobedient'.)

Then, under a headline BLITHERING BILLY BLOWN OUT — HIS LAST TRIUMPH TRUMPED, Labor Call poured scorn on Hughes for his referendum failure:

We feel no sympathy for Mr Hughes. His tactics have been brutal and his abusive language unparliamentary to say the least of it. What does he think of the parasites, vipers, disloyalists, anti-Britishers and pro-Germans in our midst. There are only 723,142 of them. With all his vitriolic verbosity, he has condemned all anti-conscriptionists as these. But he lacks statesmanship and diplomacy. He is a pettifogger, inflated with gas. He is like a soap bubble; he comes puffed up and then disappears in vapour. Billy the Mighty Atom. The Australian Napoleon has met his Waterloo. Blithering Billy is blown out.

When he still managed to carry on, to take colleagues with him, to actually form a new party, it was all too much for Labor Call. This time there was a note of hysteria in the copy. It talked of the 'flaunting folly' of this man, his 'wrecking of industry', 'his creation of a greater army of unemployed', 'his utter inability to deal with matters of finance'. Then it went on to become more personal. There was the 'puniness of the man who allowed his overweening vanity to be so played on by the jets of fulsome torydom' and there was 'not a spark of actual ability in his pygmy frame'.

Prime Minister Joe Lyons received similar treatment when he walked out of the Labor Party to form the United Australia Party in 1931. Stanley Bruce was mercilessly knocked during his reign as Prime Minister. He was forgotten, rejected by the Australian people and a classic picture of him appeared in the Herald and the Sun on 3 April 1946 leaving for England by boat. He is a lonely figure on the deck, virtually nobody has come out to say good-bye.

If Lord Bruce thought he was knocked, no Australian politician was ever knocked quite to the degree of Mr J. T. Lang. The United Country Party, for example, in 1931 distributed all round Sydney, a 'debenture'. It was done in green, very prettily, with all the scroll work one used to see on the £1 note. It carried the head of Lang, the hammer and sickle and the letters 'ONE LANG' in each corner.

Then in big letters it had:

STARVATION DEBENTURE The Wastepaper Commissar of the Soviet Slave State Promises to Repudiate to the Bearer — HIS PROMISE TO PAY £21,000,000 (which he has not got) On Payment of the Usual 5% Commission Contracted Outside the Moratorium,

Then on the other side of the 'debenture' it had 'A Pawnticket Policy of Paper Rations for Starving People.'

The Daily Telegraph and Sydney Morning Herald pilloried him daily. He was the 'Leader of the Fruit Machine State'. He was even Louis XIV, and they could hear him saying: 'The State? — I am the State.' A typical Telegraph line was this: 'Any bankrupt in civil life who attempted to defraud and provoke the Official Assignee as Lang is doing would be behind bars in the prison at Long Bay. As an example to the rest of the community it smells to the skies.'

Smith's Weekly said: 'Will members of the Labor Caucus continue to cringe before their bully until he has destroyed the State in complete wreckage? What has been the cost of John Thomas Lang? — you might set it down as a hundred million Sterling. Australia never before saw a Mad Dog as high priced as this one.'

But the interesting piece of knocking against Mr Lang came on 19 March 1932, the day of the long-awaited opening of the Sydney Harbour Bridge. Mr Lang had no love for State Governors as such. So he insisted on opening the bridge himself.

However, he was defeated in his aim. Captain Francis de Groot of the New Guard, wearing his old Army uniform, had mingled unnoticed with the mounted troopers. Mr Lang swore that these troopers were in league with the plot, otherwise de Groot in his antique uniform would never have got away with it. Then before Mr Lang had a chance to cut the ribbon, de Groot cantered forward and slashed it with his sword.

De Groot, of course, was arrested and some of the detail of the trial was particularly revealing. De Groot told the court that the Thursday preceding the opening day he had talked to Colonel Eric Campbell, leader of the New Guard. He asked Campbell what his intentions were for the bridge opening. Campbell replied that he planned to have the Premier arrested on a charge of fraud and corruption. De Groot said: 'Why not keep that till later. I think I have an idea of a better one that will bring ridicule upon Mr Lang, cause no injury to anyone and make the whole world laugh.' Campbell agreed that the idea was the best.

At the trial the defence brought forward a large number of witnesses, who were all laudatory of de Groot and his very disrespectful attitude towards Mr Lang. The judge noticed that many of them didn't stand or remove their hats when the national anthem was played. He highly approved of de Groot's action: 'I approved of it as a protest against Mr Lang and his Government. I think it was a very brave act and one that has the approval of all decent people in New South Wales.'

Mr Lamb, K.C., for de Groot, said he was a man who had done Mr Lamb said, 'that when the name of that of de Groot will stand out as the name of a patriot.'

The court found de Groot guilty, but incredibly fined him only £5, in default of hard labour. What's more, he got his sword back, which was to become a precious collector's item.

This event was so celebrated, Jack O'Hagan's music company published a song by John Quinlan titled: 'Colonel Campbell — Mister Lang'. The first verse went:

I had a dream last night. 'Twas a funny dream. I dreamt about two men we know, And it was just a scream, Now one was Mister Lang. I near fell out of bed. The other was Colonel Campbell And this is what they said:

Oh, Colonel Campbell Yes, Mister Lang, Why don't you scramble Your precious gang, They have been a source of worry Since we opened up the Bridge, And the way they cut that ribbon, It was downright sacrilege: But, Mister Lang You were astute, You prosecuted my friend De Groot: Then for doing what he did, Why the Court fined him five quid. Did that please you Colonel Campbell? It was worth it, Mister Lang.

J. T. Lang in his nineties is now revered. He makes regular appearances on radio and television, and his financial policies so reviled in the 1930s have even come to be admired and approved in the 1970s.

Two great legal men, very eminent in Australian politics, R. G. Menzies and H. V. Evatt, both were unloved and treated with strange suspicion. They were disliked particularly because of the Australian fear of and distaste for the intellectual. Writers to the Editor in Australian newspapers hardly ever refer to intellectuals, they call them pseudo-intellectuals; the suggestion being that they are always covering up with false knowledge.

Allan Dalziel, who was on Dr Evatt's staff when he was Leader of the Opposition, wrote in his book Evatt the Enigma that Australia became obsessed with hatred of Evatt. But the keenest knocker was Mr Eddie Ward, the Labor member for East Sydney. In his book The Last of the Queen's Men Ward described Mr Menzies 'as a man with the scowl of Mussolini, the bombast of Hitler and the physical proportions of Goering'.

Ward took to the House with his questions and his interjections. When the Prime Minister was talking he would say, just loud enough for him to hear, 'just a Melbourne snob'. He would say 'it is as false as the Prime Minister's eyebrows.'

There were constant acid questions, such as these on 21 May 1950:

Mr Ward: Is it a fact that the Prime Minister during his recent visit to Sydney was entertained at the home of Mrs Charles Lloyd Jones? Did the women who attended wear priceless jewels and mink coats?

Mr Menzies: I don't know the private entertainment which my wife and daughter and I accept is a matter which relates to my department. However, since the honourable member is good enough to be interested, I did not observe diamonds, I am not an authority on mink coats, but a number of coats worn were strongly reminiscent of those I have seen worn by opposition members' wives on similar occasions.

Mr Ward: Was any check made by the Commonwealth Security Service to see whether any Communists or Communist sympathizers were present? Following lobster cocktail and French champagne, was there general discussion on the Government's effort to restore value to the pound, at which general satisfaction was expressed?

Mr Menzies: I had no lobster cocktail. I don't drink French champagne. I did not see any Security people present. And I had an evening mercifully free from the contemplation of Communists.

A month later they were at it again.

Mr Ward said to Mr Menzies: 'At least I have never tried to address the House in a drunken state — like you are now.'

Mr Menzies: That is a lying and dirty remark.

Mr Menzies was ordered to withdraw by the Speaker and he substituted for lying 'typically inaccurate'. Mr Ward continued to interject so Mr Menzies called Ward to Dr Evatt: 'Keep your heeler at bay.'

Mr Ward again demanded a withdrawal and the eloquent Menzies' eyebrows were elevated as he feigned astonishment. 'Did I really describe the honourable member as a heeler? I apologize to all cobbler friends.'

He was wearing his frogman's flippers and bikinis. This was showing the new, dashing, outdoors Prime Minister.

But within months the erosion set in. There was the Voyager inquiry; that careless phrase about being 'huskily' with L.B.J.; there was the Vietnam war; when he went to Portsea on 11 December 1967, extraordinary stories came through The political battle and unpopularity within the party had become too much for him.

Gorton came to power and the image this time was of the battler yet a man of the people, the fighter pilot too, he had done his bit, a good bloke, leaning against the back fence, a beaut Aussie bloke.

His popularity also did not last. Gallery correspondents nicknamed him 'Bungles'. One of them wrote a book From Bob to Bungles, Ray Aitchison

The assault against John Gorton was Mr Edward St John, the member for Warringah, a brilliant lawyer, a man with the conscience of the Liberal Party a notice board of the Press Gallery.

In March 1969 the annual Press Gallery dinner went on to a late hour. The guest of honour and he stayed there enjoying himself At that time he offered to drive home the only woman journalist present, Miss Geraldine Willesee, aged nineteen, the daughter of a Labor Senator.

It was the night that the United States called off its bombing of North Vietnam, and the U.S. Ambassador had telephoned earlier in the evening, inviting Mr Gorton to call by and discuss it. This he did with Miss Willesee and his Press Secretary, Tony Eggleton. Presumably Ambassador Crook was vastly astonished at the Prime Minister turning up at his Embassy with a young girl.

Mr St John coldly attacked the Prime Minister in the House over this affair. Along with a lack of honesty he showed grave defects in character. His attacks outside the House were even more damaging. He told Sydney University students that John Gorton showed a lack of discretion and judgement and a positively frivolous attitude to the important duties of office. . . . There was an irresponsibility implicit in his appointment of inadequate men to the highest posts, a brashness in his attitude to the public service, there was his ambiguity, his vacillation, his centralism, his insensitivity and lack of regard for the rights and feelings of others.

At high speed he produced his book A Time to Speak which described Gorton as 'the worst Prime Minister in Australia's history'. There were also such lines as 'the appalling bankruptcy of our national leadership' and 'because of our egalitarianism and our easy ways we have managed to let the Prime Ministership fall into the hands of a man who, despite his education and background, is cast in the great Australian larrikin tradition'.

John Gorton was not over-skilful in defending himself against Mr St John, Q.C. Had he been less fumbling he could have made the man sound like a petty, carping wowser. The strongest reply had to come from Mrs Gorton, who had this poem pinned to the notice board of the Press Gallery. It was on blue-grey notepaper with the Commonwealth crest on top:

He is not old, he is not young, The Member with the Serpent's Tongue. The haggard cheek, the hungering eye, The poisoned words that wildly fly, The famished face, the fevered hand Who slights the worthiest in the land, Sneers at the just, condemns the brave And blackens goodness in its grave. (With apologies to William Watson) Bettina Gorton (Mrs John Gorton)

Alan Reid in his book The Gorton Experiment has described an extraordinary relationship between John Gorton and his pretty young secretary Ainslie Gotto. He said that when business was over for the day Gorton and Miss Gotto used to sit down and discuss serious matters, political and administrative. Tony Eggleton, Gorton's Press secretary, was one of the very few welcome at such sessions. But Eggleton, according to Reid, probably disliked the ferocity with which personalities were dissected and their futures pre-ordained.

When John Gorton dropped his old friend, Dudley Erwin, Minister for Air, from the Cabinet, Erwin gave one of the most unforgettable knocks. Erwin told a newspaper reporter over the telephone that he had been ejected by a political manoeuvre.

'What manoeuvre?' asked the reporter.

'It wiggles, it's shapely, and its name is Ainslie Gotto,' replied Erwin.

When Gorton finally lost the job of Prime Minister by his own casting vote in the Party room he was bitter. He told John Sorell of the Herald: 'One thing I hope is that there won't be quite the same amount of surreptitious dirt and filth thrown at me now. I hope this campaign to vilify me by some fly-by-night magazines will cease. There has been a definite attempt to destroy me. These people would make up and invent any charge against me. It's been sickening, like looking into a cesspool. I'm astonished that people could do it.'

After William McMahon became Prime Minister John Gorton began to look better and better in retrospect. He applause from 'Get Gorton Back' lovers

Critics accused the new Prime Minister of being incapable of making decisions, of being hideously inept on television, and of having only one rich asset, his pretty wife, Sonia. They said whenever he was in political trouble Sonia was required to have a baby.

The Melbourne Sun's columnist Jeff Hook produced a cartoon just after Mr McMahon announced his wife was to have a third child. The cartoon depicted Mr McMahon outside the maternity ward. 'I am delighted to announce the Government's popularity has been increased by one.'

As public opinion polls recorded the ever-sinking popularity of Mr McMahon and his Government Sir Robert Menzies came out of retirement to make another of his famous quotes: 'Only if we can recapture that first careless rapture.'

The Review in Melbourne did a satirical comment on that one: 'What could the old man have meant?' Billy quavered. 'I've always thought I was pretty rapturous. I play squash; I sing "Fascination" (the others shuddered) and all in all, I'm a very rapturous sort of Prime Minister, certainly the most rapturous since the last one.'

Mr McMahon made a world tour in November 1971. He took a team of Canberra Pressmen with him. He called on every Government as he went and the theory was that the resulting publicity would hugely enhance his prestige.

The two biggest news events on the tour were that Mr Nixon, President of the United States, knew so little about him he didn't even know how to pronounce his name, and the other big event was the revealing dress with splits down the side that Mrs McMahon wore to the White House.

The London Daily Mirror in its 1971 honours list gave Mrs McMahon the 'Ladder of Fame' award 'for giving her husband a leg up'.

The Deputy Prime Minister, Mr Anthony, said there was 'an almost deliberate campaign to denigrate and undermine the Prime Minister's overseas visit'. The Attorney-General, Senator Greenwood, went further. He said there was a tradition in Australia to knock the tall poppies and Mr McMahon was being 'subjected to a one-sided belittling campaign based on trivia'.

But do politicians ever have a hope of being loved? Will Rushton made the most delicate and well-timed knock when he arrived back in Australia after Christmas 1971.

'Politicians? You're very lucky not having any here.'
