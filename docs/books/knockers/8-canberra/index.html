Canberra

13 min read.

First published 1972

Surveys the long tradition of knocking Australia's purpose-built capital, from the 1909 naming competition through Robin Boyd's architectural verdict, the controversy over the $320,000 phallic fountain, and the $5 million artificial lake, charting how a city mocked as a sheep paddock and ghost town eventually learned to love itself.

Canberra was the one Australian city that was mocked and knocked even before it began. In 1909 the Australian Star ran a competition in Sydney to find a name for the national capital. 'What's in a name?' it said. 'A good deal. Some names are glorious, others are inglorious. The capital of Federated Australia should have a glorious name.'

So it offered a £20 gold watch to the person who found the glorious new name.

It was known already that the capital was to be in the Yass–Canberra area, but these names were too dull. So for nigh on three months the Star accepted suggestions every day. There was no evidence whatever of the Star giving away free watches, but the names were interesting. Here are some of those published.

Sydbourne Melba Washington City Palladium Paradise City of Gold and Fleece Bee-hive Coo-ee Coo-ee Commons Dulce Domum Home Sweet Home Shakespeare Jumbask City Bucalypta Great Stirlingborough City of Knutsford Kookaburra Mab-sha (Capital letters of all the capitals plus Auckland) Cook Haven Telegraph City Thirstyville Wheatwoolgold Gone Broke Yasin Moran City Palestine City

The suggestion of Palestine City was sent in, curiously, by a man named Moran. Down. Of course, skilled and cynical knockers thought this a fine idea. Thirstyville, one felt, might have been suitable as to Gone Broke would have been appropriate, at various stages of the nation's history. But they did not all sty-Mel-Ad-Bris-Ho, which was an inspired combination of Syd-Mel-Ad-Bris-Ho.

Lady Denman read out the name from a gold card-case to read out the name. She announced Canberra. The name that had graced the place, Coo-ee, Kookaburra or Dulce Domum — just dull, predictable. At the time, the knockers were in action long before the politicians. In 1910, the writer John Foster Fraser told his readers that the capital was going to cause civil war against Victoria. In 1923, Sydney could boast of an Anti-Canberra League. The League's manifesto stated that even now the project should be dropped and a State capital (presumably Sydney) become the headquarters of the Commonwealth. The League said:

Nobody claims that Canberra has any commercial or industrial future or a self-supporting future of any sort. It is going to be a sort of half-town, going to sleep when Parliament is out of session. The place lacks any known resources of coal and iron, of water-power, and it is even short of water in the sense that its drinking supply must be pumped up to the plateau as a permanent business. It is on no main railway line or road or artery of traffic.

There were howls about the cost, £1,500,000 spent already. Then a commission was appointed to run Canberra with a Chairman at £3,000. The Age was horrified. 'Canberra madness,' it said. The Herald said too that even if some New South Wales and Queensland members were keen to make their homes in the raw, new capital, they had failed to inspire enthusiasm among members from other States. The Prime Minister, Mr Bruce, was not exactly pining to leave civilisation and the normal ways of life to go there.

Nor did the Governor-General want to live there. In 1928 the Labor Daily said there had been some pertinent remarks about the absence of Lord Stonehaven from public functions. Admittedly he was at Government House to receive the aviator, Bert Hinkler, on a Wednesday night. His Excellency arrived in Canberra Wednesday morning, left again on Wednesday and had not been in Canberra since.

Lord Stonehaven's opinion of Canberra was this. It had one virtue. It was only 300 miles from Melbourne.

Come the opening on 9 May 1927, the accounts of speeches were euphoric, but on 19 May, the Age was revealing all that the place has been

With few exceptions, who went to the Federal Parliament in 1933 — whether politicians or public servants — went there with the same enthusiasm as agents of Government sent to an outpost of empire. They formed their place of abode into a small-scale imitation of the opening-up of new areas, rather as if the transport difficulties, the cost of living, the lack of social life were actually going to make life harder to face. A public servant was quoted as saying: 'In a hundred years this place is going to be a great city, but I do not expect to see it in my time.'

Senator Sir Hal Colebatch said in 1933:

Canberra will never be a success either as a seat of Government or as a place of residence. It is utterly wrong that so much money should be wasted on the place. . . . I do not shrink from saying that I have a strong personal preference for London rather than Canberra.

Politicians eventually became used to the idea that Coke was of the place, or the transport difficulties. Round this place, too, you find bicycles — for in this City of Dreadful Distance, the designers who placed everything at the maximum distance from everything else had a sublime faith in the continuity of the petrol supply, and, disdaining vulgar trams and trains, left the humble citizen at such times as this without any means of transport at all, except the occasional bus, which probably doesn't go where you want to go anyway. You have indeed three alternatives.

You may wait for the bus.

Or you may walk your twenty miles or so a day, your charts, sextant and compass slung on your shoulders.

Or you may decide that there is really no point in going out at all and, after a lapse of twenty years, be one day discovered dead under the billiard table by a retired Colonel from Poona.

A. W. Spurr, 1942:

Strangers especially find Canberra a maze, whose elfin mission is to torment the exalted ego in man. Many, quite certain that they are following a straight if seldom narrow path, unaccountably veer wide of their destination; others simply go round in circles.

One merrily gyrating motorist, after bidding long and furiously to get out of the National Capital, was moved to express extreme incredulity by a strangely recurring piece of statuary: 'Well, most towns have their Bobby Burns statue, but I'll go hopping if I've ever seen one before with three of them.'

Rupert Charlett, 1946:

Whether in addition to costing millions it ruined a good sheep station is another matter. However Canberra has the quaint charm of its own. For instance the poet who said that woman is the most unpredictable thing in Creation was not a patron of Canberra omnibus service. If Canberra transport were a concerto in one movement — adagio at that!

It is said that one patron, momentarily speechless when the bus came along precisely at 9.30 a.m. as scheduled, exclaimed: 'Great Scot! A Canberra bus on time.' It is said that the conductor blushed faintly and confessed: 'This is yesterday's bus.'

Peter Golding, 1949:

Canberra may be well planned. No doubt it is. But I want to know what is the good of town planning if the result can only be appreciated from the air? If you drive into Canberra without any previous knowledge of the place you will get lost. I defy you not to get lost.

Robin Boyd, 1954:

Certainly, the geometrical road pattern seen from the air looks something like Griffin's plan. That is one of the elements of the tragedy — Canberra followed the weaving road pattern of Griffin by altering almost everything else Griffin wanted done: the siting of important buildings and activities, the co-ordinated architecture he planned. Rather than the splendid capital built on a great plan, Canberra built 'temporary' public buildings and 'emergency' structures. A few satisfactory buildings are swamped in an enormous sprawling suburb with no centre. We started with a dream, an imaginative, brilliantly conceived plan. But we decided we could do it on the cheap. We had the blueprint, so now we could dispense with the architect. The result is Australia's biggest example of an Owner-built, do-it-yourself, handyman's job — the world's first do-it-yourself national capital.

1958:

The difficulty with Canberra is to find it. Majestic buildings which bear the marks of age turn out to be temporary, while those around them are permanent. Concentric circles provide infinite possibilities of getting lost. It is an excellent expression of bureaucratic existence without existing.

Then came Royalty. The Duchess of Kent described it as 'just a big country town'. Then came Prince Philip who has made it a cliché ever since. The standard joke is that everything is so clean, so pure in Canberra, as if there is not the dirt, the noise, the bustle of its big sisters. There is the Garden atmosphere that makes up the charm, and some even cried that the politicians would behave better if they were shoved about on Mr Risson's trams and occasionally looked at an old age pensioner not as some deputation but as a living, suffering human being. Canberra is a creation full of artificial people. Real cities grew out of the compost of centuries.

The Argus man in 1946 gave a fairly typical criticism:

It is grotesquely un-Australian. You feel it has no right among the gum trees, that this scattered community of flat, gleaming white buildings belongs to the inter-planetary world of Super-man rather than to Australia's political heart. . . . Even when Parliament is not in session, and there are no somnolent senators to give the lounge of the best hotel a Melbourne sabbath atmosphere seven days a week, one of the things that impresses you about Canberra, apart from its parkland spaciousness, is that adagio tempo. There are the buildings of a modern city, but the inanimate tranquillity of a small country town. And it is smug and snobbish, with civil servants sorting themselves in the various suburbs roughly in ratio to their annual incomes. Now if you live in Mugga Way. . .

This attitude irritated Lionel Wigmore in his book The Long View. He said Canberra was no different to any other city. No matter where you go people tend to cluster in suburbs with similar ranges of income. He said much of the criticism of Canberra came from writers who visited there all too briefly and did not understand the place. Usually their opinions were coloured by having to live in none too comfortable hostels. So the allegations of segregation and snobbery were favourite ammunition.

Some of the itinerant visitors, of course, write better than others. James Morris, of Manchester's Guardian, one of the prettiest writers of all the English Press brigade, went there in 1962:

When you first drive down its interminable avenues you can hardly help wondering how on earth they have taken so long to build it. The pace has been agonizingly slow compared with Brasilia, Rotterdam or even Harlow, and even today the public buildings are still embryonic, and the pattern of the place is only half formulated. Reason, you see, has long had the upper hand in Australia. This is not a state that often makes the heart sing. Not only did the great depression and the war impede the progress of Canberra, but it is only in the past few years, I am assured, that the Australians really decided they wanted the thing at all.

There is something that is indeed infinitely daunting about the site. So vast, so empty, so featureless, but for its low rim of hills so changeless, but for the shifting of its lovely colours with the progress of the sun. It really is like Australia in microcosm and just as the Australians, huddled in their sea-coast cities, seem to be always overhung by the immensity of the continent behind them, so the infant Canberra feels as though it can hardly compete with the tremendous blankness of this setting.

The new wave of knocking came in the 1960s and '70s. Canberra steadily acquired imposing new buildings such as the Supreme Court, the National Mint, the Canberra Theatre Centre, the National Library, the Academy of Science and the National University. So as Canberra really began to look like a national capital capable of attracting 100,000 visitors a year there was jealousy from other capitals at money that could have been spent on high schools in Melbourne, or freeways in Sydney or storage dams in South Australia.

In 1970 the Commonwealth Government spent $320,000 on a fountain to celebrate the bicentenary of the arrival of Captain Cook. It was modelled on the famous Geneva jet rising 420 feet in the air, but the Canberra jet went to

Engineers installed the jet near Regatta Point on Lake Burley Griffin. It ran on a wind meter varying the height of its column automatically according to the strength of gusts. When word came through that not only was the cost $320,000 but it would cost $28 an hour to run with an annual bill of up to $30,000 a year, there were howls of protest around the country. This $30,000 could have built a lovely school somewhere back of Bourke.

Queen Elizabeth II officially started the fountain on 25 April 1970. Canberra's phallic symbol has not always been in action since. Frequently it has wilted and died. Canberra residents will tell you that it stands up in strength according to the state of the economy.

Perhaps the lakes scheme caused the most anger. To make sense of the plan by Canberra's designer, Walter Burley Griffin, there had to be a large artificial lake as a focal point for the many Government buildings. The lake should have been created in 1927 but it took the Commonwealth thirty-six years to find the initiative and the money. The bill in 1963 was $5 million. This 'absurd frittering away of Commonwealth funds' was a standard complaint in State parliaments at every budget session. For example, Melbourne was pining for its Tullamarine international airport and the Melbourne Chamber of Commerce issued a report saying that the 'powers that be' in Canberra showed a selfish remoteness in their thinking and a lack of awareness of practical business. And it continued: 'It now appears that they regard their lakes and all other trappings as more important than an essential commercial development, which Tullamarine undoubtedly is.'

Sir Robert Menzies replied: 'These people have a limited conception of what a very great capital ought to be.'

Canberra people very quickly learned to love their lake and they also learned its importance. Even after one year a Canberra sociologist, Dr F. L. Jones, was able to say that already Lake Burley Griffin was serving the same purpose as the Yarra or

Canberra, like Washington, always will provide splendid knocking material. A famous English architectural critic, Mr J. M. Richards, recently gave it merciless treatment. He found Canberra almost devoid of any buildings of any interest or distinction, and he kept his most scathing comments for the city's newest, most beloved monument, the National Library.

He said: 'The most recent attempt to provide the Federal capital with a monumental work of architecture is a disaster. A lot of trouble has clearly been taken to achieve a high standard of finish, to use good materials and incorporate works of art. But the form of the building is a kind of scraped-down classical temple reminiscent of Mussolini's Italy.'

The Minister for the Interior, Mr Nixon, was stung. He replied: 'Mr Richards is apparently not a practising architect, so it is difficult to judge the work he criticizes alongside the work he has done himself. And not only that — he comes here from Britain, a country which has not produced a single building of merit in the post-war period. I am very angry. In my opinion, Mr Richards' comments are totally unjustified.'

Yet Canberra rolls with the knocks now rather better than it did. Canberra young people have discovered it is only two and a half hours from the ski resorts, two hours from the best beaches. It now has theatre, music, the best outdoor sculpture in the country. It has the best schools, the least noisy university, one football field to every 1,500 people, one cricket ground to 2,000, one hockey field to 3,000, one basket-ball court to 1,200, one tennis court to 1,000 and a golf course to every 25,000. The average level of family income is 20 per cent above the national average and there is one private car to every 2.9 people. It also has the highest attempted suicide rate of any city in Australia. Perhaps the passion for advancement is keener on the old one-time sheep station than elsewhere.

Yet the Department of Interior continues to produce a stream of booklets and brochures stressing that Canberra is the official paradise on earth. There is even one titled 'Wildlife in the ACT'. Actually knockers have often complained about the lack of wildlife in Australia, but this little booklet points out that the Australian Capital Territory is an ideal place to spot the Channel-billed cuckoo, the largest of the Australian cuckoos. Even more frequently you can see the fan-tailed cuckoo. There have been sightings in Canberra of this bird all the year round.

The booklet 'Wildlife in Canberra' has a picture which is, I think, supremely symbolic. It shows a high-flying flock of galahs.
