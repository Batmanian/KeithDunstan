Each Other

40 min read.

First published 1972

A history of the Melbourne-Sydney rivalry from the 1849 separation protests through the railway gauge dispute, the Federation capital battle, the Cook's cottage war, and the long Tullamarine-Mascot airport feud, arguing the two cities in 1972 knock each other with the same poisonous energy they always have.

In November and December 1849 there was a series of mass protest meetings in Melbourne. John Pascoe Fawkner, one of Melbourne's founders, told the gathering they were slaves under the thraldom of the robber people of Sydney. He said that he did not want them to take up muskets against Sydney—there were too few of them for that—but they had another power: passive resistance. He for one would set an example by refusing to buy all excisable goods.

The Separation Bill went through the House of Commons in London on 1 August 1850, but the news did not reach Melbourne until the evening of 11 November. Melbourne's glee, delight, relief in at last being rid of Sydney, reached the point of zaniness. The Argus, the staid Argus, produced an historic leader:

A spell seems to have fallen upon all around us. Our inkstand and gum-pot have been dancing reels this hour past; our faithless lamp has persisted in staggering towards our wine glass; and thrice have we been tricked into toasting our future Victoria in a bumper of whale oil . . . the best news ever to reach our shores.

Immediately beacons were lit all around the town, at Richmond, Collingwood and St Kilda. There was a 21-gun salute and a balloon went up which distributed parchment slips announcing the good news. One reporter wrote:

We unhesitatingly assert that there never was an occasion in any city in any part of the world in which the residents so readily, so cheerfully and so unanimously united in giving expression to their joy. Every house was illuminated. Even the most dirty hovels in the most dirty of all the dirty lanes in the city followed the example of their neighbour and gave some token of rejoicing.

So Melbourne began a week of celebration. There were fireworks, rockets, gunfire and barrels of tar blazed in the streets. The buildings in Elizabeth, Bourke, Swanston and Collins Streets were bedecked with bunting and great transparencies, many of them far from kind to Sydney.

The Bush Inn sported a fascinating series. One showed the Sydney dragon disgorging all the money 'stolen' from Melbourne. Another showed Britannia addressing her children, Sydney and Melbourne. 'You are naughty children, I must separate you; Phillip my boy, go and make sure you behave like a man; Sydney, my good fellow, make sure you don't interfere with the boy.' Sydney, who is miserable in appearance with the strong manly Phillip, replies: 'It's a darned shame mother, the boy can't take care of himself.' Phillip, who has stacks of gold all around him, says: 'Don't believe him Ma. I can take care of myself. You'll see if I don't beat Sydney.'

Robert Ferguson, a linen draper in Lonsdale Street, had a transparency showing Sydney as a decrepit old man, borne down by grief and remorse, crying: 'Oh Port Phillip, we have lost thee, what shall I, poor Sydney, do without you?' Port Phillip, magnificent-looking like a rich English earl, has a bag of gold at his side. He says to Sydney: 'Through my youth you have fleeced me, now I give you a last adieu.'

How did all this go down in Sydney? The Port Jacksonites, apparently, were far from impressed. The Argus representative on the spot reported:

A very brief intercourse with people here has sufficed to convince me that there is a deplorable misunderstanding of the position and importance of Victoria. I use the word deplorable because the want of proper information is evidently one great cause of the prevalent feeling of contempt, which, whether real or assumed, very commonly finds expression here whenever Port Phillip becomes the topic of conversation. Separation however appears to be the one great puzzle. I have met with but few, who profess to understand the anticipated advantages of this great achievement, for none willingly acknowledges, that Victoria has been wronged by an alien legislature, and plundered by a rapacious Government. The week of rejoicings which hailed your emancipation, is ridiculed as a week of licensed folly. I have not met one amongst the many prophets who predicted the ruin of Port Phillip, who has seriously asked himself what Sydney can do for Port Phillip, that she cannot do for herself.

A source of continuous friction between the cities for 106 years was the break in the railway gauges. Britain already had been through the agony. In 1845, England had 1,900 miles of 4' 8½" gauge and 270 miles of 7' gauge, and in that year a Royal Commission pointed out that a break of gauge was an evil which would destroy half the benefits of the railway system. So Britain settled for 4' 8½" and Lord Grey recommended the gauge to the Australian colonies.

But Sydney, by act of the Legislative Council on 7 July 1852, went for 5' 3" and the other colonies followed suit. All would have been fine except that a year later the Sydney Railway Company changed its engineer. James Wallace succeeded Francis Shields, and Mr Wallace decided 4' 8½" was for New South Wales.

By this time Victoria had already ordered rolling-stock and refused to withdraw. The Victorian Legislative Council even appointed a Select Committee to report on the best gauge for Victoria. Incredibly, it gave a warning on the dangers of mixed gauges, yet still recommended 5' 3" for Victoria.

So the colonies built their vast railway networks: 4' 8½" in New South Wales, 5' 3" in Victoria. There was a blissful, naive feeling that it did not matter; the cities were so far apart and, besides, the real purpose of the railway system was to bring goods from country areas into the capital. The cheapest and best method for inter-colonial travel would always be by sea.

The railway systems did not actually meet until 1883, and already in 1882 colonial parliaments were discussing a matter of infinite delicacy—which colony would control the exchange point. The border was the Murray River. On the north side was the New South Wales town of Albury and on the other bank was the Victorian town of Wodonga.

Almost every week anxious members asked the question in their respective Houses of Assembly: which city would triumph? Mr Thomas Bent, the Victorian member for Railways, one of the wiliest, most astute politicians Australia ever produced, went to Sydney. There he worked a master compromise, which was to be typical of the methods used to assuage sensitive feelings in the rival cities.

This was the Bent plan. Passengers travelling from Melbourne to Sydney went right through on the 5' 3" to Albury, where they changed to the Sydney 4' 8½" train. Passengers travelling the other way from Sydney to Melbourne went on the 4' 8½" to Wodonga, where they changed to the 5' 3". It seemed a master stroke.

Mr Bent told the Victorian Legislative Assembly that rivalry was so intense between the two cities he didn't have any choice—he had to work this arrangement.

The celebration for the linking of the cities by rail took place at Albury on 14 June 1883, and there was a mighty banquet in an engine-shed, transformed with bunting for the occasion and actually lit by electricity with power provided by a New South Wales engine. Lord Loftus, the Governor of New South Wales, came by train from Sydney and the Marquis of Normanby, the Governor of Victoria, came by rail from Melbourne. There were a thousand guests.

Melbourne Punch was cynical about the whole affair. On the day of the big celebration it wrote:

So determined are the two Governments that everything today shall be done in a half-and-half manner that the caterers have had strict orders even to cut the sandwiches with a slice of Victorian bread on top and New South Wales bread on the bottom. The difficulty about the ham has been got over by purchasing the whole of it from stock cured exactly on the border line.

Mr Bent's beautiful compromise did not work out as smoothly as expected. Even railway trains can be fickle, connections did not go smoothly and frequently passengers failed to get the Sydney train or vice versa, and laboriously had to go by coach the three miles between Albury and Wodonga. Then Victoria was a Protectionist colony, New South Wales was Free Trade. So upon arriving in Wodonga, there was the humiliation of having one's bags gone through by the Victorian customs officers.

Then there was the rivalry of the Railway Refreshment rooms. Mr Bent, in a debate on 17 September 1885, said the food at Wodonga was excellent, but as far as he was concerned he had never had worse accommodation than was available at the Albury refreshment room.

According to an Argus correspondent, the through trip from Melbourne to Sydney took twenty-five hours. But for red tape it would have taken eighteen. Mr Duncan Gillies, the Minister for Railways, revealed the Sydney strategy which finally caused the breakdown of the compromise. The New South Wales Railways honoured their obligation of going right through to Wodonga, but always the train arrived in Albury 20 to 30 minutes before the advertised time, then stopped there and did not go on until there was only just time according to the time-table for the passengers to switch over to the Victorian train in Wodonga. This meant that the passengers had half an hour with nothing to do on the New South Wales side and always they had their breakfast at the Albury refreshment room. If there was any time at Wodonga the passengers just walked up and down. They weren't hungry any more.

In 1886, the two colonies worked another compromise: Albury became the one exchange station and Wodonga became the inter-colonial changing yard for cattle. Wodonga was never happy about this. The local citizens even set up a Wodonga Vigilance Committee. They complained they had received the bad end of the deal.

Separation gave Melbourne a certain confidence, but the ingredient which turned on an inspired, self-assured arrogance was the discovery of gold. Gold-hungry immigrants poured into Melbourne. Just observe the population growth. In July 1851 Melbourne was a mere 23,000. In 1861 it was 126,000, a growth of five and a half times in a decade. In 1870 it was 200,000 and Melburnians were so pleased with themselves they were calling the city 'Marvellous Melbourne'. In 1881, it was 281,000 and by the early 1890s half a million, and the population of Marvellous Melbourne for a time outstripped that of Sydney.

In 1888, Melbourne celebrated the Centennial Exhibition, a vast affair. The Exhibition building left from the 1880 exhibition was added to and for this affair the organizers added temporary structures which covered almost the entire gardens. The exhibition was to celebrate Australia's first hundred years. Seeing that Captain Phillip made his landing at Botany Bay and not Port Phillip, it was reasonable to presume that the celebration should take place in Sydney, but the New South Wales Parliament rejected large-scale festivities. Melbourne newspapers were very patronizing. If Sydney could not afford an expensive exhibition then Melbourne was only too happy to take over. As the Argus put it, this could only be a success if expense were no object. Sydney bore this bravely and sent its warm greetings on opening day.

So as Federation approached, slowly and tortuously, the citizens of Melbourne did not see why their capital should not become the capital of Australia. Or, if it could not become the capital of Australia, they would do their level best to make sure that Sydney never became the capital.

Melbourne even made a brilliant, if unsuccessful, move to become the capital in 1852. On 14 January, the Victorian Legislative Council passed unanimously a resolution that an address be sent to Her Majesty, Queen Victoria, that the Governor-General of all her Australian possessions should reside in Victoria. The petition was based on the premise that Victoria was brimful of gold, the richest of the colonies, and therefore this was the logical spot for her top representative. Understandably this didn't go down very well in Sydney. The Empire in Sydney said: 'A pure piece of claptrap, and mere bait to the dissatisfied people—a mode of making them believe, that after all their selfishness, their squatting council was highly patriotic. Whether the bait will take remains to be seen. Our neighbours, from the date of birth of their settlement, have been disposed to indulge in visions of local greatness not always distinguished by perfect sobriety.'

The Argus, in retaliation, actually preferred the comments of the Empire rather than those of the 'namby pamby' Sydney Morning Herald and its 'ebullition of feeble anger'. Indeed the Sydney Morning Herald did reply in a spirit of lofty disdain. The resolution was both unjust and offensive to the people of New South Wales, yet the august journal pointed out it would not stoop to 'ridicule' or 'vituperation'; it would remain of a calm temper. It was better to make just a few sensible observations.

Would there be any reason why the junior should take precedence of the senior? New South Wales has passed the grand climacteric; she is in the 65th year of her age. Victoria is yet in her minority; but 15 summers have shone on her brow. As a colony she is in her tenderest infancy, barely 15 months have elapsed since Separation. As to her filial relation to the elder colony, the only question is whether the relation be that of child or grandchild.

What she asks is spoilation. She would rob us and make our gracious Sovereign a party to the theft.

The Sydney Morning Herald later sent a reporter to give a true picture of this upstart, would-be national capital. He wrote:

There is a striking difference between the capitals of the two colonies. Melbourne is no doubt a money-making, flourishing and promising city, but it has a kind of temporary or tinsel appearance, which at once strikes with astonishment the visitor from other parts of the world. You look in vain for the stately edifices, the solid mansions, the magnificent churches, the splendid shops which beautify and grace the metropolis of all the Australian colonies. Its churches are small, few in number and as deficient as they can possibly be of everything like architectural arrangement or display. Of public buildings we shall say nothing as there are none worthy of the name. Sydney has grown up slowly and naturally but Melbourne seems to have risen into existence by a 'stroke of the enchanted wand'. Sydney is the production of thought, labour and art, extending over three-quarters of a century, while Melbourne is the growth of only a few years, the result of a capricious fortune, the splendid pageant of the hour.

Then came the first of many Sydney comments on the Yarra.

But there is one advantage which it does not possess, and with respect to which Sydney shines pre-eminent among all cities in the world. It possesses no harbour, except such as is afforded by the river Yarra, a miserable stream which will admit vessels of ordinary tonnage.

The Sydney Morning Herald did admit to the existence of Port Phillip Bay but this was so far off that it made the cost of bringing goods up to Melbourne enormous.

The Argus naturally let loose its own correspondent in Sydney. The Argus man made no mention of harbour, river or stately homes; he based his attack on corruption and vice. There was nothing too vicious, too horrid for that town and he made the suggestion that the 'vice', 'profligacy' and intrigue even began in the Governor's mansion.

The early jealousies which began with Separation sprouted much more vigorously and splendidly during struggles for Federation. It was a cash thing. Both cities were convinced that if the other became the title-holder then all business would flow in that direction, attracted by the glory of the Governor-General. The manoeuvring really began at the National Convention held in Adelaide in March, April, May, 1897. It was agreed then that the site for the capital should be decided by the Federal Parliament. This was debated by the Legislative Council of New South Wales and it was suggested that the words 'determined by Parliament' should be omitted and the words 'in Sydney or the colony of New South Wales' be inserted.

This amendment was wiped out by the Federal Convention which sat in Melbourne from January to March in 1898. There was a brilliant move by Sir Edward Braddon, the Tasmanian representative. If there was this collision between Sydney and Melbourne maybe he could get the capital in Hobart. That failed. Mr W. J. Lyne, later Sir William, moved an amendment that the Federal Territory should be some place in New South Wales. Mr Alexander J. Peacock, also later knighted, put a counter-amendment that the Federal Territory should be within the colony of Victoria. The first was knocked out by a majority of 28 votes and the second by 30 votes.

The next move was for a Federation referendum to take place on 3 June 1898. Campaigning began in early March and the only city to provide a truly anti-Federation group was Sydney. The electors were called upon to say 'Yes' or 'No' to the Federal Enabling Bill. The Bulletin, which was very pro-Federation, called the 'antis' the 'Anti Billious Mob'. The popular argument used against Federation was this: that the other States were after New South Wales money. Tasmania was virtually bankrupt, land-booming Victoria, after having gone bankrupt through a shameless policy of greed, now wanted to wed itself to the one colony with a surplus, New South Wales. Then there was the capital question, as the Bulletin put it; the anti-billious mob was spreading the message that out east there was just one object: 'to make Melbourne the capital of the Commonwealth and thus leave Sydney a mere, dead provincial town bleaching on the shores of the river time.'

One of the leaders of the antis was A. G. Taylor, a very tall and brassy gentleman, according to the Bulletin. He wrote a series of articles for the Australian Star. He abused 'the little duck-pond Tasmania' for trying to grab an equal number of Senate votes as New South Wales. He said:

It is an open secret that Sydney is not to be the capital of the Commonwealth, that Melbourne, as Sydney has already been the capital of Australia for over a century, but the captious cancellation of her claim . . . will do much towards ruining Sydney as commercial centre and enterprising emporium of the world's wares. It is true that the beautiful harbour has put us beyond the predatory grasp of her envious neighbours but a lot of trade will centre around the capital of the continent where the Governor-General will preside. This is a bait number one for the colony which is to be made tender meat for the political confidence men.

It was difficult to know whether the Premier George Reid was pro- or anti-billious. One time, to the fury of Melbourne newspapers, he referred to Victorians as 'thieves and wolves'. The Sydney Morning Herald was soberly and sensibly pro-Federation.

In 1923 it was suggested that Billy Hughes should appoint a Minister for Harmony to conduct the interstate battles.

The Daily Telegraph was almost hysterically against. It brought a special anti-bill issue which it distributed free to every elector in New South Wales.

On every page this issue had anti-bill advertisements which ran down the page in long columns like old-fashioned theatre advertisements.

This was one:

The Convention Bill INVOLVES
Doubling our Taxation.
Halving our Trade.
Appropriation of our Surplus by the Southern States.
Removal of the Capital to Victoria.
Government by the small States.
The Perpetuation of Victorian Bonuses.
Constant Ill-Will between the States.
Creation of a String of Insolent Provinces.
Injury to Sydney.

And another:

VOTE Yes and say extinction of the claims of N.S.W. to contain the capital of United Australia.
The Momentous THIRD.
The Bill should die tonight.
Killed by an Outraged Democracy.
Be on the side of Safety and Vote No.
The Bill Spells Calamity to this Colony.
Don't be Misled by Mere Sentiment.
Don't be Beguiled by Bands and Bunting.
Strike Out Yes.
And Save New South Wales.

Voting day was Friday and David Jones in Sydney patriotically closed for the day so that voters could get to the polls. The Leader of the Opposition, Mr Lyne, had a last say: 'Every elector who does not want to see the capital in Melbourne or buried in some Victorian village will Vote No.'

There were majority Yes votes in every State, but the New South Wales majority was not good enough. Originally Victoria and New South Wales fixed a minimum poll vote of 50,000, but the New South Wales Parliament raised its figure to 80,000, or one-quarter of the electors on the roll. Seeing that there was no compulsory vote, this was a very high figure. So on polling day the New South Wales result was 71,595 YES and 66,228 NO. So Federation was again delayed and the Sydney Daily Telegraph took the result as a personal triumph.

The next conference took place in Melbourne during January 1899. It was a gathering of all the colonial premiers and there was another famous compromise: the Federal capital would be in New South Wales. It would be contained in a special capital territory of one hundred square miles and it would be not less than a hundred miles from Sydney.

To the simple soul this would appear to be a triumph for New South Wales. Not so. There was an awful flaw in this. Until the Federal Parliament decided on the precise location for the capital in New South Wales, the Federal members would meet in Melbourne. The anti-billious team in Sydney was aghast, they thought their fat little premier, George Reid, had been duped by the smooth characters in Melbourne.

The belief was that Parliament would only have to meet in Melbourne once and the diplomats would gather there, all Federal servants, heads of companies, would settle into their warm nests, things would begin to slide and the capital would be there for ever. A correspondent to the Sydney Morning Herald put it all in a few lines: 'Does anyone believe that when once established in Melbourne there will be the smallest disposition to leave that central—indisputably more convenient domicile?'

Meanwhile Bathurst, Yass, practically every major New South Wales town began to preen itself as a Federal capital candidate. Even Ballarat, although it wasn't in New South Wales, at one stage believed it had a hope. Victorians, of course, pushed for Albury, the nearest New South Wales town to Melbourne. Once upon a time it was the hated rival of Wodonga, but now it was Melbourne's friend, and one had only to look at the map to see it was economically tied to Victoria.

In December 1898 Melbourne Punch virtually stated that Albury would be the future capital and it brought out a special Albury supplement. This was larded with full-page photographs, sylvan views along the Murray, noble shots of the main street, views around the district which depicted Albury as an Eldorado or Elysium of the border. Land prices in Albury soared and for months not a block was on sale.

The crisis began to give off fumes as the New South Wales Parliament opened its sittings for 1899. First the Legislative Assembly had to vote on George Reid's resolution that the capital would be in New South Wales, but not less than a hundred miles from Sydney. Normally an intercolonial cricket match would be the only event worth covering in Sydney but this February and March the Melbourne newspapers sent their special writers to Sydney. On 21 February, the Argus man wrote: 'The visitor to Sydney cannot escape astonishment at finding how deeply the impression has bitten into the minds of Sydneyites, pure and simple, that Melbourne has out-manoeuvred Sydney on the question of the Federal capital. An astonishing number of Sydneyites believe they have been "got at" by Melbourne and "sold" by their own representatives.'

And it was true, Sydney was very upset. One Sydney newspaper suggested that the Federal capital should be in Melbourne for the first three years and Sydney the next, and eventually the permanent capital should be built exactly half-way between the two.

There was particular alarm among businessmen and shopkeepers. That weekend Sydney's Sunday Times published a survey showing the general feeling of despondency. One businessman said: 'Mr Reid has simply fooled Sydney, and his action has put Sydney back for an indefinite period. The visitors from England will regard Melbourne as the leading city in Australia and make it their headquarters.'

Another said: 'A large amount of capital must be diverted from Sydney to Melbourne.' A hotelkeeper moaned that no leading hotel in Sydney would be able to keep up its present standards and there would be large-scale dismissal of employees. Once the Governor-General took up residence in Melbourne, there would be a boom over there and a shift in population, and depression would hit Sydney. One correspondent to the Sunday Times just headed his letter: 'God Help Us.'

The anti-billious team made plans for a 'monster petition' to be presented to Parliament. On every street corner in Sydney they had tables and chairs, paper and bottles of ink. Beside the tables were signs DOWN WITH MELBOURNE . . . COME AND SAVE SYDNEY . . . TRUST TO THE UPPER HOUSE.

In just a few days the 'anti' team gathered 14,780 signatures and in the assembly Mr McLaughlin MLA told the House that Melbourne would be the home for the Governor-General, the seat for the High Court and all the judges; the Admiral of the Australian fleet would take his ships there permanently and all the great navigation companies would make Melbourne their terminal station. Immense sums of money would be paid in salaries to Federal public servants in Melbourne, and that city would expand only until its own arrogance was satisfied. Correspondingly Sydney would dwindle and the shores of Port Jackson would become almost depopulated.

Until now the Melbourne newspapers had been just reasonably sour, but as their men sent home these anti-Melbourne sentiments, the Argus, Age, Punch, etc. turned out the most anti-Sydney editorials ever printed.

Melbourne Punch opened its blast:

The assertion that the Commonwealth will never be able to shift the Federal metropolis from the clutches of the rapacious Victorians is all bunkum, and is intended merely to give a specious show of reason to the clamour of the big publican and the portly warehouseman and the great greengrocer.

The greed of Sydney is proverbial. In all the intercolonial dealings for years she has grasped the lion's share with shameless rapacity. Victoria has been called upon in a score of ways to bolster up the mother metropolis, and this same greed is responsible for the general outcry. Nobody supposes that a thought for Australian interests influences the attitude of the Sydney fatman. When he abuses Reid for not having secured the capital for Sydney he is speaking from the bottom of his pocket and thinking only of how the sales of beers and his meat and groceries and his building sites will be affected during the few years when the business of the Commonwealth will be conducted in Melbourne. New South Wales is to have the capital eventually, the Bill secures her that, and Sydney is foaming at the mouth because in the meantime Melbourne is to be the Federal Washington. Very well . . . let's reverse the whole business. Give the capital to Victoria, for Ballarat say, and allow Sydney the privilege now conferred on Melbourne.

How will that please the greedy sister? If the best interests of the Commonwealth had been preserved Ballarat would have been declared the best possible site for the capital, but the Premiers yielded to the provincial greed of New South Wales and gave that State the capital as a bribe to accept the Union. This is not enough, Sydney wants all; like the horse-leech's daughter, she has cried 'Give, give', ever since Federation became a really live political question. It is all extremely nauseating.

In the same issue Punch did a satirical sketch. It gazed into its crystal ball to the year 1905 when Sydney and Melbourne had already been at each other's throats for five years. Premier George Reid called out the Sydney Lancers, and, mounted on a white charger, marched on Melbourne. They crossed the Blue Mountains and the Army reached Melbourne in eight days, for 'victory or death'. On the way they laid waste all pubs, leaving behind a long white track of desolation and drought. There was a fierce battle at Brunswick, but the Sydney Lancers carried all before them.

From Brunswick the Army went by cable trams to the city and there they seized the Governor-General and his household, and the Federal Parliament. Whereupon they took all back to Sydney. The following year the Melbourne Scots Regiment marched on Sydney and reversed the process. Finally all this became so tedious and so expensive that a general conference was held. It was decided that the Commonwealth Parliament would be fixed half a mile high in a balloon. The balloon would be anchored six months a year over Melbourne and six months a year over Sydney.

In subsequent years it was found, during vigorous debates, that occasionally honourable members fell out of their balloon, but they went largely unmourned by the populace.

But it was The Age that really stirred Sydney. This editorial was reprinted complete in Sydney newspapers. It was quoted in Parliament, reprinted in newspaper letters, and whenever the anti-Federal campaign was lagging it was handed around among Sydney campaigners.

A truly patriotic Sydney citizen always felt this Christian virtue ebb away at the mention of Melbourne. There was something so malign in the wickedness of this 'Queen City' presuming to grow up on the banks of the Yarra, and eclipse in population the mother settlement on the shores of 'the Beautiful Harbor', that human patience faltered in the presence of such iniquity. Sydney people never forgot their dignity of city. Victoria at the best was nothing but an offshoot of New South Wales—only the colony of a colony. We were no more territorially than a magnified 'cabbage garden'. That such a speck of a colony should presume to prosper at all after her impudent separation from New South Wales was exasperation enough, but that we should have done so under a policy of protection, building up our industries far in advance of the parent state, and naming our city a centre of populousness and opulence which Sydney has toiled after in vain, was a provocation which no properly constituted Sydneyite could be expected to look upon without indignant passions surging in his bosom.

Sydney never had the smallest chance of being the capital of Federated Australia. In the first place the climate is not fit for the home of a progressive people. It was evolved when Dame Nature was surrounded by her stewpans; and it has never lost the character she then gave to it. It must be the depressing atmosphere of Sydney which has ever laid like a pall upon the energies of her citizens, and kept them half a century in the rear of Victorian progress. For this reason alone—the all-dissolving and infectious limpness in the air—a Federal Parliament would never have dreamed of making its capital in Sydney, a deed to which any such preliminary condition would have barred the Victorian entry.

This was the beauty about the Sydney-Melbourne struggle; it kept leaders supplied with a continuing story. No sooner had The Age turned its finest prose to the subject of Sydney, than the Daily Telegraph used its best leader-men to return the compliment.

The Telegraph said:

The 'Pall over Sydney' which at present exists only in the imagination of the Age, is one of those transparent fictions which it would be needless to refer to but that it shows the deep-rooted jealousy of this surely-progressing city south of the Murray.

The Age, according to The Telegraph, had shown absolute effrontery and it continued:

Melbourne, with its losses of 30,000 to 40,000 people in half a dozen years, while Sydney has gained fully a like number in the same time; Melbourne with its dearth of shipping, while Sydney Harbour is full; Melbourne, the tomb of a thousand and one financial skeletons—would have had some hope of booming again under the attractions of a costly Federal Government, in spite of its hot winds in summer and its winter fogs.

The Lower House in Sydney passed the new Federation Bill, but it struck trouble in the Upper House. The Council passed an amendment, similar to the one used before. The bill could only become law if it received one-quarter of the votes cast, i.e. 80,000. As one critic put it, if the bill received 79,000 FOR and only one AGAINST, then it was defeated. There were 324,000 electors on the roll but Sydneysiders, no doubt influenced by that stewpot climate mentioned by The Age, were not good at turning out for the polls. There was still no compulsory vote.

This was too much for Melbourne. The New South Wales Legislative Council was described as the house of fossilized specimens. They were described as a house of miserable old men, who represented nobody but themselves. And there was one beautiful line in the Bulletin: 'They hold their places by the vote of not one man, or woman, or dog, in all New South Wales.'

The Age too turned on fresh fury. It ran a feature giving a run-down of all the acts of 'sheer provincialism in history', starting with jealousy between the cities of ancient Greece, the Phoenicians and the Thessalonians, working forward through Persia, Thebes, Florence, Pisa, Parma, the Balkan States, the creation of Washington to the present day. The trouble was they said, the New South Wales Legislative Council believed the Southern Hemisphere was bounded by Botany Bay and the North Shore and that was it.

So the Council imposed impossible conditions on the Lower House. The Premier, George Reid, acted at last. Just as the Bulletin had pointed out contemptuously the Legislative Council was not an elected body. The members were Government appointees. So Mr Reid announced twelve new members, and they were all quite amenable gentlemen who knew exactly what their Premier wanted. The Daily Telegraph was upset and made great cries about 'the death of Democracy', but this was the end of a deadlock. The two Houses met and the Legislative Council amendment was withdrawn.

The second referendum was held and this time there was no doubt. The counting recorded in the Daily Telegraph was 101,274 FOR and 82,701 AGAINST.

The battle for the capital was now virtually over; that is, as far as Melbourne was concerned. There was a ten-year struggle before Parliament chose the lucky district in New South Wales to be blessed with the future capital territory. A New South Wales Government Royal Commission to investigate sites was launched in November 1899.

Its recommendations were not even considered, for Federal Parliament instituted yet another Royal Commission on 14 January 1903, to report on sites at Albury, Armidale, Bombala and Lake George. The Commission did not like any of these and selected Dalgety in the Southern Monaro.

Federal Parliament agreed to Dalgety and the bill even received the Royal assent on 15 August 1904, but the struggle was far from over. Negotiations went on and on between the Parliaments, Federal and State. Finally they agreed on the Canberra-Yass site and 900 square miles in this area actually became Commonwealth Territory on 1 January 1911.

That was the story of the birth of Canberra, another compromise, and to ease inter-city jealousies it had to be half-way between the two. Actually Sydney won on the deal because it was not precisely half-way. The actual figures are 148 air miles from Sydney and 315 from Melbourne. The anti-billites who direly predicted that the Federal capital would linger on in Melbourne indefinitely were almost right. Federal Parliament continued to sit in Melbourne until 1927. Also it was home for a succession of Governors-General, but it did not become the headquarters for the Australian Admiral and his fleet nor did Sydney become depopulated, a withering thing on the shores of time.

So inter-city relations improved, but there were constant irritations. In 1933, a Melbourne philanthropist, Mr Russell Grimwade, presented Victoria with Captain Cook's cottage. He had it shipped to Melbourne from Great Ayton in Yorkshire and re-erected very charmingly in the Fitzroy Gardens. There were plenty of knockers. There were those who claimed that Captain Cook had never lived in the cottage, or if he had, it was only for a very short period. Others thought we were showing a 'lack of reverence for history' and behaving like William Randolph Hearst in the United States.

Sydney was particularly upset. What did Captain Cook have to do with Melbourne? His history was entirely associated with the East coast and Botany Bay in particular. Mr Russell Grimwade pointed out that the Captain's first sighting of Australia was actually a stretch of the Victorian coastline, Cape Everard.

Said Mr Grimwade: 'Cook's sighting of the Victorian coast was an example of his discrimination—Victoria being so much harder to see than New South Wales.' He said too that he had taken particular care that no future impoverished Victorian Government could ever sell the cottage. It must always remain the property of Victoria. This was a condition of the gift.

Moves were started in New South Wales to buy a rival Captain Cook cottage. After all, the Captain had been reasonably peripatetic. There was a Cook cottage at Marton in Yorkshire. It was proposed to the New South Wales Premier, Mr Bertram Stevens, that this cottage be re-erected at Kurnell, at the very spot where the Captain set foot at Botany Bay in 1770.

The move did not come off. It turned out that the cottage at Marton had been destroyed in 1786.

With some glee, the Herald in Melbourne wrote: 'Perhaps Sydney will be able to get a good substitute—say, the cottage that was once rented by Captain Cook's brother-in-law, or his cousin's step-father.' Even so Melbourne never quite got over a faint feeling of guilt. In order to solidify the matter and make it clear for all time, the Victorian Premier, Sir Henry Bolte, called for a little ceremony at Cape Everard, during the Captain Cook Bicentenary celebrations in 1970. On 20 April 1970 he changed the name of Cape Everard to Point Hicks, in honour of Lieutenant Hicks, the man aboard the Endeavour who actually had the honour of first sighting Victoria through his telescope. Sir Henry led a 200-strong party to the water's edge at Point Hicks to unveil a plaque and celebrate the 200th anniversary of the sighting.

The knocking battle went on. Just before Sir Henry did his unveiling, Geoffrey C. Ingleton, a naval historian, wrote that it was doubtful that Cook had ever seen Cape Everard. From ten miles off it was too low. After examining the log and Endeavour's position at noon and 6 p.m. on 20 April 1770, there was only one conclusion: Point Hicks must have been a cloud formation merging into the coastline to the north.

Mr Ingleton thereupon urged Sir Henry not to expunge the name Cape Everard from the charts. Sir Henry was unmoved.

After the Second World War there was another battle, just like the old battle of the broken railway gauge. Melbourne did not have an international airport. It was noted that the well-heeled tourists and businessmen always stepped ashore from their Qantas, BOAC or Pan-American aircraft in Sydney and often they went no farther. For this reason the U.S. Rest and Recreation leave troops from Vietnam always went to Sydney. Kings Cross, not St Kilda, provided their love and romance, a matter involving millions of dollars every year. Sir Maurice Nathan, Melbourne's leading protagonist for an international airport, said in 1965: 'This just doesn't mean loss of tourists. Look, the Victorian Promotion Committee has been negotiating for a big German firm to come to Melbourne. Now I learn today they're not coming. You know why? No jetport. They're coming to Sydney because they will be exporting some of their stuff by plane.'

Sir Henry Bolte listed seventy cities smaller than Melbourne that had a jetport. At one stage he even listed the city's little namesake, Melbourne, Florida, which had a population of 100,000.

From the beginning Sydney businessmen believed that Melbourne's move for an international airport was a plot to grab both trade and tourists. The early shots were fired in the Sydney City Council. Alderman Moran described the whole affair as a 'political conspiracy'. After all, the Prime Minister, Sir Robert Menzies, was a Melbourne man, the deputy Prime Minister, Mr McEwen, was Victorian and look, the Federal Treasurer, Mr Harold Holt was also from Melbourne. The airport at Mascot was substandard, yet Commonwealth money was being used to build an international airport at Tullamarine in Victoria. 'We will have a horse and buggy airport in a supersonic age,' he said.

Some of Alderman Moran's words had the classic ring of 1898-9:

Sydney is the premier city of Australia. It was the first place in Australia to be settled. Sydney built the first railway in the Commonwealth. It is the finest seat of learning and the academic centre of the nation. Mascot was Australia's first airport, blessed with a climate that allows air operations to go unhindered twenty-four hours a day, every day of the year. Yet we now find that the first airport built in Australia is being relegated to second place because the Federal Government is wilfully conspiring to downgrade Sydney's airport to second place.

The Age pondered these lines and said: 'It is only a matter of time before Alderman Moran calls for the formation of a New South Wales Air Force, with fighters ready to buzz any aircraft rash enough to head south across the Murray, instead of forcing their way through the seagulls over Sydney.' The Minister for Civil Aviation, Senator Henty, said it was utter nonsense; Sydney had not been by-passed for Melbourne. Alderman Jensen, the Sydney Lord Mayor, led a deputation to the Prime Minister, but it was turned away. Senator Henty went further: he said that Sydney and Melbourne would have international airports, but that Sydney airport would be bigger.

Alderman Moran came back. He said Senator Henty was evading the issue. It was interesting that all five top members of the Department of Civil Aviation lived in Melbourne. 'I am convinced,' said he, 'that the Federal Government is backing a Victorian conspiracy to favour Tullamarine against Mascot.'

The battle continued all through 1965. It was debated repeatedly in Federal Parliament. On 2 September, Mr Griffiths (Labor, N.S.W.) protested at this 'favouritism' for Victoria. Mr Jess (Liberal, Victoria) accused him in return of parochialism with the line: 'My State's got an extra toffee apple, yours hasn't.'

The continuing story of Tullamarine went on through 1966 and 1967, when Sir Henry Bolte stirred matters. At this time all New South Wales Federal Members of Parliament had been invited to a special conference in Sydney to discuss the Tullamarine threat. When asked to comment, Sir Henry said this New South Wales concern was just 'a big joke'. It was inevitable that Tullamarine must become the major airport in Australia because of its geographical position and the volume of passengers. Victorians had been patient while Sydney was the main gateway to Australia so those in New South Wales were expected to be patient when Tullamarine replaced it.

He said: 'Tullamarine's advantage is that you will be able to fly directly from New York to Melbourne, without first having to waste time calling at Sydney.'

And according to the Melbourne Sun, he added with a smile: 'However, Sydney will always be important.'

The Sydney reaction was instant. Mr A. Bridges, MLA, chairman of the Kingsford Smith Airport Committee, said: 'Tullamarine will become Australia's number one international airport over our dead bodies. We will do everything we can to preserve Sydney as Australia's premier airport.'

And Sir Henry's reply to that: 'They're just like a lot of schoolchildren who have lost their all-day suckers.'

A letter to the Editor of the Herald at this time had a vintage quality:

How sorry I am for those pampered pets of Port Jackson. Sorry not for the position they are in fear of losing but for the petulant attitude they are adopting now that Melbourne is close to taking its rightful place in the international air network. For years now they have had first call on millions of air travellers of all types who have passed Mascot in their droves every day.

Let's face it. Sydney has got away with it for far too long. Victoria, the nation's most progressive State financial capital, has had to stand aside and watch the Kingsford Smith complex grow to being the only major international terminal.

I feel it's now time for these men of Botany Bay to bow gracefully to the winds of change.

T. G. A. Glass, Glenair St.,
Lower Templestowe.

Yet again a nice compromise was turned to suit all tempers. Sydney received its international airport, runways and terminal at Kingsford Smith Airport, Mascot, and Melbourne won its international airport at Tullamarine. The terminals were almost identical. At both one arrived on the high level and left at the low level. Both had the same blush-pink reflecting windows. The big entrance halls, too, were almost the same with great stretches of unrelieved terrazzo going off for ever into the distance. Indeed the airports were so similar that one could have forgiven a returning pilot for forgetting where he was, and after leaving Tullamarine asking a taxi-driver to drive him to Bondi.

Yet in this compromise Sydney feelings were eased by always giving Kingsford Smith airport a slight edge. Sydney opened on 2 May with an official launching by Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth, Tullamarine had to wait until 1 July and it only rated the Prime Minister, Mr Gorton.

Then while the design was the same, everything at Sydney airport was bigger by one-third and one marvelled at the mathematical precision. The Sydney terminal was 770,000 square feet. Melbourne was 500,000. Sydney had parking space for 1,600 cars, Melbourne had parking space for 1,360. Sydney had six baggage carousels, Melbourne four; 36 baggage inspection counters for Sydney, 24 for Melbourne; 20 immigration desks for Sydney, 18 Melbourne. And whereas Sydney had three VIP rooms, Melbourne had two. Nobody actually pointed out the number of toilets, but no doubt Sydney outshone there also.

But there was one last brilliant move by Sir Henry Bolte. Because of jet noise there was a night flying curfew into Sydney between 11 p.m. and 6 a.m. Sir Henry announced before the Melbourne opening that there would be no restrictions on night flying into Tullamarine. Therefore jets unable to get into Sydney would have the convenience of coming into Melbourne.

Just a fortnight before Tullamarine was due to open, the Minister for Civil Aviation, Senator Cotton, announced a night flying ban also on Tullamarine.

Sir Henry was furious. He said: 'One of the most shocking decisions I have ever heard,' and he made it clear it was all a Sydney plot. The ban on Tullamarine was lifted after the opening and the Sydney-Melbourne battle settled down to more routine struggles, like the struggle for trade and migrants.

Always there has been competition between Sydney and Melbourne to attract industry from overseas, to entice the best migrants. Even in 1861 there was a pamphlet available which was little different from those being written today. It was called 'British Migrant's Guide to Victoria' by N. W. Pollard Esquire, of the Victorian Railways Department, and late Superintendent of Public Works at Trinidad.

He starts by saying: 'This magnificent province, the great gold-producing colony of the British Empire,' is prepared to assist migrants with their passage money. 'The migrant,' he wrote, would find a field for his industry with which no other country in the world can compare. Her resources are unbounded, her climate most salubrious and her strides in civilization such as distinguished her among the nations of the earth.'

He makes it clear that to be among Victorians is the very dream of mankind. 'In the British system birth bestows an entrance into the aristocracy. Here in Victoria we acknowledge no separate, no privileged class. Here no man is a political nonentity. In this arrangement we recognize a vast improvement on the British political system . . . if bad men are returned [to the legislature] they can, and very soon will, be ejected.'

On health and charity service he says: 'Victoria shines in brilliant colours, she eclipses every other country in the world.' The indigent and the infirm, orphans and 'those who have strayed from the path of virtue' are all cared for by 'noble charities'. Melbourne itself with a population of 80,000 is described as 'a city of which any nation on earth may be proud'. Then in the last paragraph Britons are invited to migrate to 'the dwelling-place of God's Creatures'.

Sir Henry Bolte, Premier of Victoria since 1955, has never doubted that Victoria is the dwelling-place for God's creatures and at regular intervals he has personally led round-the-world trade missions to persuade capital and industry to come to his State. Premiers in New South Wales have done precisely the same thing. So industrialists in New York, Chicago, London, Paris and Hamburg must have the same feeling when approached by Australia's premiers as the African native when approached at the one time by a rival assortment of Catholic, Anglican, Baptist and Presbyterian missionaries.

For example, on his 1969 tour Sir Henry gave a hearty Press conference in London. He told English reporters that Victoria was a more successful State than New South Wales and when asked whether he believed that fruit machines had broken down the moral fibre of the people of New South Wales, he said: 'I do, and I'm convinced of it. And I've got the facts. Home ownership is greater in Victoria than in New South Wales. Our savings are a hundred dollars a person higher. And this being so—where is all this being dissipated in New South Wales?'

However, whenever Sir Henry Bolte went overseas boosting, barracking for Victoria, inviting, as one writer put it, the good fairies of Wall Street, the gnomes of Zurich and the giants of British industry to stake their money on Victoria, there was always a call to action stations in Sydney. To coincide with Sir Henry's 1969 tour the New South Wales Government inserted a four-page advertisement in London's Economist magazine. Charged with pictures of Sydney surf beaches and Wollongong blast furnaces, the advertisement advised its readers by implication not to be gulled by Sir Henry but to invest in New South Wales.

The advertisement upset Douglas Wilkie in the Melbourne Sun. He said that the NSW 'blurb' was 'shot through with half-truths and baloney'. He printed extracts from the advertisement and put his own comments in brackets underneath.

It is titled 'An Introduction to N.S.W., Australia's Richest State'.

(In fact, wealth per head and savings are bigger in Victoria.)
Sydney is by far the largest city for its age in the world.
(Melbourne is nearly as large, forty years younger, and growing faster.)
Sydney is very much the financial heart of Australia.
(Tell that to BHP and a majority of the big corporations which have their head offices in Melbourne.)
N.S.W. has the cheapest power source in mainland Australia.
(Wot? Never heard a natural gas explosion.)
Eighty-five per cent of the nation's people live within 600 miles of Sydney.
(For what this comic figure is worth, it may be bettered with the claim that 86 per cent of all Australians live within 600 miles of Melbourne.)
The NSW Government has done most to expedite and assist the establishment of industry.
(Possibly, and a good corrective to Sir Henry's own vague counter-boasts. But we'd like to see it worked out on paper.)

Of course, Victoria pays money for its own advertisements. Take this one in London's Daily Mirror. It was headed:

In Victoria what makes you so attractive?

Currently, industry in Victoria, Australia, is developing at a fantastic rate. The only problem is that we need more people. People with Trade, technical or professional skills. In January, 1971, we are promoting over a migrant-resettling mission to the United Kingdom. What you would be worth knowing . . .

VICTORIA THE GREAT
Queen of Australia's States

The battle is really no different even in the 1970s: Victoria is unable to come to terms with New South Wales over the price of natural gas, with the result that New South Wales is prepared to build a pipeline more than double the length to get its gas from northern South Australia, and the language between the States has the same old quality that it had in the days of Separation, railway-building and Federation.

Unquestionably it is time for a burying of hatchets between Sydney and Melbourne. A picturesque ceremony with Town Clerks burying gold-plated hatchets in Martin Place or Collins Street would not be enough. The two States would need to draw up a proper Treaty of Versailles. Perhaps the discussions could take place between the two premiers in a railway carriage, very carefully placed between Wodonga and Albury, half on one side of the border half on the other. For meals they could eat courses cooked alternately in each State.

A good start would be the exchange of cultural and diplomatic representatives. The only contacts at present between the two cities are the rival tourist bureaux. Victoria could appoint a High Commissioner to Sydney and Sydney could appoint a High Commissioner to Melbourne, both having the task of spreading humility and good will.

No one can deny that this idea would be useful, and besides, Premiers are always looking for jobs for unwanted senior Cabinet Ministers.

A history of the Melbourne-Sydney rivalry from the 1849 separation protests through the railway gauge dispute, the Federation capital battle, the Cook's cottage war, and the long Tullamarine-Mascot airport feud, arguing the two cities in 1972 knock each other with the same poisonous energy they always have.
