God Save Fish'n'Chips

5 min read.

First published 1991

God Save Fish'n'Chips

Yes, I have waited patiently for nearly a week, expecting the thundering editorials, the curious hysterical outcry. There have been few letters to the editor, no public outcry. You will remember last Wednesday the Agricultural Outlook Conference in Canberra was told that the fish and chip shops were dying. They were being killed off by the fast fooderies.

Come the year 2000 fish and chip shops would be just a memory.

Do we really want this to happen? The greatest things we should be grateful to the English are: our legal system, the curious hysterical manner in which we conduct democracy, the game of cricket, and, not the least, fish and chips.

For more than a century fish and chips wrapped in newspaper has been a great example of British culture. Back in the 1950s when fast foods first began to sweep the United States some enterprising Poms opened English fish and chip shops in New York.

They advertised that these were genuine English fish and chips wrapped in English newspapers. It was a very serious enterprise and they imported copies of London's Daily Mirror to make it possible. For a time it was a huge success.

That has been the problem here. Nightly we are subjected to advertisements from McDonalds, Pizza Hut, Colonel Sanders and Rooster, but what promotion do you see for our beloved fish and chips? None.

For my money Melbourne fish and chips always were the best in world because - with a bit of luck - the fish was flake, surely one of the great under-rated eating fish. We used to think flake, purchased fresh on the pier at places like Lorne, then cooked in batter, was one of the glories of the summer.

I don't know which public relations genius back around the turn of the century first thought that it would be nicer, more tactful to housewifely sensibilities, if we called shark 'flake' but at the time it seemed to work.

Melbourne became the shark eating capital of the world. In Sydney sharks tended to eat people. While in Melbourne people tended to eat shark.

But flake, to my mind, had a splendid firm texture, it came in good sized pieces, it had almost no bones, and it wasn't one of your anaemic tasteless fishes.

The time has come to give both flake and fish and chips some real promotion.

Let's advertise 'Shark and Chips,' 'The Shark You Eat Today was Wiggling Yesterday in Port Phillip Bay'. I think I would prefer they ate it out of copies of The Times or even Mr Murdoch's Sun which always has an over-developed nude on page three, but if they wanted some panache they could import some panache from London they could import copies of The Times.

At least our fish and chips wrapped in newspaper is educational, not like Mr McDonald's fishburger, which is merely an unnamed, anonymous fish wrapped in a roll so soft it can be eaten without teeth.

American fast foods are swamping everything. I would suggest that by the year 2000 other deeply indigenous Melburnian items will be in deep peril. It is well known that Melbourne is the meat pie capital of the world. We eat more pies than anyone else, even though Four'n'Twenty is trying to spread the message to California, Japan and even England.

This is the centre, we are greater pie lovers than Sydney, Brisbane or Adelaide. But don't under-estimate the danger of the pizza and the chicken invasion. The pie scene is not what it was and a whole generation is now with us that has lost that great art of how to eat a hot pie in a paper bag without spilling the sauce down your shirt.

Other fast foods deeply indigenous to Melbourne are also among the endangered species and should be given a grading by the National Trust. We were the crumpet capital. The crumpet had the superb high cholesterol skill of being able to absorb at least three times its weight in melted butter. Places like the long-departed Glaciarium used to serve hot crumpets, supercharged with melted butter then laden with honey.

Such items 'ready to go' are now rare indeed.

The dim sim, too, we should recognise and put on the endangered list. Always there has been the suspicion that the dim sim never came from Canton or Shanghai, it was invented right here by the Melburnian Chinese.

For me it remains one of the most glorious dishes ever given to mankind. If the situation at our house is too desperate to do any cooking it is pure bliss to slip out for a dozen dim sims, all pale and crinkly. Begging your pardon, it looks as if it has been cooked in a condom. This must be protected from the Sanders and the McDonalds.

You must appreciate there are varieties of dim sims. In Sydney they tend to prefer them fried. Here in Melbourne we go sensibly for the classic, more succulent boiled dim sim, all pale and crinkly. Begging your pardon, it looks as if it has been cooked in a condom.

Other indigenous items born of Yarra-town are Vegemite, the Violet Crumble and the Cherry Ripe. Vegemite has survived and blossomed as a cult spread. It has become a mystic Oz thing essential to our survival, like betel juice is important to the highland tribes of Papua.

The Violet Crumble and the Cherry Ripe are under threat from the American Mars invasion and should be watched. I noticed all through the matriculation and university exam agony period there were advertisements on television announcing that the Mars bar was an essential item for sustenance and survival.

I would have thought that the indigenous Ocker Violet Crumble or Cherry Ripe was preferable. But in the mean time let us first save our shark and chips. Remember how the ice cream shops always sported an illuminated ice cream outside. I would suggest that every fish and chip shop have an illuminated shark. Put a little bite in your day.
