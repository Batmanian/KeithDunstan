Why Did Christ Do Everything in One Year?

3 min read.

First published 1991

A closing reflection on Christmas as a punishing annual ritual that would suit everyone better held once every 30 years, working through the diplomacy of whose in-laws host dinner, the doomed New Year's resolution to shop early next time, and a final wish that the reader's new toy trains prove more reliable than Melbourne's public transport.

As a child I was always puzzled by the Christian calendar. How did they whiz through Christ's life in no time at all.

Here he was born on Christmas Day, next minute you were suffering through Lent and he was dead by April. How long did he live? I didn't know, but it seemed to me he was at least 30.

So it should be 30 years before he was born again. Christmas, we find, is very hard on the nerves, unbelievably hard on one's Bankcard, Mastercard, cheque book. It would be lovely if we took it gently, and indeed it would be a marvellous climax if we did have it every 30 years.

And apart from being the happiest time it is always the saddest. The let down can come, say, at four or five in the afternoon. Memories flood back of those wonderful people with whom you once shared Christmas.

Then the older you get the more complex it becomes bringing everyone together. When your children have grown up and are married there is the subtle diplomacy, do the children come to you or do they go to the in-laws.

Mother cooks the dinner just the same and you know what invariably happens; lucky children get two Christmas dinners.

The parents prayer, that the kids won't receive too many build-it kits too complicated for your tired brain to handle; that they won't receive too many shrieking video games; that the toys will last three days without breaking down and there won't be too many gadgets that require batteries.

The hardest thing? Fitting the toys back into the boxes they arrived in.

And another hard thing. Remembering which present came from which aunty, which grandmother, and getting them to say thank-you.

Some time, perhaps around 8 p.m. tomorrow, you will make two noble resolutions. (a) You will carefully fold up all the Christmas wrapping and save it for next year. (b) You will avoid all the last minute shopping for Christmas 1991. Instead of paying last minute absurd prices you will buy your 1991 presents at the January sales. (c) You will be absolutely brilliant with your Christmas cards. Instead of going broke with airmail prices you will send out your overseas cards in mid-October.

You won't, of course. In 1991 you will be in exactly the same muddle as this year.

If there are any electric trains or rail vehicles in the Christmas stockings, fondle them kindly. They are sure to be infinitely more reliable than the Melbourne public transport system.

You think you are having a hard time. The worst job would be working in a department store later this week taking back all those presents people didn't want on Christmas Day.

Have a Happy time.
