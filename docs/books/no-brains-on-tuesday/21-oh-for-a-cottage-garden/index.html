Oh for a Cottage Garden!

3 min read.

First published 1991

A survey of Australian garden fashions from all-native nationalism after Alan Bond won the America's Cup to the current English cottage garden craze, guided by nursery man Paul Hooper and featuring a $40,000 gazebo with Michelangelo's David lurking behind the delphiniums.

About how we are finding life very difficult; no longer are we keeping up with the trends.

Five years ago, when we returned from California, the new all-Australian garden was very much the vogue. Nationalism knew no bounds after Bondy won the America's Cup. Environmentalism had promoted the all-Australian garden. It had become essential to plant Australian.

We had to go into melaleucas, grevilleas, callistemons, banksias and all those dreaded grasses which dropped everywhere. None of your English trees which dropped sap you could not get off the steps.

The pressure was on. Our nursery man, Paul Hooper, the loveable bloke who had helped us with the garden for years, boldly changed. He suggested Australian shrubs.

We first noticed the change when the folk in our street started to go for picket fences. You're very odd now if you haven't got a gorgeous old English fence with picket fence gates.

We noticed the nurseries were changing, getting bigger and bigger. The Croydon nurseries, the ones that used to be English only, had changed. There was an astonishing boom in annuals and English-type perennials.

Absolutely everybody is going for cosmos. Very, very lovely.

'And lavender. I have never known it come back so well. I seem to have eight to ten varieties. French, English, lavender — the biggest one is Hidcote, dwarf, deep purple, lovely.'

'Oh no,' he replied, 'they have a delicate beauty all of their own. You must get Buff Beauty, Sparrieshoop or Ballerina. They only bloom once, not twice in the season like modern roses, but you do get wonderful rose hips in the autumn.'

Then he waved his hand at the neatly trimmed hedge in the cottage garden. You must have your hedges, English box, or Dutch box, at a height of about 46 cm.

He inquired about a gazebo, a place where we could sit in the garden by the herb garden and take in the scent of the evening. It looked very charming, like a grandstand. Ye Gods, the price was $40,000 and little sundials at $1280.

Our nursery man explained, too, that there was a boom in garden statuary, nothing over-large, discreet, you understand. Or how about Michelangelo's David? Just put him in a spot where the delphiniums would hide anything that was untoward.

Little fountains with several cupids on top were good and there was an exquisite cast iron wall fountain, the sort of thing you might find in a charming little café.

I used to dream it would be good to get out of journalism and become the curator of a splendid estate. I bet by the time we left the nursery there would be an entirely new trend. They would be into cactus and desert succulents.
