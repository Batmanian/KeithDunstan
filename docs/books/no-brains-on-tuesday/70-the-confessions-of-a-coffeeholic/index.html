The Confessions of a Coffeeholic

5 min read.

First published 1991

A defence of coffee addiction runs through a Harvard study that found no link between coffee and heart disease, a history of the drink being banned as satanic or driving men impotent, and famous devotees from Voltaire's 50 cups a day to Fontanelle, who blamed his own 100-year lifespan on the stuff.

Isn't it time some of us thought of taking legal action against the medical profession?

I mean look at these experts; for countless decades they have been describing coffee as a poison, a lethal brew that will cause heart attacks and strokes.

Now the Harvard School of Public Health has put 45,589 men through long extensive tests and found not the slightest link between coffee and heart disease.

Oh wait a minute, there was just one problem. They found a slightly higher incidence of heart attacks and strokes among people who drank four or more cups of decaffeinated coffee a day.

I am, let's face it, a coffeeholic. The coffee bean is very nearly the most beautiful thing God created. It is the divine elixir that lubricates the tongue, inspires the mind, sends the olfactory senses into a state of bliss and gives the excuse to dally and commune with friends.

The smell of good coffee, just ground, is the perfect aroma, the aphrodisiac which can draw you in from the street. I have often wondered why women use those absurd and expensive scents like Chanel which they buy from France. It would be far more enticing if they placed just a pinch of fresh coffee behind the ear.

These remarks do not apply to instant coffee and regrettably 80 per cent of all the coffee consumed in Australia is instant. It has no aroma at all and tastes like it.

But let's get back to the real subject; constantly the doctors have warned against coffee. Sanctimoniously, health shops have refused to sell it. Instead they have sold those vile, probably extremely damaging, herbal teas.

As I have gone through life drinking my fourth, fifth, sixth cup of black coffee for the day, receiving warnings from loved ones, there has been constant stress. In restaurants there has been the lovely idea, the bottomless cup. Many a lovely waitress has returned again and again: 'Can I fill your cup Sir?' Thank-you very much. But there has been this worry, this constant fear of impending death.

Surely that is worth damages before any wise, respectable court.

For more than five centuries coffee has suffered from bad P.R. In 1511 Kair Bey, the Governor of Mecca, banned all coffee drinking. One night when he was in the mosque for evening prayer, he spotted a group preparing for an all-night devotion. It was too much for the Governor. They were drinking coffee, this sort of thing might lead men and women to all sorts of dangerous behaviour.

There were constant attempts throughout Egypt, Turkey, Yemen and Arabian cities to ban this black liquor which gave such happiness. In 1656 the Ottoman Grand Vizir Koprili prohibited coffee because it caused the citizens to meet too much and talk too much. For a first offence a coffee drinker received a terrible beating. If he or she persisted in drinking coffee they were sewn into a leather bag and thrown into the Bosphorus.

There was a similar problem in England when coffee came under attack from the Puritans. Coffee houses were patronised only by men. In 1674 the women of London took up a petition against coffee.

It said, not only did coffee render men impotent it caused them 'to trifle away their time, scald their chops, and spend money all for a little base, black, thick, nasty, bitter, stinking, nauseous puddle water.'

The women could have been right. I have always had a suspicion about English coffee. At the end of the 16th century coffee was banned in Rome. The priests would not touch it. They thought this black drink an invention of the devil. The Muslims forbad the use of wine (sanctified by Christ) and imbibed this insidious coffee as a substitute.

A cup of coffee was presented to Pope Clement VIII, who thought it necessary to taste this villainy so that he could make a papal decree. But upon tasting it, he was stunned. He said: 'This is delicious. Why should we leave this drink to Satan? Christian power is greater than that of the devil. We will take it for our own.' Coffee has been drunk in Italy with remarkable enthusiasm ever since. But the idea of it being a poison has always persisted.

Sir James Mackintosh, a Scottish philosopher, asserted in 1800 that the powers of a man's mind were generally proportional to the amount of coffee he drank. Voltaire drank 50 cups a day, and Balzac could hardly write a line without his coffee. He used to go to bed at 6 pm, sleep until midnight, then write non-stop for 12 hours drinking coffee all the time. The ultimate remark about the dangers of coffee is attributed both to Voltaire and another philosopher, Fontanelle. When asked whether coffee was a poison Voltaire/Fontanelle said: 'I think it must be. I am 85 years old and not dead yet.' Perhaps coffee wasn't very good for Voltaire, he passed on at 84. Fontanelle lived on to be a 100.

But really, you should know about some of the experiments on coffee. In 1981 there was the remarkable case of Dr Thomas F.X. Collins, a Government scientist in the U.S. He started feeding caffeine to 300 pregnant rats. He gave his rats the equivalent of 12 to 24 strong cups of coffee a day. The result: a high proportion of his mother rats gave birth to baby rats with deformed toes and 'some delay in skeletal development.'

But were rats ever meant to drink coffee? Give them concentrated carrot juice, yoghurt and muesli the result could have been just the same.

However, now that we have received such wonderful news from the blessed scientists at Harvard, I think we should celebrate with Alexander Pope's ode to coffee:

As long as Mocha's happy tree shall grow, While berries crackle or while mills shall go, While smoking streams from silver spouts shall glide, Or China's earth receive the sable tide, While coffee shall to British nymphs be dear, While fragant streams the bended head shall cheer, Or grateful bitters shall delight the taste, So long her honors, name and praise shall last.
