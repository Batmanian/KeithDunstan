How to Murder Your Computer

5 min read.

First published 1991

A dark fantasy about shooting a personal computer at Bulletstop, an actual American firing range where frustrated owners can empty machine gun rounds into old electronics, brought on by a catastrophic lost-data 'data fault', unreadable manuals and the fact that a nine-year-old PC is already onto its fourth obsolete generation.

Oh yes, I am an absolute, almost violent, member of the anti-gun lobby.

But I am wondering whether I should change my mind. I have just read about a character named Paul La Vista who lives in Marietta, a suburb of Atlanta, Georgia. I have just read about him in an issue of New Scientist.

Paul runs an indoor firing range called Bulletstop. He hires out at all kinds of horrid firearms, ammunition, even machine guns. The idea is that you can indulge in your own violent urges in the harmless atmosphere of a target range. Now here's the point, particular favourites at Bulletstop are personal computers and computer manuals.

When I read this a tear ran down my cheek. I understood perfectly. I have had a love/hate relationship with personal computers that has gone on now for nine years. It all began with a Teleram P.C. called a Teleram, which weighed 20 kilograms and was laughably described as 'portable'.

When you turned it on a message came on the screen: 'Hi, this is your Teleram P.C.' Then when you turned it off it announced chirpily: 'So long, that's all folks.' It had one appalling characteristic, it was sensitive to static electricity. I would have written a 1000 words to keys, the screen would suddenly a spark would come from fingers to keys, the screen would go blank and everything I had written irretrievably disappeared.

If only Paul La Vista had been available then, Rambo would not have known such fury. I would have blasted that Teleram with a bazooka. Finally I discovered the only safe way one could operate this PC was to work in bare feet.

The manufacturers of computers know damn well they are peculiar, fickle, untrustworthy, strange. The absolute law of computers, is BACK-UP, BACK-UP, BACK-UP. In other words never do more than half an hour's work without copying everything on to disc.

I had a serene six months and became dangerously over confident. I overloaded a disc and there was one terrible morning, I punched the keys and all that came back on the screen was 'Data fault'. The same thing frequently happens with my own memory, whenever I try to remember a dear friend's name, press the mental buttons and back comes 'data fault'.

But on this disc were 17,000 words of a book and there was no back-up. I didn't become violent and attack the computer, I think I began to cry. I went to the local agents, all the top computer doctors, surgeons, experts on disc cardiac failure. The disc was sent to computer psychiatrists in Sydney, but the data had all gone, gone to some mysterious PC black hole where all computer data disappears.

It was one of the agonies of my life and quietly I had to start all over again. It is not generally realised that computers are sulky and have moods. I live in the country and daily send data by modem through telephone wires, and my computer talks to the big brother computer in the newspaper office. But some days either one computer or the other is in a bad mood and they play games of no speak. Not even the greatest experts can explain why.

Paul La Vista reports that one group brought into Bulletstop an enormous computer manual, 2000 pages. They rented three machine guns, an Uzi, an M3 and a Thompson. They proceeded to cut it to pieces with bullets until it was like confetti. This done they went away with happy smiles, rid of a terrible frustration.

If you have had anything to do with computer manuals your sympathy for this act would be overwhelming. They are written by boffin mathematicians as a puzzle, designed deliberately to entire shelf loads will be used to cover a simple act which could be explained in a paragraph.

There is an entire industry now, in trying to explain what computer manuals mean. For example, I use soft wear books called Microsoft Word; there are at least four available which try to explain what the official manual is talking about, Microsoft Word Made Easy. You can never get them under $50 each. I bought one item of ingenious software, the manual so indecipherable, I used to ring the manufacturer in Pennsylvania, asking him what the hell I had to do next. He was utterly charming and we became such good friends, but the expense was awful.

Yet I haven't mentioned the most maddening thing of all. You know how Detroit invented built-in obsolescence for the automobile? It is as nothing compared with computers. You don't get new models, you get new generations, for God's sake.

Already, in nine years, I am on my fourth generation. They keep coming out with brilliant new advances, hard discs, mini discs, extended memory where facilities where you can index a book practically three Bibles on the one disc, lightning speed, alphanumeric skills, automatic dictionaries, thesauruses, even in an afternoon, genius inspired devices that will brush up your grammar.

Unfortunately I found every new gadget or device irresistible. So now I have a room full of aged old vintage computers. I would like to shoot them, gun them down while they whimper, but I can't do that either, because they don't even have the appeal of old gramophone records, which people still pick up.
