The Romance of the Home Bottler

7 min read.

First published 1991

A nostalgic account of the 1960s home wine bottling era, from Brown Brothers kegs railed to the local station and a lavatory-cistern bottling rig to a 20-gallon bottle exploding in a Carlton street, cork weevils eating through the woodshed, and the current revival driven by inflated wine prices.

You are looking at a crazy character who one time possessed over 5000 home bottlings.

Not peaches, plums, or skinned tomatoes, but top quality riesling, cabernet, shiraz and merlot, all splendidly bottled corked and labelled.

It was the wonderful dream time era of home bottling and I can date for you, almost precisely, the time that it all came to an end, 1969-70.

The reasons? Australians suddenly discovered wine as an alternate to beer. The wine producers, who had suffered so long, did not need to sell in bulk any more, they put all the good stuff in bottles themselves. And, besides, along came the cask, the odious plastic bag.

One is telling you all this, because an extraordinary thing has happened, home bottling is booming again. Oh, the reasons are obvious. One, Mr Keating has discovered that wine, like whisky, gin, beer, and rum, is a glorious device for taxing human beings. Two, consumers in places as far apart as London, Hong Kong, Tokyo and New York, have found that Australian wine is good to drink. So the prices have gone berserk.

But there are other and more subtle reasons. Back in the dream time we were referring to earlier, you could name the available vineyards on a couple of hands. Now they run into hundreds, no thousands. For example, we live on Victoria's Mornington Peninsular, where 20 years ago we had nothing but chooks and apples. Now I can count at least 90 vineyards.

Wine is now being produced in quantities and varieties we never saw before and there is excellent stuff available for the home bottler. But for a moment, just like the best movies, do you mind if we indulge in some flashbacks?

I began home bottling back in 1960. We used to get our wine from Brown Bros at Milawa, from Eric Purbrick at Chateau Tahbilk and A.P. Birks in South Australia. We used to have an old 18-gallon beer keg. We would send up the barrel to John Brown or Mr Purbrick and he would send it back full, to our local railway station. Heaven knows how many trips that barrel made.

I ask you to believe that we bottled superb wines for a shilling or around 10 cents a bottle, plus two cents for the cork. They were very good indeed and I can prove it because I still have some of those treasures from the early sixties. The most blissful occasions were the bottling parties. Our leading bottler, was Nick, who dealt in the big time, hogsheads. A hogshead would hold around 315 bottles.

All right, Nick had a superb system. He would put his hogshead on the concrete roof of his garage. A large plastic tube ran from the barrel into a refined, begging your pardon, lavatory cistern, controlled by a ball valve.

From the lavatory cistern ran two more tubes complete with taps, so we bottled two bottles at a time, and as we bottled there would be a back up team with brass corkers, corking like crazy. Round about 11 p.m. if you were in Nick's street, you could witness team members pushing home loads of wine in wheelbarrows, a very romantic sight.

Some of us were snobs. We would go to the city's leading restaurateurs like Herman Schneider and Leon Massoni and collect all their empties. Always there would be a vast pile on Sunday mornings. How nice it was to put our home bottled wine into a Lafite, Margaux, Haut Brion or maybe a late departed Mouton-Rothschild.

Nick was a reverse snob, despised that sort of thing. He had an 'in' where he could get brand new turpentine bottles. If you are old enough, you will remember, they used to come in a triangular shape. One time, with incredible courage, Nick took one of his home bottlings into Melbourne's august Florentino. The shocked head waiter took away the turpentine bottle for uncorking, holding it with two fingers, as if it were a dead rat.

I won't say we didn't have disasters. I used to own one of those bottles like old time pharmacists display in their windows. It held about 20 gallons. One sunny February I took it to Milawa and John Brown filled it with his best cabernet-shiraz-mondeuse, an utterly gorgeous blend.

We were almost home, driving through Carlton, and I heard a terrible crack in the back seat; expanding gases with the heat had blown my beautiful bottle. Can you imagine 20 gallons of red wine let loose in an FJ Holden? All I could do was pull up at the kerb, open the back door and it flowed like a waterfall into the gutter.

An Australian would have laughed. But an Italian was standing there and he was almost in tears: 'Mama Mia, What a tragedy.' Oh, it was he who understood our grief at that moment. Losing the wine was bad enough, but it soaked everything and I don't want to go into all the details, but the car for the next five years had the odour of an old keg.

Storage was the problem in those days. My theory was this; a gentleman should purchase twice as much wine as he could drink, so that always there would be bottles nicely maturing for his old age. So, Heaven forgive us, we had wardrobes, put them in the garage and filled them with old wine. We went without wood so that we could fill the wood shed. The wood shed was not such a good idea. That little weevil, the cork borer, got into the corks. One day I discovered seeping wine coming out of the cartons. The beetles were drinking more than I was.

I went to that great wine expert, Dan Murphy: 'Dan, what do I do now?' 'Simple,' said Dan. 'You recork the lot.' So one incredible week-end we had to recork 5000 bottles with even the children in the production line. That's another of my sins, the children were expert at bottling and labelling.

One member of our group made the supreme sacrifice. He covered his floor only with mats and rugs, and kept the floor boards loose. His entire house was literally floating on wine. Going to his place for dinner was an amazing experience. He could never remember where he put his prize bottles, so he would be pulling up boards in the living room, in the study...

So fellow wine lovers, all this is returning, except the price is not the same. Golly, these days you pay 20 cents even for a cork, and 10 cents would buy about enough wine to put in your ear hole. In the 1960s wine was the one wonderful liquid that was not taxed. Now home bottling a quality wine costs around $3 a bottle.

But the difference is they do it better now and with better equipment. There are firms which cater entirely for the home bottler. You will find them in all the capital cities. The biggest customers are schools, churches and youth groups, who bottle for charity. It is all done, quite legally, taxes paid etc., and they bottle under supervision.

The groups vary, maybe from a very nice Catholic church school to rowing clubs, to parish churches, to cricket clubs, to young Communists and even policemen. But why has it to young Communists? There are still serious wine drinkers, who want wine at a reasonable price and they don't want cask wine.

Now, with so many vineyards producing wine, you are constantly getting situations where vignerons have small lots which are not big enough for a commercial bottle run, ideal to be sold in bulk for the home bottler. You get very distinguished wine makers who sell their wine, at say, $25, but as always happens they have some wine, which is not quite the style that suits their label, good wine nevertheless, so they sell it anonymously, wonderful for the home bottler. Then there are vignerons who have production costs, need money quickly, so they sell in bulk. Great for the home bottler.

I was delighted to note that the domestic home bottler is thriving as never before. The home bottling parties are cropping up again in Balwyn, Chadstone, Vaucluse, Paddo and all over.

The wine jug comes in 27.5 litre plastic casks, designed specifically for the home bottler. They hold precisely three dozen bottles.

Which is a pity. When I was home bottling, I always liked three quarters of a bottle left over. I mean, even if it is eleven in the morning a home bottler has to check it out.
