A Prostate Could Make You AA

5 min read.

First published 1991

The indignities of the modern credit-rating system, from Moody's down-grading the State Bank and a credit card application requiring a month-long inquisition, to an automatic teller swallowing a card and a South Yarra liquor store refusing a cheque, culminating in the observation that Bob Hawke's prostate operation will surely restore his public approval rating to Aaa.

You can feel for the poor, unfortunate State Bank and the once illustrious State Electricity Commission.

It seems that they are about to be down-graded by Moody's. Moody's is that snooty, superior, international credit agency which decides what common creatures are worth.

The State Bank and the SEC, at least until last Friday, were Aaa. Where will they head now? Australia already has slipped down the scale to Aa2 and is poised to drop to who knows what depths.

Mr Keating was livid and refused to have any more truck with Moody officials. I can understand his chagrin. This credit monster has become a complete part of modern living. The Big Brother is eyeing us up and down at every turn.

Four weeks ago I applied for an international credit card. They sent me a loving letter in the mail. If only I joined it would be subscription free for the first year and they would send six bottles of wine with their compliments.

Nothing like that happened. Instead they sent an inquisition and a big bill. They wanted to know my employer's name, exactly how much I earned. They needed a reference from my accountant, my bank account number and the whole process has taken a month. I suspect now they are checking my church attendance record with the Vicar.

I bet they have also checked with Moody's. Down to AA4.

Then last week I went to get the car filled. I thought it would be nice to use the electronic card. They play weird tricks these cards. I swear I got my number right and I knew there was plenty of money in the bank. A little message came up on the screen: TRANSACTION DECLINED.

The girl at the service station gave me a long slow look meaning: 'Ahhh, we know your kind.' I paid cash, but I bet Moody's knew about it at once. Bb3.

Then I went to a suburban bank, Saturday morning, long queue at the automatic teller. There are so many crazy numbers you have to remember now, telephone number at home and office, Bass number for buying theatre tickets, passport number, security number in case the alarm goes off and you have to get a clearance with the security agency. Then there's the darned number you have to remember at the credit union.

O.K., there was a memory lapse this awful Saturday morning. There were three attempts with the result. Alas, this nasty machine swallowed my card and refused to give it back. I can't tell you the humiliation. The rest of the queue give you long looks even more meaningful than the girl at the service station. 'Uh-uh, another one trying to get a free bob.'

Moody's would never forgive that one. Cf7.

There are all sorts of ways of putting you in your place. You go into your favourite store to buy a new pullover for the winter. You have been a regular client for only 45 years, but it takes a while to break through.

'You would like to put that on the account, Sir.'

'Yes please.'

'Your address? Full name.'

Then there is the pause of about five minutes while she rings through to the accountant, Moody's, Bank of Tokyo, or wherever. Five minutes elapse before she gets the O.K. But you feel you have sunk again. Dd8.

We are living in difficult times. Nobody is trusted any more. Every creature is looked upon as a serious financial risk. If late with your payments there was a time when public organisations sent a polite note: Sir/Madam, we are sure you have overlooked your account with us. Now it is blunt: 'If we do not receive your payment within 14 days we will be forced to take legal action...'

We made an error with our rates this year. We misread the awful complication of numbers and fine print on the notice. We underpaid by a small amount. There was no kindly pat on the shoulder from the Shire treasurer, just a blunt demand and a surcharge of 20 per cent interest. I'm sure Moody's understand perfectly. Or I4.

And cheques? There was a time when there was never a bother paying by cheque. Now all cheques are suspicious objects, less loved even than the credit card. I went into a South Yarra liquor store to get a little lubricant which I hoped would smooth the evening.

When I offered the cheque, the refusal was unequivocal. I offered the usual driving licence. 'No, Sir.'

'You can check with my bank. It is immediately over the road.' 'No, Sir.'

So away I went, no purchase. What would Moody's say now? 2x2.

Everything we do is assessed, graded, catalogued. I am particularly interested by the approval ratings put out weekly through the Bulletin and almost all other journals. Why do these public judgements fluctuate so wildly? Hawke, Hewson, Cain, Peacock, Greiner, they go up and down week by week.

Take Bob Hawke and his prostate. For a very long time I have been an observer of what we can slip in to the paper and what we cannot. I can remember back to the forties when expletives like God and hell were not allowed and such items as brothel and rape were absolutely too horrid for the public print.

But look what we have done to Bob. R.G. Menzies had some appalling things done to his plumbing while he was in office, but no mention of it got into print. The Prime Minister was merely poorly or taking a rest.

Now all is exposed. Last week we had full sketches of a penis and what goes on with a prime ministerial prostate operation. I feel that now we have arrived and as far as public frankness is concerned there is nothing more to be achieved.

But what I want to know is this. What will it do for Bob's approval rating? I suspect from here on he will be Aaa.
