Spare Us the Arsehole

3 min read.

First published 1991

Derryn Hinch's on-air farewell shot at 3AW prompts a history of forbidden language in Australian newspapers, from the 1940s taboo on bodily functions through the gradual admission of 'damn' in 1962, 'bloody' in 1968, and the momentous newspaper debut of the word that finally gives this chapter its title.

Sexy and Vulgar Columns

You may have noticed there has been a well-publicised war amongst Melbourne's radio personalities.

Interesting that you don't have people in radio, you have personalities.

There is nothing unusual in a personalities row, is there? They do it from time to time when their ratings are ailing. It is a means of generating publicity. Politicians are always doing it. Prize fighters have always done it.

I am fascinated by a bout between Derryn Hinch and John Laws of 3AW. When Mr Hinch departed from 3AW he said on air: 'Personally, I should say 3AW hasn't lost its soul and it's just lost its arsehole.'

Now arsehole is a most uncommon expression in newspaper reporting, or it was. Melbourne's Herald, displaying the finest news instinct, gave it front-page treatment.

Oh dear. In the days of my training a newspaper editor who used to work here, a devout reader who worried about offending the Methodists, Presbyterians and Baptists out there, used to say: 'The places of vulgarity you let slip into the newspaper create enemies every week.'

In those days, bodily functions and sex were on the black list. They simply didn't exist at all in the nineteen forties and early fifties. It was almost impossible to describe any part of the human anatomy. Arses were non-existent. No 'arse'. The word used was 'sit-upon'.

Females were too ladylike to have anything at all. Genitalia, certainly not. The nearest you could get was 'relations', or 'a certain act'. If you were raped you were indecently assaulted.

An abortion was an illegal operation. Women didn't even get pregnant, they were 'in a certain condition'. And as for brothels — they were in Little Lonsdale; I still can't quite bring myself to call them anything but a house of ill fame.

And that's another thing. You couldn't have 'My God' in the paper. Putting 'Gee' into the mouth of an interviewee was 'just a cheap way of getting blasphemy'.

That's the way it was right into the nineteen sixties. Then bloody — what a marvellous word — made it in 1962. Damn made it in 1968. We had an abortion in the paper in 1968.

In 1979 there was a court case that shocked the world: the word actually made it into the Age. This was a watershed decision.

Oh dear. In 1980 we acquired bugger and that other word. There had been nothing to which we could look and refer. The inflation of profanity, however, is not steep. Say something really disastrous happens — say the television gives up — I can understand all that. I think about life on a more elegant serene plane. Alas, I cannot.

Mr Blackman, no doubt, is deeply grateful to Mr Hinch. He seems to have given us a whole new range of outrageous words. The most disgusting words we can find in our language. There are words in Hungarian that would dirvel your hair. Bless us all.

'There's no one quite as famous as...' — arsehole. As famous as Blankers.
