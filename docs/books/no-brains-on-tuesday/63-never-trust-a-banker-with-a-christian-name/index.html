Never Trust a Banker With a Christian Name

5 min read.

First published 1991

The vanished gravitas of banking, from stern manager-arranged 'positions' for a mother's favourite son and hard-collared tellers in Corinthian-columned monoliths, to glass-fronted branches staffed by first-name tellers called Cheryl or Jason and pushing Easy Money loans, with the State Bank of South Australia's expense-account glory days as Exhibit A.

There is a bush song written by that great poet Anonymous which goes:

For the banks are all broken they say, And the merchants are all up a tree; When the bigwigs of banking go bung What chance for a squatter like me?

Actually the most disturbing thing is not the losing of money but what has happened to the fine old institution of banking. When I was leaving school it was the object of every mother to get her son into a bank.

If you knew someone high up in a bank, what bliss it was. 'Could you please find a position for our Henry?' Yes, you got a position. Never crudely was it called a job.

Mother knew that once Henry was in the bank he would be trained according to the highest moral ideals. His manager would wear a three-piece suit even on days of 40 degrees. His care with money, his meanness, would be correctly short back and sides. His manager would be the perfect example for young Henry.

When any customer went to the bank for a loan, the money was not just handed out. Customer indeed! He or she was a penitent, one who had to confess. The customer had to grovel, to plead and, before signatures were placed on paper, the bank manager gave a lecture on the importance of money borrowed.

T.S. Eliot was an example of the majesty of banking. He started his career very correctly as a bank clerk. When he told his manager he was leaving there was shock all around the building. The manager asked what he proposed to do. Eliot said he intended to make a living as a poet. The shock was even greater. 'Pity,' said the important man, 'I saw in you possibilities as bank manager material.'

Look what we have learned in the past week. We were told at one inquiry that the former management of Victoria's State Bank was like an 'old boys club', even a way of life. They had their own bathrooms and toilets, originals on the walls, chauffeurs for their cars and more than anything, they looked forward to their overseas trips.

The same thing was true of South Australia's State Bank. We were told over there that the banks indulged in long lunches, wonderful trips, good living and, what impressed me more than anything, Max Schubert was wine advisor to the Board. Max Schubert, as you know, created the marvellous Grange Hermitage for Penfolds.

Then Tim Marcus Clark, managing director of the South Australian State Bank, retired with just on a million dollars severance pay after receiving an annual salary of $500,000.

Does that startle you? No, no, no, not the money, the reference to 'Tim'. When I was leaving school, managers and young Henry going into the bank didn't even possess Christian names. Mister Clark it would have been and that would have been the end of it.

This familiarity goes on with bankers everywhere. Who was appointed as the new chairman of the suffering State Bank of South Australia? Nobby Clark. Nobby Clark was the former chief executive of the National Australia Bank. I have noticed for a year or so that whenever this particular Clark turned up in the financial pages he was always 'Nobby'. National.

It is easy to trace the deterioration in banking. In the old days banks were always built like monuments or war memorials, lots of immense solid masonry with Corinthian or Doric columns. The windows were small to emphasise their impenetrable security. You spoke in hushed tones and crossed yourself when you went in. The bankers themselves wore hard collars, sombre ties and looked like funeral directors.

The idea was to awe the customer into submission and show that money did not come too easily.

Now there is no masonry at all. The buildings are like super markets, all glass, your corner Unsafeway Store. The top man is called Nobby or Tim and there are Christian names for everybody.

At the teller's window you note that you are talking to Cheryl, Samantha, Wayne or Jason. Sometimes Jason doesn't even wear a tie and he sports an ear ring.

Even more disturbing is their liberality with money. You know these Easy Money machines. At our bank as soon as you pop in a card up comes a message on screen: ASK US NOW ABOUT AN EASY LOAN. You can get $40,000 dollars as fast as you bat a bank book.

The bank is all decorated with loan possibilities. They have pictures of speed boats, sports cars, holidays in Rome, Paris, Tahiti .... There are pictures of warm, kindly, credit managers, who will help you on your way: Nevile, Scott and Patricia.

One bank down the road recently had little brochures: NO NEED TO BLUSH IF YOUR CHEQUE ISN'T FLUSH... A personal holiday? Apply here. Need a car loan? Personal loans $2000 to $99,000.

Unquestionably the whole banking system has to revert. I won't feel confident again until managers wear waistcoats, until managers have haircuts, until managers and staff abandon Christian names. I don't mind if there is a sign at the Teller's window: 'Your attendant is T.S. Eliot.' I don't want to know more than that.

Our local branch some weeks back was decorated with balloons to celebrate the joyous nature of their lending. Little cypress funereal shrubs would have been more appropriate.

In the meantime what profession would you advise for your son or daughter? You would never contemplate anything as racy and as risky as banking. Unquestionably you would seek something safer, like a bookmaker's apprentice, or even a good position working amongst one-armed bandits or baccarat tables.

Young Henry might rise to a really wonderful secure position like manager of a Tabaret.
