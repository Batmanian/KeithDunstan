Who Smashes the Beethoven, Bach and Brahms LPs?

6 min read.

First published 1991

A column on the death of the vinyl LP as Polygram stops pressing new titles, tracing Keith Dunstan's own audio history from an HMV portable gramophone and 78s smashed with a hammer through to CD and the first Compact Disc Video players.

Columns Which Drip With Nostalgia

Looking back on it my audio life has been a mess.

We heard this week that Polygram Records are not making any more LP discs. It's all tape or CD from now on. You will notice I am not saying compact disc. If you don't slip into the vernacular with CD you are nowhere.

All right, Polygram is one of the big ones. They produce Phillips, Deutsche and I don't know what else. Maybe other companies are still producing LPs, but like the wind up clock, and the 26 ounce beer bottle, it's practically all over.

All the new releases now come on CD or tape. They don't even think of LP. So there it is, all your lovely LPs, hundreds of them maybe, beautifully arranged in their specially designed LP sized cabinets, obsolete. To say nothing of that wonderful turntable, that the hi-fi shop assured you was the state of the art in balance and majestic motion with the automatic pick up which would rise and fall with the delicacy and lightness of a snow flake, obsolete.

Barry Humphries, when he gets into one of his reminiscent moods, tells the tale when he worked for one of the record companies. He had just left Melbourne Grammar and it must have been back around the dawn of the fifties. These LPs were the greatest miracle since the invention of the electric lamp. They would run for 40 to 45 minutes on one disc. But that wasn't all, there was a whole new dimension of sound so brilliant it was better than listening to the real thing.

All the old 78 rpm acetate discs were obsolete. Barry said they put him in a room and he had just one job. He had to smash 78 rpms with a hammer. You will remember Beethoven's 9th took six or seven records. He worked his way through all Beethoven. Smash! Smash! Bach. Smash! Smash! Mozart. Smash! Smash! Brahms. Smash! Smash! . . .

After two days he was knee deep in acetate chips, the ruins of all the great artists and orchestras and orchestras. He felt like a man in a mad surrealist dream, and although the pay was good, he quit.

Is there already some university student with the job of smashing LPs? Although I guess it's not so easy. They seem to be made out of some curious plastic. Perhaps you have to be made out of some curious plastic. Perhaps you have to be made to boil all Beethoven, Bach, Brahms and the entire orchestras of Europe in one great pot.

Just last year I went to our local hi fi shop and reported that it was time I acquired some new gear. The salesman, who was so elegant he could have been at Sothebys, said too many people had made the mistake of not doing things properly. I am not very technical but I think he explained that I would need German speakers, equipment with upper woofer and lower woofer, transient and ambient speakers. I would need a tape deck, a CD player, a quality amplifier. Yes, I could probably get out of it for as little as $2500.

That was when I started to weep. It all made me dream of the beautiful days when the playing of gramophone records was so uncomplicated and you never dreamed of laying down tracks.

I started with an HMV portable gramophone. Classical records were always 12 inches and pop records were always 10 inch.

The HMV was all right for the 10-inchers, but for the 12-inchers you had to give it another wind halfway through and this had to be done with great delicacy so as not to upset Fritz Kreisler.

The next step forward was the electric turntable with the pick up arm. This was fantastic. Astor made them and they cost 12 pounds and sixpence. If I remember rightly, you connected one lead to the top of the valve and the other to earth. I tell you, this was the ultimate in sound, as good as being in the Town Hall.

Yet it wasn't long before people considered Mickey Mouse radios not good enough. It was vital to go for a big radiogram complete with pick-up arm fitted with fibre needles.

We went one better. There was no question that the perfect sound was provided by cactus spikes. These had to be sharpened on mother's cardboard nail file. The spike had to be changed for every side. We cultivated so much prickly pear at our place it looked like an outback Queensland cattle station.

Then someone invented the automatic record changer with the sapphire tipped needle. It rendered all old 78s obsolete because they had to be in a special sequence. You put them in a stack of five or six and they would drop down one by one with an appalling clunk.

No sooner had we invested in this remarkable device than out came the microgroove LP which once again rendered our entire record collection obsolete. Oh yes, it's been just one plot after another.

So we proceeded, the fi got hi-er and hi-er. We proceeded on to stereo sound and even quadraphonic sound, where you needed four tracks and four speakers. My mate Alf had the best equipment, 60 watt output and no distortion whatever at full power. It was important, he said, to actually make the windows rattle to receive the full majesty of the highs and lows.

His sound, he insisted, was better than in any concert hall because he could place my ears in exactly the right position. My ears did not work for three days afterwards.

So now we start all over again. For a time we were in danger of switching to a new miraculous digital tape. Apparently CDs are in full command. From a mouse squeak to the triumphant sounds of Beethoven you can hear it all on a Nakamichi 550 . . .

But don't think you are through. I asked at Thomas' record centre in the city what was the next cab on the rank.

CDV, they told me. CDV? Yes, Compact Disc Video. You put on your disc and the orchestra comes up on your video screen at the same time. It should be here in a month or so, gold disc which will cost around $39.95. Except, of course, you will need a new player. They tell me the visual is superb, such definition far better than your normal TV, and such clarity of sound, you will have to get one.

But please don't boil up your old CDs. Keep them. I'm thinking of digging out my old HMV gramophone.
