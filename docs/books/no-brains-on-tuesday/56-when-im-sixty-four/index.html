When I'm Sixty-Four

5 min read.

First published 1991

Turning 64 to the strains of the Beatles song of the same name, Keith Dunstan works through each verse in turn, from the price of home-bottled wine and the vanishing art of mending a fuse to babysitting grandchildren Vera, Chuck and Dave and the long-gone two-a-day postal delivery.

I was driving home in the car last week and on came that sing-song plaintive tone of the Beatles:

'Will you still need me, will you still feed me, When I'm sixty-four.'

Good grief! It was my birthday and I was sixty-four. When the pink-cheeked, almost innocent, long haired Beatles wrote that song a sixty-fourth birthday seemed a date so distant in the future, it would never arrive. Oh, but it has.

The marvellous nostalgic song droned sweetly on:

When I get older losing my hair Many years from now

Oddly enough I don't feel any different. None of this business of serving and sprinting to the net any more. Indeed sprinting is gone for ever. Do you know what I notice particularly, everybody is bigger, particularly the girls. If you go out jogging all the girls seem six feet tall and they go past as if you're standing still.

Will you be sending me a Valentine Birthday greetings a bottle of wine.

Yes, she did that all right. As for the bottle of wine, particularly the whites, the standard has improved no end, the chardonnays and the champagnes. But dear friend, what has happened to the price? In our pre-Beatle glory it was untaxed and remember how we used to home bottle our reds for a shilling a bottle?

If I'd been out until quarter to three Would you lock the door?

Would you. She just might. The shock would be so problematical that. My normal problem is just keeping awake past 9pm, particularly when television is going through its 240th repeat of 'Upstairs Downstairs' or 'Executive Stress.' Quarter to three, I might even lock it around quarter to eleven.

I could be handy mending a fuse When your lights are low.

I love that line. About the only brilliant handyman things I could do were mending fuses and changing washers in taps. But time has moved on. We have four heaters, now with these fancy pop-up fuses. If we use four heaters at once, you just turn off a few heaters, push it back into place again. She doesn't need me for that job at all.

Doing the garden, digging the weeds, Could you ask for more?

Not really. Funny thing that, in the first years of marriage gardening is far too slow-moving, no time for that. But then you discover that planting a bottle brush, raising the hydrangeas through another season, gives a nice feeling of continuity, even eternity. As for the weeds, I knew a great journalist, Arthur Richards, who would say: 'I always like to come back home, stick a fork in the ground and pull out a weed, its long root still intact. Pulling out a weed in your garden is the true sense of ownership.'

Every summer we can rent a cottage In the Isle of Wight, if it's not too dear.

Don't know about the Isle of Wight, but Lorne, Palm Beach, Sorrento is now off the foreshore at Portsea, even Whale Beach. My father always used to say, never try to live in two houses. You don't know where anything is. Inevitably your trousers end up in one house and your shirt in another.

We shall scrimp and save Grandchildren on your knee Vera Chuck and Dave.

Scrimp and save is correct. We had to scrimp and save to pay the babysitters, then drive them home at 12.30 a.m. Now we are the baby sitters, maybe twice a week. Rather nice really. We keep the fridge full of ice blocks and Eskimo Pies. The spare room walls are covered with astonishing drawings of Grandma and Grandpa. We don't have to discipline Vera, Chuck and Dave and we can package them off home. Then when they have gone you find hunks of playdough, bits of Lego under the sofa, and little Chuck has changed all the digital clocks and even set the alarm for quarter to three.

Send me a postcard, drop me a line Stating a point of view.

Not many people drop a line any more, more likely a fax. But if I did drop a line in my sixty-fourth year I would point out that things were infinitely better. Remember a city wine glasses compulsorily removed from the table, the sepulchral Sunday, when everything closed including the local tennis courts? Remember, shudder, staying at some of those country hotels? There were two postal deliveries daily plus a delivery on Saturday mornings, but the telephone was awful. Sometimes you would wait five minutes before the girl said: 'Number please' and when you got the wrong number you started all over again.

As for entertainment there was the Hoyts suburban theatre and the Lux Radio Theatre on the wireless on Sunday nights. As for sport our tennis players seemed to win more often, our cricketers I am sure were better, and as for football, that is infinitely worse. There was a time, particularly in Melbourne, when the clamour went for only six months in the year. Now we start it at the height of the bushfire season.

Give me an answer, fill in a form. Mine for ever more. Will you still need me, will you still feed me When I am sixty-four?

Oh, I hope so. It would be a desperate situation otherwise. It's not only the feeding. Who else would find car keys, and reading spectacles 45 times a day? Who else could remember the names of the people we went out to dinner with last night? Who else would find all the column ideas and who else would help with all those weeds?
