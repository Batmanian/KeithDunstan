The Morality of Chookery

5 min read.

First published 1991

The moral difficulty of retiring backyard hens once their egg laying drops off is worked through via a 1930 poultry manual's brisk instructions for wringing, chopping or slitting a chook's throat, none of which the family can bring itself to use on its four pet leghorns, known collectively as the String Quartet.

There is a dreadful moral crisis that comes to all those who keep chooks.

What do you do when they get too old? At our house we are weak, sentimental creatures. We cannot face up to the role of the executioner.

I have been to the State Library seeking guidance. There they have 222 books on keeping chooks. I sought out Backyard Poultry Raising, which Mr John F. Adams wrote in 1930.

He was clear on the subject. Hens are excellent layers for the first year. A splendid fail-me-never white leghorn, says he, will lay 150-250 eggs in her first year, six to eight times her own weight.

Next year the performance is down 25 per cent, and so on. Never keep your hens more than two years, said Mr Adams. Butcher them or they merely become boarders indeed. What about kindness and pensions for faithful long service? First separate out those fowls you want to butcher, said Mr Adams. (I see, chooks have their own special death row.)

Do not feed them for 12 hours before butchering. Give them only water. (Good Heavens, they don't even get their last condemned cell banquet.)

Mr Adams explained there were three ways to kill chooks.

(a) You can wring their necks. Grab the bird by the neck and give the body a couple of rapid and determined swings. A good method is to just hold the bird firmly at arm's length and pull about nine inches. Adjust your pull carefully. Pull too hard and the head comes off. Don't pull hard enough and the hen will remain alive. 'And VERY disturbed,' says Mr Adams.

(b) Stretch the head and neck on a chopping block. Sever the head smartly with an axe or a hatchet. You must hold the bird smartly with a firm grip because fowls are very muscular. After the headman's stroke requires a little accuracy, adjust your grip and with a vigorous reflex action thrust the body into a large bucket for several minutes, 'explains the calm executioner,' Adams.

(c) Mr Adams likes this one best, but it sounds even worse. Hang the birds by their legs and, using a sharp slender knife, cut their throats.

I went home feeling ill. We love our chooks. We have four white leghorns which we call 'The String Quartet.' They are beautiful creatures, haughty, proud, pure white with fiery red combs.

We let them out on parole from 3 p.m. until dusk every day. The first time we let them out, there was terror. Nobody told us Leghorns could fly. They took off and settled way up in the top of the old prunus. I had to climb up and fetch them down.

No trouble any more, they follow us around to see what we are doing and they are much easier than the grandchildren. After an hour or two they just wander back into the chicken house to bed.

We take a childish pleasure in collecting the treasure. I mean, inevitably we go up to the fowl house three or four times a day to see if there are any more eggs. It's marvellous. The String Quartet give off contented little clucks, pointing out: 'We did that.'

Sometimes on cake-making day there is an egg shortage. Marie stands over them, 'Come on girls, faster, we need more.' Inevitably they oblige.

We don't stand for any of this nonsense about cholesterol. Could anyone seriously believe that anything as natural and pure as the hen's egg could be bad for humans? My friend Mr Adams is eloquent about this. He says: 'How can you describe the wonder of the taste of the genuine fresh free range egg?'

'You can always tell the difference,' he says. 'The yolk has that rich deep colour, like a hunter's moon just coming over the horizon.' As for the shape, 'a genuine farm fresh egg will reveal a yolk tall and perky as a young breast, almost a perfect hemisphere.'

But the non fresh ordinary egg, ugh. He says the breast has gone flat, the colour is pale and the white is mere water around the edge.

You know the best way to eat this gorgeous creation, the farm fresh egg: just boil it for three minutes, then empty the contents onto a slice of hot buttered toast.

My grandmother used to keep chooks. When she had a choice piece of gossip, and that was often, she would say: 'All right children, go outside and see if the chooks have any water.' It's a ploy that remains very useful.

Our Henry, rising four, is fascinated by the String Quartet. Several nights back he complained that he had left his Match Box Ferrari outside. O.K. we get the torch. He leads me straight to the chook pen, where he inspects the four chooks all cuddled side by side on their roost, tucked up for the night. 'Is it Henry?' 'Oh,' said Henry. He didn't even mention the Ferrari. He too went off to his roost and bed.

They have been through everything with us. Last winter, when we went off on holidays, we thought it best to leave them with a daughter in Maldon. So as we set out we put them, each one, in a bag. As we approached Maldon we had horrid feelings of guilt. 'There wasn't any air in those bags. They'll be dead.'

No trouble at all. They were their usual perky selves, and would you believe, at the bottom of each bag there was an egg.

I mean, I ask you. What are we going to do? We have another book on backyard poultry. He is even more ruthless than Mr Adams and he says: 'You must eat your own off even after one year. Confidence has to be restored. Therefore we will be running backyard poultry.' They are not the tasteless variety you get now. They bring back all the wonderful memories of how chicken used to taste when you were a child.

But I don't think we can go through with it. Eat the String Quartet! That's cannibalism. I think we will be running the Eventide Chook Retirement Home. But if you will forgive me, I think I'll just nick out and see if there are any eggs.
