When I'm Sixty-Five

5 min read.

First published 1991

A self-audit of newly official 'geriatric' status at 65, rating memory, eyesight, hearing, sleep and sense of smell as patchy but still sport-umpire-grade sharp where it counts, and finding real compensation in a pensioner's cheap gallery ticket and a deepened pleasure in ordinary things like birdsong and the last glass of a good bottle of wine.

The terrible truth is that this columnist has just become an official geriatric.

Tram conductors and railwaymen get their early retirement plans and whizz off in their fifties, for most the official geriatric figure is 65, judged even when they are 80, Popes and politicians refuse to be gagged even when they are 90, but look out for writers. Writers keep going until they drop nose first on to their word processors. I think it was Thea Astley who said they should be given some sort of pill which would force them to stop.

Mind:

It has been an average year, the greatest difficulty has been memory. Often it has been like putting a disc into the disc drive of the computer, pressing the buttons and nothing comes up on the screen. Names are the very devil. It would be a great help if it were compulsory for all human beings to wear name tags.

But memory is such a precious thing. I was reading an article the other day by a famous author...oh dear, what's his name, it's right on the tip of my brain. He wrote a book...I'll have the title in a minute if I just start thinking of something else...Anyway you must have read it.

This author...you know who I mean...confessed that he went into his library, looked at the great expanse of books on all his shelves, every word a treasure, a life time of absorbed knowledge, then he said to himself: 'Do you know I don't think I can remember more than a line that is in any of them.' 'Oh, I know the feeling.'

Sight:

Still operable, except that glasses now have to be tied permanently around the neck or 25 per cent of the day is spent looking for them. Telephone books have become outrageous, but the worst are street directories. Have you ever been out in the car, 7.15 p.m., fading light, trying to find Finschafen Road, Highett, Map 264, K7, and Melway got it all wrong.

But then there are times when the quality of eye sight is truly amazing, perhaps world class. I know immediately when a tennis linesman has made an error even though I am 15,000 kilometres from the event. Umpires rarely know what they are doing. I can detect an error in an umpire's lbw decision, even though it is 100 metres off in the grandstand.

Sleeping:

Very curious. You tend to sleep when you don't want to. I mean sleep is almost impossible after 4 a.m., but it is easy in railway carriages, trams, concerts, plays, dinner parties. One notices also on television that mini series are becoming more and more mini. Some of them are all over in five minutes.

Hearing:

Becoming distressingly conservative and unadventurous. I have come to the opinion that little noise produced after say 1910 or 1920 is worth hearing, except contemporary stuff orchestrated by our magpies and wattle birds.

Particularly worrying is the music provided in many restaurants. I have an old friend who always calls up first: 'Do you have music in your restaurant?'

'OH YES SIR, WE DO. WE DO.'

'Thank-you very much. I'll go somewhere else.'

We have a mutual understanding in our house, the intensity of our lack of hearing increases in proportion to what we don't want to do. It is remarkable how difficult it is to hear important information when one is reading the newspaper.

Physical Activity.

Actually improving. There is more time now to do the things one likes doing. We cycle more, we walk more. I remember in the old days when serving at tennis, the first serve always was a thunderbolt, the next dead slow to get it in. Now the first serve is slow and the second fast out of desperation. My backhand is not functioning all that well, but come to think of it, I don't think it ever did.

Sense of smell:

Very good shape thank-you, good for another 20 years. Love the smell of eucalyptus particularly when falling branches through the mulcher. Why do even males wear such appalling perfume in restaurants and picture theatres, when the simple smells are the finest on this earth? Any female could carry me right off if she had freshly ground coffee or a few leaves of basil behind each ear.

Advantages:

The Government takes a kindly view and issues you with a special geriatric card. It is possible to get cheap travel all around the world, to get into art galleries half price. Movie theatres offer special rates and recently there was even a geriatric week when geriatrics got in free. But I tell you what, it is downright infuriating when you ask the girl at the counter whether she would like proof of age and she gives you a long tired look: 'No Sir, that won't be necessary, thank-you.'

But there is another big advantage, an intensity of pleasure. How can one put it? You know when you savour a specially good bottle of wine particularly the last glass? In the same way life's simple pleasures become more important, the daily companionship of laughter from the kookaburras, announcing the dawn and the sunset; the sound of the cricket ball on the bat from the cricket ground over yonder, checking to see whether the peace rose has created another artist's miracle, or watching those astonishing New Holland honey eaters pushing their needle beaks into every flower, how diligently they gather breakfast.
