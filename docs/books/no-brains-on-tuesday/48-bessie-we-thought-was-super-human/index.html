Bessie We Thought Was 'Super' Human

5 min read.

First published 1991

Keith Dunstan says goodbye to Bessie, the family's toy poodle put down at 15, recalling her escapes from a Prahran vet and a West Wyalong garage, her four kilometre solo dash home through heavy traffic, and the strict bedtime and mealtime rituals that made her, in his telling, closer to a small opinionated human than a dog.

Bessie died on Saturday. We took her down to the vet. A very sympathetic young lady gave her a needle and put her to sleep for ever.

Actually I didn't have the guts to go through with it. I sat in the car while my wife took her inside. Simple, really. Price for death and disposal, $25.

Perhaps right-to-lifers would not approve, but we are really kinder to animals than humans. Bessie was 15, half blind, an epileptic and all systems had begun to fail.

As I sat in the car, rain running down the windscreen, I had memories. Bessie was a toy poodle, chocolate coloured and tiny, absurd.

She would sleep in a basket in our bedroom and at 4 a.m. there was a ritual. Bessie would jump up on the end of one of the beds, sleep in single beds, and for Bessie it was like conferring an honour, a C.B.E. I scored the honour only once, when I made it. But once on the bed, she would stay there motionless, asleep at the back of your knees.

Oh, we were worse than that. Bessie went everywhere with us, in trains, out to dinner, away on holidays. She was so small she could fit in my wife's handbag, the one object that could always sneak her into motel rooms. We had to be careful though, she would always bark at the arrival of the breakfast tray.

We went off to California for three years and this was a heartbreak. We left Bessie with our eldest daughter, who loved her as much as we did, but the worry was, would Bessie ever remember us. And she didn't, not for five minutes. It was not visual. It was her sense of smell that set off something in her memory bank.

Suddenly she let off a loud squeal and started doing spectacular Warwick Capper leaps all around the room. From then on she followed my wife at the range of two millimetres just in case she might try that California trick again.

The day Bessie was spayed was dramatic. We had to take her to a vet in Prahran and as we left her she was shivering. She knew something appalling was about to happen. Half an hour later the vet telephoned us, very alarmed. He said Bessie had jumped out of his arms and with the speed of light dashed out the door. The whole staff had set off in pursuit. There had been an incredible chase, down Chapel Street, in, out and around the Housing Commission, up and down corridors, but she had escaped. He was terribly sorry.

As he spoke there was a yelp. Bessie was pushing her nose against the front window. Now Bessie had covered four kilometres of heavy traffic, and crossed at least three roads, and come straight home. She had never been across that route before. She didn't need a Melway street directory to find her way round.

Yes, last Saturday morning was very bleak and the memories kept coming faster and faster. There was the extraordinary episode at West Wyalong, and the man at the garage who fixed the windscreen said, 'Leave her in the car with me and all will be ready when you return.'

It was a big mistake. Bessie did another flit, this time straight through a shattered glass hole, and when we returned the desperate garage man had given up his chase. For 24 hours we hunted. You don't know how stupid you feel wandering up and down strange streets, pathetically shouting 'Bessie'. We had an appointment in Forbes, and by the time we reached the Forbes motel we were both in tears. 'I can't stand it,' I said, 'we're going back.'

All day we hunted. We were just about to give up, when suddenly there was a familiar yelp. She had been waiting on a street corner close to the point where we first lost her. Captain Cook, when he returned from his first voyage, wouldn't have received a better welcome.

There were certain things she would not do. Chasing sticks was rather pathetically childish. She loved long walks and adored the beach. She would whimper with joy at the first sign of a jog. However she would not tolerate jogging, totally unproductive and probably bad for the heart.

You just had to treat her like an adult human being. Even at 15 she was not smart enough to read or write, but she understood perfectly the English language. So sensitive matters, like the fact we were having chicken for dinner, or the existence of a Violet Crumble, we would spell out in front of her. However, tell her it was time for her epilepsy pill, and she would front up immediately.

Her absence will be noted by a host of other creatures. Flat dwellers have to indulge in very firm procedures. There is an incredible exodus to the park at 6.45 a.m. daily. There Bessie would meet an English Sheepdog, two German Shepherds, an Airedale Terrier and, best of all and her own size, a Cocker Spaniel. The same ritual would take place about 10 p.m., peak hour coming immediately after the best TV program was finished.
