Foreword

3 min read.

First published 1962

Sir Edmund Herring's foreword to 'The Paddock That Grew', recalling the M.C.G.'s landmark cricket moments from W. G. Grace and the first Test match of 1877 through to his own boyhood memory of Victor Trumper making a pair in 1908.

This is a book that is going to give a great deal of pleasure to a much wider public than members of the Melbourne Cricket Club. For it recounts in a highly entertaining way the history of what may well be described as a great national institution. No history of Melbourne, or for that matter of Victoria or Australia, would be complete without some record of the part played by this remarkable club in the life of the community and in the development and management of international cricket.

Founded only three years after the first beginnings of Melbourne, it dominated cricket in Australia until 1912. It set the stage for the early English touring sides, and itself brought out a number of them. It was not until 1903 that the Marylebone Cricket Club brought out its first team. Before that the initiative had come from Australia, and mainly from this club.

In this book is to be found the story of the great men who over the years have built the club, from F. A. Powlett, its first President, onwards. Here, too, is the story of the club's cricket grounds. The first at Batman's Hill where the club's first cricket match was played on 22 November 1838. The present ground is the club's third, acquired in 1853 and in 1861 described by Stephenson, who brought England's first team to Australia, as being better than any ground in England.

What a thrilling story is that of the Melbourne Cricket Ground and what historic events have taken place there! Here the reader will find graphic descriptions of many of the great matches that have made cricket history. Here, once again, the giants of the past are brought to life. One reads of Dr W. G. Grace and that first Test match against England, which began here on 15 March 1877, when Charlie Bannerman retired hurt for 165 and Australia won outright by 45 runs. One can sense the tremendous excitement that swept Melbourne and Sydney.

Here, too, is the tragic story of the first Test match I ever saw played in February 1908, when Victor Trumper, whom I had really come to see, made a duck in each innings. And so it goes on from one dramatic story to another. This is the fascination of the great game we call cricket, a game that has done so much to mould the character of our people. Trumper, I may say, made a dazzling 166 in the next Test match played in Sydney two or three weeks later.

But it is not only of cricket that this history tells. The Melbourne Cricket Club has fostered and promoted all kinds of sporting activities. And the Melbourne Cricket Ground is much more to Melbourne than a mere cricket ground. Its story is woven into the life of our community. Here for some twenty years was held the Austral Wheel Race, which at one time bid fair to outshine even the Melbourne Cup. Here have been played, year by year, the football finals before ever larger crowds. Here came our gracious Queen to see the school children in 1954, here were staged the Olympic Games in 1956. It is truly a wonderful tale that our author has to recount. It is a pleasure and a privilege to commend it not only to the members of the club but to all who are interested in the history of our great city and State.

E. F. HERRING.
