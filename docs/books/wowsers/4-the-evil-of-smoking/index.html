The Evil of Smoking

8 min read.

First published 1968

Surveys a century of Australian anti-smoking crusades, from E.W. Cole's illustrated warnings and the Juvenile Smoking Suppression Acts of 1900-1905 to the Methodist church's long, losing battle over whether its own ministers should be allowed to smoke.

Americans during 1967 consumed 562,000 million cigarettes—nearly 138 packets for every man, woman and child. Yet the United States of America was the hope of the world. In 1966 the U.S. Congress decreed that every packet of cigarettes was to carry a warning that smoking was a danger to health. Alas, the warning turned out to be a boost to consumption. Instead of cutting down, Americans smoked 10 million more cigarettes in 1967.

The real trouble, perhaps, is this—modern anti-smoking drives have little of the zing and superb scare power of the great campaigns of eighty or even forty years ago. Australia's leading anti-smoker was Edward William Cole, the publisher and proprietor of Cole's Book Arcade.

Who will forget his marvellous sketches entitled: 'Brother who smoked thereby destroying his Vital Organs, his Good Looks, and Stunting his Body', and 'Brother who Didn't Smoke and therefore grew Good-looking, Big, Healthy and Strong'?

Also one could not fail to be frightened by the photograph which was labelled 'A good-looking young man soothing, comforting, poisoning, and gradually destroying himself with Tobacco'. Then no sensible parent could fail to take note of his examples of handwriting. There was the example, written in May 1905 by a boy when he was a victim of the insidious habit. The writing was wretched, illegible. Next there was the example taken in March 1906 when the boy had given it up. The writing was clean, pure and copperplate.

The perfect reference book is Coles Funny Picture Book No. 1, which is still being published. It has all the information:

The vast majority of the medical fraternity condemn smoking, especially by the young.

Smoking injures multitudes of boys in many respects.

Smoking often leads boys into bad company.

Smoking often makes them precocious, undutiful, impudent and callous.

Smoking often ruins the health.

Smoking generally stunts the growth.

Smoking generally sallows their complexion.

Smoking often leads them to lying.

Smoking often leads them to stealing.

Smoking often leads them to drinking.

Smokers cannot excel in athletic sports.

Smokers are always at the bottom of the class in school, and backward at all kinds of study.

Medical men and observing experts affirm that many diseases are caused or accelerated by the use of tobacco among which are:

Heart disease, consumption, cancer, ulceration, asthma, bronchitis, neuralgia, paralysis, palsy, apoplexy, indigestion, dysentery, diarrhoea, constipation, sleeplessness, melancholia, delirium tremens, insanity. Smoking frequently leads to prolonged suffering, often destroys the appetite, sometimes leads to loss of memory, often leads to despondency, smoking sometimes leads to suicide. Tobacco fumes, especially in small and poorly-ventilated houses or rooms, injure or destroy the health of multitudes of wives, and injure the health or kill multitudes of infants and children. Tobacco using injures the unborn child by giving it a puny body. Tobacco is fast degenerating the race. A third of the recruits for the Army are disqualified through smoking.

In the eighties it was the thing to do to sign the pledge against smoking, just as one signed the pledge against alcohol. On 21 October 1889 The Abstainer, the official journal of Melbourne's Total Abstinence Society said: 'Smoking causes restlessness at night, with disagreeable dreams and weakening of both body and mind. Tobacco contains an alkaloid known as nicotia, which is the active constituent of the plant. It is almost as powerful a poison as prussic acid. A single drop given to a rabbit killed it in three and a half minutes.'

The issue of 21 September 1889 pointed out that in Turkey the Sultans and Priests treated smoking as a crime punishable, in many instances, by the most barbarous of deaths. In Persia smokers were treated as criminals. In Russia towards the early part of the seventeenth century, their noses were cut off. And as an example of the spreading menace of the weed, The Abstainer quoted this song:

Tobacco engages
Both sexes, all ages
The poor as well as the healthy
From the court to the cottage
From childhood to dotage
Both those that are sick and the healthy.

After 1900 all Australian States brought down Juvenile Smoking Suppression Acts in the hope of ordering the weed out of existence.

In 1900 Dr Ross in the New South Wales House of Representatives introduced a bill which would have made it against the law for a child under sixteen to smoke. He said:

The habit which I wish to stamp out is 10,000 times worse than the plague. It is sapping the very foundation of the youth of the country. You cannot pass along the streets without seeing the dwarfed constitutions, the broken down nervous systems, the sallow complexions which are the result of smoking.

There is not a day when the paper does not report a sudden death from heart failure. Why is that? It is due to the use of these abominable, accursed cigarettes, which are turned out by manufacturers in America at the rate of half a million a day. The Emperor of Germany died from cancer from smoking cigars.

(Mr Norton: No. He died from the hereditary King's Evil.) We know that the Duke of Edinburgh died the other day from carcinoma brought on by smoking cigars. I would rather see my children in premature graves than see them victims to the use of tobacco. It destroys the stomach, the brain, the liver and the nervous system, and it dwarfs the constitution.1

The bill was passed in the Lower House but it got no further. Eventually in 1903 the N.S.W. Parliament passed a bill which made it an offence for a tobacconist to supply tobacco, cigars or cigarettes to juveniles under a penalty of £5. South Australia passed a similar bill in the same year.

The Victorian bill was introduced by Mr Outtrim in 1905. Evidence was produced that 236 out of the 350 inmates at the Kansas Reformatory for Boys had admitted their life of crime was entirely caused by tobacco. And at last real evidence was produced as to why smoking stunted the growth. Mr Outtrim quoted Sir H. D. Littlejohn M.D., president of the Royal Institution who pointed out that smoking caused constant excitement of the salivary glands, causing constant expectoration, and disorders of the stomach at a time when nutrition of the body should be kept at its highest.2

The penalty for a child caught smoking was fixed at ten shillings for the first offence, but no-one in the House could explain with satisfaction how a law man would be able to tell the ages of young smokers, so once again the onus was put on the unfortunate tobacconist. The penalty for selling to a juvenile was £1 for the first offence, £2 for the second and loss of licence for the third. Presumably the awesome power of the law was sufficient to give the tobacconist the wisdom to be able to tell whether a child was sixteen, even if a policeman could not.

It was not quite done for a clergyman to smoke; for a Methodist clergyman it was distinctly improper. The Spectator, the Methodist newspaper, pointed out in 1885 that this question was put to every candidate for the ministry both at the beginning and the close of his probation: 'Do you take snuff, tobacco or drams?'3

The awful drug, however, was making progress all the time, for in a letter to the same journal in 1907, a 'Methodist Mother' wrote: 'It has long troubled me, when I have heard of one Methodist minister and another taking to the disgusting habit of smoking. It is never a step towards righteousness. Then why are so many of our ministers exhibiting such a bad example for our young men.'4

The Methodist Conference in Melbourne in 1936 decreed that ministers were 'at liberty to follow their own consciences'5 if they wished to smoke, but there was one Methodist who stood out, the Reverend W. F. Betts, Superintendent of the Fitzroy Methodist Mission. In November 1932 he declared: 'I am going to have a clean-up. I am going to start with the pulpit. In future no minister or preacher who is a smoker is going to occupy this pulpit.'6

He kept his word. Two years later he gave an address at the mission entitled 'Tobacco—Weed or Witchery—Which?' He described how the pulpit had been closed to nicotine and he said: 'I have been charged with having kept men from it, but my answer is that they have debarred themselves because they have elected to smoke.'

And he continued: 'I feel sorry for the wife who has to sleep with a smoking husband, and I congratulate my wife that every night she sleeps with a man who does not smoke.'7

But as medical evidence against smoking became convincing, the drug became steadily more respectable. At the Methodist Conference in Sydney in 1939, Mr H. Bushell, a layman said: 'This smoking business is going beyond all reasonable bounds, it is against every principle we hold to be dear for our women and girls.'

The motion before the chair was this: 'That the Christian Anti-Smoking League be recognized as a valuable agency within the church for instructing young people upon the need for abstinence from smoking.'

The motion was defeated and Mr Bushel said that ministers had no right to shelve the question simply because they smoked themselves.8

The battle was lost and rarely did it come up again at church meetings. More drama was needed, perhaps something after the style of a meeting of the Anti Narcotic League in 1888. On this occasion in Melbourne a lady opened her speech with the remark: 'Cannibals do not eat people who smoke tobacco.'9

Whether this was an argument for or against tobacco smoking was not entirely clear.
