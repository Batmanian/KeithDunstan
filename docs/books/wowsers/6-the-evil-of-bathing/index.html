The Evil of Bathing

18 min read.

First published 1968

Charts a century of council by-laws policing Australian beaches in the name of morality rather than safety, from Sydney's 1833 bathing curfew and W. H. Gocher's defiant 1902 daylight swim at Manly, through Melbourne's sex-segregated Edwardian beaches and the 'no loitering' laws of St Kilda, to the bikini panics of the 1960s.

Sea bathers always have suffered. Up and down the coast they have been persecuted by every town and city council. Year after year there were new regulations. These regulations were rarely intended to protect their comfort, or even to protect their safety; they were to protect the perfection of their morals.

The prudery of Sydney and the northern States was never as bad as the prudery of Melbourne. Perhaps it was the serious southern demeanour, perhaps it was the cold winds blowing up from Mawson causing shorter summers and less time for experiment, but whatever the reason, Melburnian councillors and matrons always were more easily shockable than those of the north.

And they were shocked. The swimmers themselves never thought much about it. It was the non-swimmers, the lookers-on, who wanted to pass all the regulations. There was something quite frightening in the idea of male and female frolicking in the water together, legs and arms totally bare. Heaven knows what might transpire.

The regulations started early. The New South Wales Government passed an Act in 1833 prohibiting bathing in Sydney Cove or Darling Harbour between 6 a.m. and 8 p.m. Then came another Act in 1838, providing 'that it shall be unlawful for any person to bathe near or within view of any public wharf, quay, bridge, street, road or other place of public resort within the limits of any of the towns between six o'clock in the morning and eight in the evening'.

The same thing happened in Victoria. A law had to be passed forbidding bathing in the Yarra between 6 a.m. and 8 p.m.1 This was in 1841, a time when bathrooms were hardly a common luxury in Melbourne. They went there rather to wash than for the pleasure of swimming, so if they had to sport themselves naked, how much better if at least they were clothed by darkness.

Yet it was not long before public baths became popular. Mr Riddle's Baths opened on the south side of the Yarra in January 1844.2 In 1853 Mrs Ford opened her sea baths at St Kilda and she was followed in 1854 by the famous Captain Kenny.3 These were on the south side of the jetty at St Kilda, and were actually the hulk of an old sailing ship, the Nancy. His circular said:

Bath in the Best Bathing Site
In the Colony.

FENCED IN AND FREE FROM FISH.

The bottom is composed of a thick layer of sand, and consequently the Diver can enjoy his plunge without danger. The depth of the water varies from twelve feet to three feet, affording facilities, as well for the inexperienced and experienced swimmer.

Captain Kenny's was the smart place for 'the gentry of Melbourne', but there were others along the waterfront, including the Royal Gymnasium Baths, which became known as Leggetts and then as Hegarty's Baths. In January 1865 the St Kilda Council had to complain about Hegarty's. The screens were inadequate to hide the bathers from the beach. They swam out to the fences and climbed upon piles and exposed their nudity quite unnecessarily. And in 1873 there was a notice:

In consequence of the proximity of the Esplanade, and the houses bordering on the same, gentlemen using these baths are particularly requested not to unnecessarily expose themselves. Instructions have been given to the police to take action against any persons who may offend in this way.4

In 1861 Captain Kenny opened an establishment for the ladies. You will be pleased to hear that the sexes always were kept well apart. The male swimming instructor did not get in the water with a young lady. He stood aloof on the boards, and the lady swam off, rope around her middle in case of disaster. As for swimming in the open sea, there was not a great deal of this. If a lady wished to bathe there was the bathing machine, the box on wheels which was trundled right into the water, and the dip was taken with the utmost discretion.

As for mixed bathing in the sea, there was little of this until 1908 or 1909, and Melbourne was a long way behind the forward people of Sydney. At the turn of the century the laws had barely changed since the eighteen-thirties. At Sydney's surf beach of Manly only night bathing was allowed. All surfers had to be out of the water by 7 a.m. and a man used to give the signal with a large dinner bell.5 In September 1902, on a hot Spring day, some yachtsmen decided to cool off with a swim. When they came out police were waiting for them. They were all arrested and taken to the local lock-up. The charge—they had broken the local by-law by swimming after 7 a.m.

Mr W. H. Gocher, Editor and Publisher of Manly and North Sydney News, was outraged. The law, said he, was an absurdity and an injustice. In the next issue of his newspaper he announced that he would test the law by 'bounding in for a bathe on the morrow', and the police would have every opportunity to arrest him. The day fixed was a Sunday and the proprietor of the Steyne Hotel, Mr Rosenthal, gave him the use of a room to change into his costume.

Mr Gocher said: 'It was the most enjoyable bathe of my lifetime. But nothing happened. Very few people were about. No posse of police came flying down with drawn batons to the water's edge to yell out to me to come forth and be arrested. There was no mighty concourse of citizens to cheer me as I came shooting in on No. 4 breaker, breathing salt spray and defiance. Outside my few bosom pals present on the sands the passing pedestrians took but a tired sort of interest in my plunge for public liberty and the popularity of the great old sport on all beaches.'6

Actually, one passer-by stopped for a second and asked who the lunatic in the water was, that's all. Mr Gocher was a little depressed that so little notice was taken of him.

The council meeting was the next Tuesday and Mr Gocher received little sympathy. The Mayor, Alderman Quirk, M.L.A. said that he would not tolerate all-day bathing on the beach. Before he did this he would pull down all the sheds, and every Alderman but one agreed with him.7

So Mr Gocher tried again, on the same beach and at the same time of day. A few people wandered up to watch this curiosity, but nothing else happened. So he tried a third time; at last the police arrived and he was arrested.

[FOR THE BULLETIN]

One day my little son I took,
  Clad all in stockingette, to swim;
But as we played a voice shrieked "Look!
  Oh, goodness gracious!—LOOK AT HIM!"
The agitated maiden's eyes
  Were wide with virtuous alarm;
The bathers viewed me in surprise,
  "When he's but nine," I said, "what harm?"
    Whereat they fled.

In breathless haste they dressed, and stood
  Around the brim in shrinking shame—
An army of the genus prude,
  Fair embryos of future fame.
Next day a letter came to me:
  "Dear madam; He must come no more;
The ladies have complained, for see,
  No boys admitted over four."
    "Verb. sap.," I said.

The episode a flood of light
  Let in upon my darkened brain,
And, with a kind of second sight,
  I looked on woman's ways again.
I know at last the reason why
  Piano-legs, in olden days,
Wore neat-frilled pants of dimity—
  It was to save a modest face
    From blushing red!

I understand why girls won't go
  Across a green potato field . . .
The young fresh spuds have eyes, you know,
  And might unto temptation yield.
Alas! if these good women heard
  The naked truth of what is what,
Or knew that sometimes there occurred
  A bare majority—Great Scott!
    They'd drop down dead!

Lala Fisher.

Mr Gocher interviewed Mr Fosbery, Inspector General of Police. Mr Gocher said: 'I felt sure he had been well posted regarding the happenings at Manly. He stated that no magistrate would convict me, but that men would have to wear neck to knee costumes and that the ladies would have to take care not to expose their bosoms.'

On 1 December there was a letter on the Council table from Mr Gocher, calling for all-day bathing. But it was 'received' and nothing more. On that same table there was also a letter from Mr A. G. Cambridge, complaining about bathers misconducting themselves on the Ocean Beach. It was more than 'received'. The Council decided to call in the police to stop such misconduct.

However, almost a year later, on 2 November 1903, the Council did rescind the by-law which banned bathing after 7 a.m. The new by-law said all-day bathing was permitted, provided everyone over the age of eight years wore a neck to knee costume. Penalty for offenders was fixed at not more than £1 and not less than five shillings.

Mr Gocher had won the gratitude of local sufers for ever. They presented him with a watch which carried this inscription: 'Presented with a purse of fifty sovereigns. W. H. Gocher Esq. Pioneer of all day surf bathing, by his Manly friends, February 11th, 1907. Entered the water 2nd October, 1902.'

Mr Gocher died on 18 August 1925 and he was buried in the Waverley Cemetery.

In 1911 the Argus8 reported that surf bathing was the chief pastime of the people of Sydney. Every summer there were thousands more devotees. 'Of course, the bathing is mixed,' the newspaper said, 'women are as enthusiastic as men. Some of them— not many—are clever breaker shooters.' Why, one young lady, Miss Ivy Schilling, 'the surf belle of Manly', title holder of the 'Sirens of the Surf' competition, and the leading dancer of the show 'Our Miss Gibbs', was a very fine swimmer. Only a week before she had rescued a man who was overcome with cramp in one of the dangerous undertow channels.

Yet in 1910 and 1911, all around Victorian beaches strict regulations were enforced. In some areas bathing was banned altogether, at others bathing was allowed only if there was strict segregation of the sexes. The thing to do was to divide the beach down the middle, men on one side, women and children on the other. At Warrnambool the beach was divided, male-female; at Port Campbell it was in two parts, one exclusively for ladies and children and the other for mixed bathing.

At Moorabbin the situation was serious, Cr Scudds said. Bathing was prohibited within forty chains of any Corporation baths; yet people still indulged in this practice. The matter was so serious, and of such concern to decent, respectable people with grown up families, that he would be pleased to use the horsewhip on some occasions.9

In 1912 this was still going on at Williamstown. The girls had to bathe on the western side and the boys on the eastern side. On 7 December, Truth printed this piece of verse:

Now Williamstown, in Grundy's gown, will segregate the sexes.
How with his girl to have a bathe, poor Johnny it perplexes.
Upon the sands, she lonesome stands,
Like Nancy Lee, she waves her hands
But may not join her cobber.
Chorus: She may not join her cobber.

Reading through the newspapers at this time it is fascinating to know how many decent people were genuinely horrified. Here is what the Reverend T. Adamson had to say at the Methodist Conference of 1912:

No modest woman can be associated with mixed bathing and no man who respects the opposite sex could take part in it. I know that arguments will be raised against me. Some excellent men have told me they have been associated with mixed bathing enjoying it in company with their wives and daughters. It is interesting to hear them talk but another gentleman has told me that his wife absolutely refuses to be associated with the practice of mixed bathing. Married women ought to take up a strong position in this matter. If some of them who bathe could hear what is said about them they would be startled.10

Yet the startling thing was the time allowed for bathing. It was banned on Sundays, Good Friday, and Christmas Day, and utterly forbidden any day after 10 a.m. At first reading of the regulations and journals, it is difficult to understand why this was so. If any immorality were to take place, surely it had a better chance in the dark.

The Argus campaigned unceasingly. On one occasion it said, 'Water, Water everywhere, yet we cannot bathe,' . . . 'the property holders along the foreshore may watch the waves temptingly lapping the sands at their front doors, yet they dare not enter the waters when the sun is beating down with all its fury. Yet South Melbourne councillors in their wisdom insist that the people must swelter all day. Towards evening, when the atmosphere becomes cooler and nature does not cry so consistently for relief, the restriction is graciously withdrawn.'11

At last the real answer was found in a protest deputation to the South Melbourne Council on 1 November 1911. Mr Niquet told the council that South Melbourne ratepayers had spent a great deal of money on improvements to the Beaconsfield Parade. Therefore the residents, 'despite the desires of a certain section of the community', had a right to walk along the parade between 10 a.m. and 5 p.m. 'without having their susceptibilities offended'.

And that was the big trouble. All those neck to knee costumes were offending susceptibilities. The Argus wondered if this problem might be solved if the council insisted that every man, woman, child, horse, and dog that proceeded along the Esplanade during bathing hours be blindfolded.12

Regulations slowly improved in the summer of 1912. South Melbourne cracked under pressure and allowed mixed bathing all day, but it was still a case of never on a Sunday. This was the summer when that strange word 'sun bathing' first made its way into print. Some called it a 'new form of worship'. It was another practice that had come over from Sydney. It was amazing how many evil practices seemed to start in Sydney.

In this case the Argus, which was fairly liberal on most new ideas, was dead against it. On 8 March 1912, it said: 'Lolling on the sand, either for individuals or for mixed groups should be regarded as strictly forbidden, not only by law, but by etiquette, which is more effective than the law.' The Argus said sun bathing was a new form of idleness.

The pastime has an astonishing charm for its devotees. Sometimes it becomes almost fanaticism. In these cases men and women can find time for nothing else. In their blind enthusiasm for the sun they are prepared to neglect such necessary formalities as the earning of a livelihood. With such people, indeed, the gradual browning of the skin under the sun's rays appears to constitute half the fascination. Hardened sun bathers become almost permanently brown skinned, even the long winter months failing to bring back quite their native whiteness.13

Sun bathing was bad and the St Kilda City Council passed a law which directly forbade it: 'Any person bathing shall on leaving the water forthwith resume ordinary clothing, and no person shall loiter save as far as is necessary, to enable him or her to dress, run or walk in the vicinity of the beach in bathing costume, or without having resumed ordinary clothing.'

Many people were fined. H. C. Chrysty of Caulfield was convicted at the Sandringham Court, just for standing on the beach in his neck-to-knees and drying himself with a towel. He pleaded that he had to stand there while waiting for the women to come out of the bathing box, but it was no good. He was fined five shillings with 2/6 costs.14 This 'no loitering' law was still on some Council books thirty years later, and it became a seasonal joke for feature writers.

Just when everything seemed to be getting better, on 27 October 1914, the St Kilda Council brought out an incredible series of regulations. The beach was divided into areas. In some spots bathing was allowed only between 4 a.m. and 10 a.m. and others between 4 a.m. and 6 p.m., but on Sundays, Christmas Day and Good Friday, it was not allowed at all after 10 a.m.15

On 2 November 1914 there was a great protest meeting of local citizens at the Methodist School, and they decided that the following Sunday 8 November, they would have what we would call today a mass bathe-in.

Over a thousand people turned up that Sunday morning, and the idea was that they would all march defiantly into the water. Many of the protestors had bathing togs on under their overcoats, but sadly, God seemed to be against them. It was bitterly cold and the rain was falling. Only a few crusaders of vast courage were brave enough to enter the water.16

The St Kilda Council disapproved of the whole affair. It felt that the indignation meeting of the week before had couched its protests in offensive language, therefore it refused to listen to them. Cr Allen, speaking in favor of the regulations, said it should be realized that there was a class of people who visited the beach on Sunday afternoons—people with their wives and families, for instance—who were more modest than the others, and they would consider it objectionable to see anyone showing their legs.17

The citizens had to wait until 18 January 1917, before mixed bathing became completely free, with Sunday hours up to 7 p.m.

For more than a decade, things were relatively quiet. At least Australians were allowed into the water. The next battle was over what they wore, or rather, what they did not wear. The neck to knee, or what was described as the Canadian costume, was considered the only respectable garment for the water.

The Box Hill branch of the Women's Christian Temperance Union passed this resolution in 1925: 'That we deplore the disgraceful exhibition of men and women, who not only parade our beaches in a semi-nude state, but pose in most unbecoming positions to be photographed. Believing the same to be most hurtful to the morals of our community, particularly the young people, we strongly urge that such disgraceful conduct be strictly prohibited by our authorities.'

Yet in the post World War days it was the women who caused the most trouble. The year 1932 saw the backless costume. A Congregational Minister, the Reverend S. H. Cox, described it as 'the hottest thing ever introduced on the beaches'. He said it was a return to the old Paganism of the Epicureans who lived only for the pleasures of life. The costumes would succeed in attracting the prurient minded, and would affect the essential modesty of the pure woman. Girls who wore these daring, disgusting, backless beach costumes were not the kind to uplift the moral tone of the community, nor, when married, to teach morals to their children.

Then in 1933 Cr C. E. Sheppard, president of Warringa shire in Sydney, warned of the shape of things to come. 'Women soon will want to go into the water nude,' said he. The costumes were backless and almost frontless. They consisted of a small brassiere attached to a pair of trunks by thin cords.

After that the men were causing trouble. In 1936 it was a case of 'to roll or not to roll'.18 The top half of male costumes buttoned on to the bottom half, or, alternatively one could roll down the top to display a naked chest.

Anyone who dropped a shoulder strap was liable to a fine of £2.

In 1938, councils were starting to crack, but Cr Levy of Brighton was still able to point out that beaches were the playgrounds for children of tender years and some men were unsightly—'like hairy monkeys'.

And so we progressed. Bikinis first appeared around 1954. The bikini was named after the H bomb on the theory that this was the ultimate. One could not go any further. Surfers Paradise immediately welcomed them as a tourist attraction, and the South Coast Town Council gave them the 'go ahead' on 19 February 1955.19

Bikini girls in other parts of the country still had some difficult battles to fight. On September 30 1961, Aub Laidlaw, a Bondi beach inspector, ordered a shapely young brunette off the beach. She was Joan Barry, twenty-five, of Curlewis Street, Bondi and she was wearing a bikini which would have disturbed few beach inspectors, say, in 1967 or 1968. Aubrey Laidlaw admitted in the Central Court that he had not been dressed according to regulations either. On the day his top had been rolled down revealing his naked chest and also, his beach inspector's badge was obscured.

Exhibit number one in court was the small gold bikini. Detective Sergeant Baret, who had charged Miss Barry with offensive behavior, said he had told her: 'I've never seen anyone wearing less than you are, and I've seen many put off the beach'. On the other hand, Miss Barry said: 'I was shocked and embarrassed. I never thought for a moment my bikini would be objected to.' Miss Barry was fined £3.20

That was Sydney 1961, and they were still causing trouble in Melbourne as late as 1964. In November the Victorian Federation of Catholic Mothers' Clubs, an organization of 18,000 Catholic mothers, asked 19 councils to ban all bikinis from bayside beaches that summer.21

The Federation's secretary, Mrs Mary Cullen, said: 'We definitely think that bikinis lower the resistance of young people to temptation, and contribute to immorality. I can't see anything attractive about bikinis. In my opinion they expose an ugly part of the body.'

The same week that the Catholic mothers were making their protest, Myers, the big department store, was running this advertisement:

On parade in our sun'n'surf shop . . . THE BAREST BODIES ON THE BEACH. Lap up the sun in these saucy little Ada bikinis. Be a dare devil! Show off that brown body in the cutest little 2-piecer. Ada's caught on to the idea . . . turned out teeny bikinis by the score! Cheeky checked cotton, pretty printed tetoron that dries in a wink. All prettied up with lace trims. Parades today.

And if that was not enough, over the page were the Hickory ads. for the latest bras 'Hickory's darling Dare Top swoops low or plunges deep prettily but securely to underline your most dramatic fashion necklines in years. Dare Top is pretty as a picture, gives you exactly the shape (and the security), you need, no matter how your new neckline swoops or plunges.'

Yes, the bikini, like atomic power, was difficult to check.
