The Evil of the Social Evil

34 min read.

First published 1968

Surveys a century of Australian prostitution euphemised as 'the social evil', from the Theatre Royal's notorious Saddling Paddock and Melbourne's Little Lonsdale Street brothels under Madame Brussels, through the 1891 mystery of Parliament's stolen mace, to Brisbane's condoned, medically inspected brothels and the bikini-era brothel economics of the 1960s.

Prostitution in the nineteenth century was almost as common as hotelkeeping, but it was a word too awful to mention aloud. The Victorian Parliament in 1873 called for a report on prostitution but it did not appear under that offensive title. It is listed in the Parliamentary Papers as 'A report on the Social Evil'. Ladies and gentlemen of the Victorian era always recognized that the social evil was prostitution. Victoria, New South Wales and South Australia had organizations for combating it, and these were called Social Purity Societies. A brothel could be a Disorderly House, a House of Ill Fame, an Immoral House, but never a brothel.

Parliamentary reports on the social evil were usually kept under cover, and debates, if any, went unprinted in Hansard. In 1907 the South Australian Parliament came out bluntly and passed a Suppression of Brothels Bill, but Mr Thomas Price, when moving the second reading, said that Adelaide was no worse than any other place 'in regard to the matter'; therefore there was no necessity to discuss the bill. It was not wise, he said, to advertise the evil. Only one member protested, and Mr Price pointed out to him that he was tabling a report; however he hoped that no unmarried members would think of reading it.2

Prostitution was common in the young colonies because opportunities for women were so limited. There were few good jobs—needlework, selling, teaching, working in a shoe factory, waiting on tables, but what else? If a girl could not be kept at home, she usually went into domestic service at ten shillings a week plus keep. These jobs were not easy to get, and the lady in charge had a terrible hold over her staff. After being sacked without a reference it was hard to get a good position. So what then? The streets? The pay was much better.

Dr John Singleton, in his Incidents in the Life of a Physician, said that during the gold rush days at least half of the female immigrants became prostitutes. 'These young women', he wrote, 'when hired from the Immigrants home on deport, should they not suit their employers, or should they leave their service otherwise, have no home to go to, and no mother or relations to care for them, and they are driven to seek lodging in the immediate vicinity of the brothels with which the city abounds. Vast numbers of them without control and friendless have been seduced from virtue's paths.' According to the police reports there were 112 brothels, 257 prostitutes and 500 women in Melbourne without means of support.3

The Argus in 1859 gave a very frank picture: 'Melbourne swarms with prostitutes. Morning noon and night they are seen exhibiting themselves at their doors and windows, and with all the effrontery of harlotry. In the streets they may be seen at all times, frequently without their bonnets, walking arm in arm and three abreast. Around hotels they congregate every evening, they rendezvous at the Theatre Royal bars for special practice at their seductive art.'4

The Theatre Royal in Bourke Street had a special bar—one could almost describe it as a prostitutes' booking agency—called the Saddling Paddock. Indeed most cities had their Saddling Paddocks. The proprietor of the Theatre Royal in Adelaide, Mr J. McDonald, even seemed a little proud of his Saddling Paddock. At the South Australian Royal Commission of 1879, he said he had a special bar set aside called the Saddling Paddock where larrikins, prostitutes, people of all kinds, congregated. One of the Commissioners asked: 'You believe it to be conducive to morality rather than otherwise that these people should have a place where they can drink by themselves rather than they should mix with the general public?'—'Yes,' he said.5

At the Theatre Royal in Melbourne there was a large meeting spot at the entrance to the stalls, pit and upper circle. This was the Vestibule and in the eighteen-fifties it was sleazy and rather unsavoury, with liquor bars down either side. Polite society, larrikins and prostitutes all mixed in together. In late 1858 the management

cleaned it up, charged an entrance fee of sixpence, put in tables and chairs, supplied tea and coffee, decorated the walls with watercolours by S. T. Gill, dressed the waiters in white and the barmaids in black silk. At the far end of the eastern bars, close to the stalls entrance, were two doors to a special room set aside, complete with its own refreshment bar. Here the vice trade was segregated—this was the Saddling Paddock.6

Julian Thomas, in his Vagabond Papers, described the Paddock: 'It is a small bar, presided over by a man, the proceedings here are too unpleasant for a barmaid to witness. Here the most notorious women of Melbourne nightly throng, and run in the companions they have caught in the Stalls or in the Vestibule. The men are of all classes—"gentlemen", betting men, sharpers, clerks, junior officers of merchant vessels and new chums.'7

Julian Thomas described it as 'a disgraceful, flagrant, heinous scandal, flaunting boldly and shamelessly in the face of decent society', and he was terrible in his contempt of these women. He said in France it was possible to take one's wife or daughter to any public place and sit next to the most notorious member of the demi-monde without perceiving by her dress and manner that she was different from the rest of the world. How different it was in Melbourne. Their painted faces, the flaunting of their dress, the vulgarity in their face and manners showed them for what they were. They were also conspicuous for their lack of grammar and their utter vulgarity of speech and soul.

Legend has it that the Saddling Paddock was even under regal patronage. The second son of Queen Victoria, Prince Alfred Duke of Edinburgh, visited there in 1867. J. M. Forde wrote that there was some fun even before the Duke arrived. Some law clerks in Chancery Lane decided to have a joke on Sir Redmond Barry, who was chairman of the presentation committee. 'They hunted up a demi-monde in the saddling paddock at the Theatre Royal, one Carrie . . . She fell in with it, and a couple of days afterwards Mr Gilbert, the secretary, received a letter from Carrie asking for a position in the procession as a public body of some note. In all seriousness the letter was read before Sir Redmond, but Sir R. blandly intimated that though the lady applicant may be a public body in one sense she was not so in the sense which Royal Commissioners understood the phrase and her offer was declined with thanks.'8

Legend has it that Prince Alfred, accompanied by the Chief Commissioner of Police and other dignitaries, went to the Saddling Paddock at the Theatre Royal and then on to Sarah Fraser's 'fashionable bagnio' in Stephen Street. J. M. Forde, an old reporter who covered the police beat for fifty years, was definite on the subject. He wrote:

The Duke made no secret of his visits to a particular bagnio in Stephen Street, the particular attraction there following the Prince to Sydney and taking a cottage in Castlereagh Street, where I have conclusive evidence that His Royal Highness visited the lady, who was certainly one of the handsomest of her class and time. Though the Duke may not have known it, he was carefully shadowed by a Sydney detective while in that city, and I have had that officer's note-book in my possession where he gave in detail the visits to various places by the Duke and the time spent in those places.9

The Duke, captain of H.M.S. Galatea, was twenty-three years old.

At this time Stephen Street was lined on both sides by brothels. Police cleaned it out in 1878 in time for the International Exhibition and the name was so notorious, the Council decided it was time for a name change and it became Exhibition Street. Yet there were still other streets, almost as famous: Little Bourke, Lonsdale and even Swanston Street.

This was the Australian breakdown on prostitution according to the Victorian Government Year Book of 1885-6.

City Population No. of Prostitutes No. per 10,000
Melbourne 282,947 597 21.10
Sydney 224,211 613 27.34
Brisbane 31,109 99 31.82
Adelaide 103,864 500 48.14

The Year Book was self-righteously pleased with the Melbourne figures but it added: 'A very large amount of prostitution appears to exist in Adelaide but the figures being given in round numbers must be regarded with suspicion.'

There were some with the courage to attack all this. The Reverend John Coles was appointed to the Wesley Church in Lonsdale Street in 1875. He described himself as the Bishop of a Dirty Diocese, and he decided to meet sin wherever it lurked, particularly in Little Bourke. 'A viler hell on earth, reeking with every description of abomination could scarcely be found. The horrible scenes that I witnessed appalled me. Unless I had seen what I did I could never have believed that human nature could have sunk so low—lower by far than the beast of the field.'

This was his diary for 19 December 1875:

From ten to one o'clock I visited amongst brothels and thieves' rendezvous. I have been in the lowest parts of London, but it would be well nigh impossible to witness more horrible scenes of moral delinquency and degradation. It was disgusting and loathsome . . . At a quarter to four we had an open air service near Bilkington Square. A great crowd surrounded us. Many of the women who were looking out of the windows and were standing at the doors, were in a state of semi-nudity, as were many of those that stood around us. Most of them were young women from 15 to 25.10

Coles claimed great success, many conversions and much sympathy, but there was a call from his superiors, who said: 'We all appreciate your zeal, Brother Coles and applaud your desire to raise the fallen, and to get sinners converted, but we think that the meetings in houses of infamy late at night, is carrying the thing too far, and it might bring disgrace upon the cause.' Brother Coles was very disappointed.

'All I did was to bow to this decision,' he said. 'What I should have done was immediately to have resigned.'

There were not many Reverend Coles; but the touring English Evangelist, Henry Varley, was a constant campaigner. He claimed that in forty years' experience in all parts of the world, there was no city worse than Melbourne for prostitution. There was one notorious procuress who had assured him that she wouldn't sell her houses for £10,000, and she had just spent £400 in making one place more attractive.11 Henry Varley wrote that the worst days of Rome and Greece 'knew nothing more repulsive', and he called meetings 'for men only' at the Town Hall to tell Melbourne all about it.

The Spectator had regular articles on 'social impurity' and the 'social evil'. In 1885 it wrote: 'Whereas intemperance slays thousands, social impurity slays tens of thousands. Impurity is a greater sin than intemperance amongst the men of the metropolis.' It estimated the number of 'fallen women' in the city at 2,000, a very different figure to the 597 given for the same year in the Government Year Book.12 Also, in bold type, the issue had the somewhat enigmatic message:

EDITOR'S PROVERB FOR YOUNG WOMEN
BETTER HAVE YOUR MAID NAME ON YOUR COFFIN LID
Than Marry One of God's Enemies

The literature on this was interesting. A typical example was 'Where Do You Spend Your Evenings?—A PLAIN TALK WITH YOUNG MEN' by the Reverend Theodore L. Cuyler. It appeared in the Christian Colonist in Adelaide on 8 August 1884 and in The Spectator in Melbourne on 14 August 1885.

However a young man may be situated, the evening hours bring to him certain temptings and perils. The day's work is over and nature craves recreation. No-one understands this natural necessity better than the devil; he makes the most of it. The evening is the harvest time and then he opens the numberless doors of temptation; then he lights up his places of enchantment—brings out his dice boxes, and cards and billiard tables and roulette wheels; then he tunes his horns and violins, and flings wide open his crimsoned and chandeliered gateways to sensual indulgence. It is in the black and dark night that the shameless wanton prowls the streets in 'quest of the young men void of understanding'.—yes, and of some grey-headed fools likewise. How many a one, while father and mother are in their unconscious sleep, 'goeth after her straightway, as an ox goeth to slaughter', and knoweth not that it is for his life.

But the best antidote to all such dangerous pleasures is to find the safe one. No matter how plain or cheap your boarding quarters may be, my young friend, if you have plenty of goods books you may spend many an evening in company fit for a king. Secure a ticket in a public library, and then bring in your room such authors as you can afford to be intimate with. You need never be lonesome while Shakespeare will take his seat beside you, or Macaulay, or Dickens, or Scott . . . Shun every book that stirs lewd passion, or that shakes your faith in God, as you would shun a rattlesnake.

Then, of course, there were others who looked back on this era with nostalgia. One of them was George Meudell, financier, stockbroker, company promoter, bon viveur, and above all creator of the remarkable gusty book which told the inside story of the Melbourne Land Boom: The Pleasant Career of a Spendthrift and His Later Reflections. Just a few extracts give an idea of his ebullient character: 'Doctors prescribe medicine of which they know nothing to cure diseases in a body of which they know less. They cannot cure cancer, consumption, baldness or rheumatoid arthritis.' He approved of only one doctor, Dr George Anderson Crichett, of Harley Street, London. 'Instead of getting stale magazines in Sir George's waiting room, sherry, port and biscuits were provided, and patients invited by printed notice to help themselves. I think the sherry was '56 and the port a '48, both excellent. Wines have the effect of clearing one's vision.' He said also, 'My way of joking is to tell the truth. It is the funniest joke in the world.' And he said that if only someone had smiled during the Land Boom, the 1893 depression would never have occurred: 'Every bank should keep a laughing department where absurd valuations and ridiculous securities are laughed off the premises.'

Long after six o'clock closing came, he looked back on the Melbourne of 1876, the year Briseis won the Melbourne Cup. He said Melbourne then was a roguish sort of a town, wide open and frankly immoral. Hotel bars did not close until 11.30 and as wowsers had not been invented then, and the police force was below strength, there was no repression of drink or of gaiety. The Exchange Hotel in Swanston Street, with bar upstairs and down, was filled with well-dressed people, gay, laughing, chatty. All were drinking bubbly, and nobody was tipsy. Diagonally across the road at the Rainbow Hotel there were six bars filled with about 100 'filles de joie'. The kerosene lamps shone brightly and the barmen were busy opening Moet and Chandon, Pommery and Greno and Krug by the dozen bottles, and everybody was jolly. The next port of call was the Earl of Zetland, where there were many more gay girls in a crowd of fifty. From there it was not far to the 'Saddling Paddock' of the Theatre Royal, a vast vestibule with a quarter of a mile of bars enclosing an area crowded with well-dressed men, diggers in red shirts, bookmakers, jockeys, club men and literally hundreds of ladies of pleasant manners and easy virtue. Then right up Bourke Street there were places where liquor was sold, plus good crab and oysters. 'The demi monde were everywhere in crowds,' he said. 'Where did they live and how, and where have they all gone? The city only mustered 250,000, and here were loose women of no importance in hundreds.'

Meudell said that Lonsdale Street East was the centre of the better class of prostitutes, living in the famous houses kept by Scotch Maude, Madam Brussels, and Biddy O'Connor, while round the corner was Mother Fraser's, a favourite pleasure house of the clubmen.

He told how it all ended:

Gradually the Puritans annexed the money and power of the community, and the Scots church began to head the list of collections on Hospital Sunday. The police under a pillar of the Church, Chief Commissioner H. M. Chomley, began and carried out a social clean up. One by one the street walkers were locked up, one after another the gay houses were closed, and the inmates of dozens of suburban joints were frightened by Presbyterian policemen, while the joyousness of Melbourne's night-life was silently squelched. That class of useful and necessary handmaidens has vanished from the public gaze, although still alive in increased numbers.

Meudell added bitterly that the motor car now was the perambulating brothel, and the hip-pocket flask did the rest.

The fashionable establishments like Madame Brussels', mentioned by Meudell, were just over the road from Parliament House. Madame B's establishment, was at 36 Lonsdale Street, no more than two minutes walk from the Italianate structure itself. It was suggested frequently that honourable members were there when the bells were ringing in the House. In November 1892 there was a furore when a Baptist minister, the Reverend Dr Bevan, announced that a telephone had been connected to a 'house of ill fame', and a Government that allowed this 'was a standing shame and disgrace in the presence of all decent men'.13

Melbourne Punch commented: 'Dr Bevan did not suggest that the telephone was laid on so that the waverers could be rung up when wanted for a division, or that the telephone was for any immoral purpose—but that the Government allowed it to be laid on. We presume that before a wire is connected with any establishment Dr Bevan would require evidence as to the moral character of the occupants.'14

Remarks were always being passed about the moral character of the occupants of Parliament House. The Reverend A. R. Edgar had something to say in 1894. He was a fiery anti-drink, anti-gambling preacher, yet he was much more than that. He was a radical thinker, a fighter for the working man, and the main drive behind the Anti Sweating League at a time when factory conditions and hours could be described as shocking. As superintendent of the Methodist Mission, he was the first man to make 'The Pleasant Sunday Afternoon at the Wesley Church' famous in Melbourne.

On 19 August his Pleasant Sunday Afternoon was well worth reporting. He said: 'You have individuals in Parliament who are a disgrace to the community, men who are proven to be liars and profligates, men who are prepared to tread underfoot everything that is noble to obtain their ends, men who afford some justification for the saying that "Every man has his price", and I tell you that the constituency who will send such a man to represent it is also a disgrace to any country.'15

Parliament did not waste any time. His remarks were 'a scandalous breach of privilege' and the following Wednesday he was called before the Bar of the House. All the galleries were filled, and the Weekly Times reported that by 4 p.m. there was an immense crowd outside Parliament House.16

At last [reported the Argus] he was called in by the Usher of the Black Rod, and the bar was drawn across the entrance to mark the line over which it is not safe for an outsider, not even a clergyman to step.

The Speaker Mr Thomas Bent, asked the Reverend Edgar whether the Age report had been accurate.

'Not quite accurate,' replied Mr Edgar.

'Oh,' said Mr Bent, obviously very much relieved, 'I suppose there is nothing more to be said,' and very craftily he asked Mr Edgar to withdraw.

There were cries of protest from all around the Assembly and only after much debate did the Speaker allow him to return.

Upon his return the Reverend Edgar asked permission to read a statement. He said he had the greatest respect for Parliament, but when addressing his people he was not referring to the Parliament of Victoria, but to all Parliaments. Furthermore he had been misreported. He said scoundrels and blacklegs were too often returned to Parliament, not liars and profligates.

There were repeated attempts by members to ask questions, but Mr Bent deftly turned them all aside, and finally the premier, Sir James Patterson moved the motion: 'That this House having heard the statement of the Reverend Mr Edgar that he was not accurately reported in the Age newspaper proceeds to the next order of the day.'

The Argus called it a one act farce, an animated piece of nonsense: and observed how amusing it was that, as Parliamentarians were now blacklegs and scoundrels and not liars and profligates, there was nothing to challenge.17

It was all too obvious why Mr Bent was so anxious to get rid of Parson Edgar. His remarks at the Pleasant Sunday Afternoon were actually a faintly veiled reference to the missing mace scandal. He did not wish the story to be raised again. This was still the classic unsolved crime and the strangest story in the history of any Australian Parliament.

Parliament sat late on Thursday night, 8 October 1891, and it rose at twenty past one the following morning. The Sergeant-at-Arms took the mace and locked it in an oak box in the ante-room abutting on the corridor to the north of the Assembly chamber. It had been in use since 1866 and it was a copy of the mace in the House of Commons. It certainly was not solid gold, but it did cost £300. At 2 p.m. that afternoon the Clerk of the Assembly, W. V. Robinson, discovered that the oak box had been broken open with a chisel and the mace had gone. The police were called in, and all employees at the House were told not to say a word to anyone.

There were no statements from the Speaker, Sir Matthew H. Davies, and not a word from the police, but as they made their inquiries, there were soon rumours all about town. There had been women up at the House, there had been parties. One of the first reports appeared in Melbourne Punch on 22 October 1891:

The story of the missing mace is an open secret around Parliament House, although it has not yet been recovered. It seems there is reason to believe that it was taken from its place by a Member of Parliament in a drunken freak. We hope it is not necessary for us to appear before the bar of the House for stating the possibility of a member having a drunken freak . . . Twenty-three members of the Assembly have since been racking their brains to think what they did after that last drink on the night the mace disappeared. And each of them wonders whether he had better swear off liquor in the future.

Yet it was another year before it began to blossom into a full scandal. Just before Christmas, 1892 Table Talk suggested that the affair, despite the persistent silence of the police, had not died out. There was a theory that the mace had been taken to a 'house kept by a woman of bad character' and was still there.18

Table Talk only went half way. The Ballarat Courier took it from there:

After many days the joke is leaking out. The mace was not stolen, but was taken from Parliament by some 'festive cusses' for a lark, and is even now, so it is freely stated, in a bawdy house in Melbourne where it is freely exhibited to clients, and where it has, so it is said, been used in low travesties of Parliamentary procedure . . . The authorities have winked at the scandal, the police have been restrained by those in authority, and the journalists who know the facts, concealed them in the interests of some persons' reputations.

The Courier added a nice hint for those of their readers who knew their brothels, that: 'the mace was abstracted from Parliament House by some courtesans who had been entertained by public men, and that the abstraction was viewed at the time as a joke, equal to anything in Boccaccio.'19 Annie Wilson, Madame Brussels' greatest rival, ran an establishment called Boccaccio House, and according to legend this was the spot where the girls ran their mock parliament.

Parliament took offence and six days later, on 12 January 1893, the proprietor and publisher of the Ballarat Courier, Mr Robert Clark, was required to appear at the Bar of the House. Melbourne Punch mused that no doubt the Courier would continue to be published even if its sinful proprietor was hanged, drawn and quartered. Yet probably his life would be spared if he consented to apologise and explained that what was published was only rumour.20

Punch summed up the situation accurately. Robert Clark turned out to be a frail, old man. He said the story was quite untrue and his apology was so red-faced and humble it was almost embarrassing. Although he was proprietor, he had no idea his editor was going to do this sort of thing. 'I regret exceedingly its publication,' he said, 'and I apologise humbly to this honourable House therefor . . . I am prepared to publish an ample apology in the same leading columns themselves.

Mr Clark's apology was accepted and he received a mild reprimand, yet Major Williams, the actual editor of the Ballarat Courier and the man who wrote the article, was not called, even though he was in the precincts of the House. The Bulletin suggested that, had Major Williams been called, he was prepared to assert the truth of the article and defy the House to do its worst. Now the whole mystery of the missing mace was left quite undisturbed.22

The appearance of Robert Clark at the Bar of the House caused so much comment, and the situation was becoming so absurd, parliament was forced to conduct an open inquiry; an inquiry that could hardly be described as independent. The four members on the board, including the chairman, Captain Taylor, were all members of the Legislative Assembly. It was like asking the suspects to try in which the article appeared.21

Apart from the brothel theory, there was only one other—an employee had stolen the mace, and the best possibility was Thomas Jeffrey, the House electrical engineer. Mr Jeffrey came to the inquiry with high-powered counsel, Mr David Gaunson, the best criminal lawyer in Melbourne. Perhaps he had not been successful in his defence of Ned Kelly, but he had kept Madame Brussels out of trouble. James Merrick, a cable tram gripman, testified that on the Friday the mace disappeared, between noon and one p.m., ' a man came from the rear of Parliament House carrying a large parcel five feet long, thin at one end and bulky at the other. He didn't hail the conductor and when he jumped on the dummy the parcel struck the stanchion making a metallic noise.' Merrick identified that man as Thomas Jeffrey, who denied all. He was not on the tram that day, although a few days before this he did take home a brown paper containing some wire, pieces of casing and zinc.

Practically all the evidence was inconclusive. To the horror of Melbourne the lady of the brothel was called, and she, not unnaturally, denied that the mace was at her place. Samuel Myring, a cordial manufacturer of Benalla, said that the late Mr Brock M.L.A., told him that the mace had been taken in a drunken spree to the house of a woman named Annie Wilson, and that he had seen it there. 'Mr Brock was not joking at the time,' said Mr Myring.23

There were some furious exchanges between Captain Taylor and Maurice Brodsky, editor of Table Talk, with demands that he produce the sources of his information, but little was achieved. The official report said: 'There was never the faintest ground for the paragraphs which appeared in the Ballarat Courier and Table Talk, nor for the rumours current to that effect, and that in the opinion of the Board they were only promulgated by idle talkers assisted by the desire of a certain section of the press to serve up to the public sensational paragraphs.' So the Board said it was unable to fix the guilt of the theft upon any person or persons.24

The most unsatisfactory feature of the affair was this; the police had made a report on their inquiries, but this was kept secret, and not a line of it was ever made public. And as Table Talk said after the inquiry: 'None of the witnesses stated what really happened after Parliament had adjourned in the small hours of 9 October 1891, and Captain Taylor was careful not to ask questions which might have elicited information on the matter. A revel might have been held, and the mace might have been carried off in a frolic for all the evidence to the contrary.'25

Parliament carried on for a time using the original mace which had been ordered back in 1857, and which pre-dated the mace that was stolen. Parliament ordered a new mace in 1901, but what happened to the mace which went astray on 9 October 1891, is still unknown, or perhaps has never been revealed. Annie Wilson, for one, did not leave any memoirs.

The attacks on the brothels came in sudden bursts, but mostly it was only the weak establishments that were hurt. At the turn of the century, three remained in Lonsdale Street, in particular, that of Madame Brussels, who had been in business at least since 1876. She was frequently in Court but she hardly ever suffered. On one occasion, when she was being sued by an unfortunate grazier who had lost his all at 32-34 Lonsdale Street, there was an excellent contemporary description of her. She arrived with her first officer, Martha Atkinson, and a team of her young ladies.

Madame certainly was quietly attired. The spectacled, middle-aged woman with her reticule on her arm, and her fan languidly waving, had more the appearance of a benevolent mid-wife than the keeper of a notorious brothel. And her lieutenant, Martha Atkinson, looked as if she might have just come from a love feast in connection with the Wesleyan conference, so simple was her attire and demure her demeanour. But the rest were bobby dazzlers. Their millinery left nothing to be desired—except immunity from payment of the bill. Their rustling silks and scintillating satins had been made up by past mistresses of the sartorial art.26

In 1898 there was a drive to clear all the prostitutes. Only three establishments survived in Lonsdale Street—those of Madame Brussels, Maud Miller and May Baker—and they were all charged with occupying houses frequented by women of no lawful means of support. They were ably defended by Mr Gaunson and others. First the magistrates dismissed the case. The police brought the case before a Judge, who held there was evidence for a conviction and ordered it to be reviewed by the magistrates. When it came back to the magistrates, the Chief Magistrate, the Mayor of Melbourne, Malcolm McEacharn was on the bench, and he ordered imprisonment of the ladies until 'the rising of the court'. This meant, of course, that when His Worship went off home to lunch, they were free to go too.

Cr McEacharn said he wanted to make it clear that there was no point in turning these women out of one area where they were well known, for they would only go to some other district where they would cause a great deal more harm.

In 1908 there was a further clean-up under a new Vagrancy Act, and the last of the Lonsdale Street houses went forever. Madame Brussels was closed down. However it did not affect Madame B. In July, maiden name Caroline Baum, married name, Mrs Jacob Pohl, her obituary notice appeared. The notice was very small, although it did mention she was survived by a daughter, who had been brought up respectably, and thoroughly out of sight of Lonsdale Street.28

But what of her husband? Her first husband died many years before. Caroline Baum was born in Germany and so was her second husband, Jacob Pohl. They married in 1896 but it was an odd liaison. She was forty-five, he was twenty-seven, they lived 'at home' in Lonsdale Street, and it lasted only ten months. In the Divorce Court in 1906, a witness told what happened when he served the divorce papers on Jacob Pohl:

Counsel: 'What did he say?'

Witness: 'He said, "Come and have a drink old man. That is the best news I have heard for a long time. She's too old for me." He had a gaudily dressed young woman with him and pointing at her he added: "I want something like this."'29

The problem with prostitution always has been the difficulty of obtaining convictions. Even in 1961 the Victorian Chief Secretary Mr Rylah said:

Prostitution is not in itself an offence against our law. It is certainly immoral by any standard that a woman should habitually and indiscriminately offer her body for gain, but it is not illegal and the only offence which a prostitute commonly commits is soliciting her victims in a public place, an offence against good order. The law has not, from the dawn of history, intruded into the personal morals of men and women except in so far as public morality and order are concerned. Most communities have found that the activities of the traditional prostitute may be controlled by forcing her off the street. She then has to resort to other ruses if she is to obtain her livelihood and finds that she has to work through agents, through various call services and in certain brothels. It is here that the law steps in.30

In 1906, Albert Tucker, a policeman who had worked in the city for seventeen years, told a Royal Commission that even soliciting was hard to prove. If a girl stopped a man in the street, one still had to prove what she was after. She could be asking the time, seeking directions. Even if she did this three or four times, she could say the other men did not know. As for brothels, if the police cleaned them out, they only moved elsewhere. The girls, he said, should be under proper control, medically inspected and licensed. If a girl did not have a licence she could be stopped very smartly from soliciting in the streets. And the licence would have this effect, a girl would think twice before she applied for one. 'Now,' he said, 'they just walk straight on the town.'31

Of all the areas of social impurity, the most impure was Little Lonsdale Street, better known as 'Little Lon', and this was the haven for women for sale for nearly eighty years. Little Lon was a narrow street of small double storey and single storey buildings, an area for little factories and sweet shops, but no cigarettes or lollies were sold, it would have been absurd to ask for them. The girls used to lean against door-posts or sit on seats in doorways—they were the real lollipops for sale. Visiting evangelists, missioners, preachers, city councillors, women's Temperance workers, Salvation Army, all railed against this evil, but the houses of shame of Little Lon were as secure as the . . . one was about to say 'as the bank', but the banks of Melbourne were anything but secure in the nineties and the credit of 'Little Lon' was always very strong indeed.

Over the years the police tried everything. They hit the girls on vagrancy charges, but the girls always produced books which proved they had splendid means of support. They charged them with accosting, but the girls always pointed out that they were actually inside their doors, not even an ankle out in the street, so they were not soliciting in a public place. They tried to get them on assisting in, or keeping, a house of ill repute. But what constituted a house of ill repute? Was a place a brothel when just one girl was there, say, living in a shop? The City Council made it against the law for a landlord knowingly to permit premises to be used for vice. But then, how did the landlord know? Did the police tell him? And the court usually felt that this was insufficient.

Hugh Buggy, an Argus reporter, has written vividly of this period. He said girls used to come on the day shift, start at 8.30 a.m. and then return home with peak hour crowds in the evening. Mother never knew. She thought her little girl was working innocently at an office. One girl, he said, even kept her boyfriend under the delusion that she worked the night shift as a nurse at the Royal Melbourne Hospital.

Buggy says the end came after 1932. Sir Thomas Blamey was Chief Commissioner of Police. He picketed the shops of Little Lonsdale Street with shifts of twenty-eight police constables. 'Slowly but surely the old fortress was sapped and mined,' he wrote. 'Girls changed their accustomed shifts. They appeared at the doors in the morning before the pickets arrived. Others lingered on after midnight when the pickets had gone home. Gradually the number of girls dwindled. Shops were let as small factories. Little Lonsdale Street, an institution for 75 years, withered and died. They went to St Kilda Road and Fitzroy Street.'32

And it did go elsewhere, especially to St Kilda. Prostitution is still common in every major city in Australia. Contrary to popular belief it is not, and has not been, licensed in Queensland and Western Australia.

Prostitution may not have been legal in Queensland, but until 1959 it was condoned, and the girls even underwent regular medical inspections by courtesy and cost of the State Health Department. The doctor who did this was a famous Brisbane figure, Dr Beatrice Warner. She was forthright in her opinions and efficient, and she had a reputation for being humane and kind to the girls when they were sick or in trouble. Dr Warner ran the Venereal Diseases Clinic in William Street and the girls had to report for inspection every Wednesday morning.

In June 1968 Dr Warner told her story. She said there used to be five main brothels in the city, one in Margaret Street, another in Albert Street, another at Spring Hill, a fourth in Ernest Street and a fifth at North Quay. The establishment in Ernest Street, next to the Brisbane Mirror Company, burned down on Saturday night, 23 June 1956. The girls panicked and ran screaming into the street, and some had to be restrained from running back into the fire. There was a good reason for that, said Dr Warner. Prostitutes, because of income tax problems, did not keep money in banks, and a fortune in paper money, pushed into mattresses and hidden in recesses of wardrobes, was burned that night.

She said that thirty came regularly for inspection, and she spoke of them with some affection.

'They were honest girls, varying in character, no different from any other women.'

'Did they have any sense of shame?' one asked.

'Certainly not,' said Dr Warner. 'To them it was just a job. I remember one of them, when she got up for inspection, said "You be careful of my money box." It was amazing how quickly they became accustomed to it. No, I think very few of them were in it because this was the only way they could survive. There were plenty of jobs available in Brisbane at the time. I think there is a type of girl that has a leaning towards this type of thing. It's a terribly easy way to make a good living.'

Dr Warner was a graduate of Melbourne University, and she told of the old-style treatment of venereal disease. Before 1926 the treatment at Queen Victoria Hospital, Melbourne, was to swab the women once a week.

It did more damage than good [she said]. Some were cured, or some thought they were cured. This once a week business was ridiculous, they should have been swabbed daily or even hourly.

When I came to Brisbane V.D. cases went to the Isolation Hospital behind the Boggo Road Gaol and sometimes they stayed there for seven months. But, of course, with penicillin that changed. I used to give them a million units in one mighty charge and cleared it up in one day; a big dose for syphilis, not so large for gonorrhoea. It was easier than fixing a cold.

She agreed that the curing of V.D., with growing immunity to penicillin, was no longer so easy.

Then the girls used to bring her presents. She said:

I remember when I first started a girl tried to give me £10. I was very poor, earning only £900 a year, and I would have loved to have accepted it, but I was horrified. 'Dirt money', I thought. Then during the war they used to bring bottles of Scotch whisky, something that just wasn't seen any more. One girl dropped and smashed a bottle on the clinic steps. She was very distressed. I said 'Forget it. I can't have you bringing me whisky.' Even so she went away and came back with another bottle. Those girls could get anything. You should have seen some of these dressing gowns they wore. And some of them had very nice underclothes.

One day Dr Warner received a message from the Health Department that there would be no more inspections. No longer were the brothels condoned. In 1959, the Police Minister and Deputy Premier, Kenneth Morris, ordered the brothels to be cleaned out. Dr Beatrice Warner did not see the girls again, although they presented her with a handsome brass tray on which was etched recipes for all the well-known cocktails. She still has it.

It would be foolish to suggest that this stopped prostitution in Brisbane. In 1963 there was a Queensland Royal Commission into allegations that police condoned a call-girl racket at a city hotel. The prostitutes were still around, they just had a more charming name. Dr Warner believed that the prostitutes should be licensed. She was convinced that while she conducted her inspections the amount of V.D. was reduced in Brisbane.

The situation in Perth was similar: plenty of brothels, not legal, but condoned. The famous brothel area was Roe Street, and just as in Brisbane, the girls underwent regular inspection, but their end in 1958 was novel. Most of the houses were owned by a German female national whose property was seized during the Second World War. The houses then became the property of the Commonwealth Government, which collected the rents. When the news spread that the Commonwealth was making money out of prostitution, a particularly novel form of private enterprise, there was a scramble to sell and that was the end of Roe Street.

When the American troops arrived on Rest and Recreation leave from Vietnam in 1967 they received the advice: 'Prostitution is not legal in Australia, but this is not a worry, the girls are real friendly.'

Maybe they are friendly, but according to one newspaper, in March 1968 there were 500 prostitutes operating in Sydney and at an average of $6 a customer this was a $5 million a year industry. In 1966 there were 11,458 female arrests by members of the Sydney Vice Squad.33 In 1966 the Southern Branch of the Real Estate and Stock Institute in Victoria found it necessary to complain about prostitution in St Kilda, Melbourne, and more than 400 prostitutes and men living off their earnings were arrested in St Kilda in the first 6 months of 1966.

There have been shades of the suggestion made by Constable Albert Tucker, back in 1906. At the Liberal Party conference in Western Australia, in July 1967, there was a State move to liberalize prostitution. It came from the President of the Young Liberals, Mr A. J. Pestell, and it went through by 105 votes to 97.35 The Premier, Mr Brand, commented: 'To say that I broke out in a cold rash is an understatement.'36

Then in March 1968, the Australian Natives' Association in Victoria voted overwhelmingly to urge Federal and State Governments to license all prostitutes and houses of prostitution. The general secretary, Mr G. F. Simpson, said: 'We believe brothels are needed as a safety valve in the community. With all the New Australians coming in who can't speak English and who are used to brothels in their own country we felt we had to do something.'37

In the late sixties, perhaps the social evil was not as prevalent as it had been 100 years before, but Governments still had little idea what to do about it. Only one thing was sure, it would continue, just as it had done ever since the game was first invented.
