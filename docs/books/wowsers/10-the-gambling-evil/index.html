26 min read.

First published 1968

Surveys Australian gambling culture from the colonial era to the 1950s, centring on the saga of John Wren's illegal Collingwood Tote — its extraordinary double-walled woodyard fortress, the police occupation that failed to shut it, and the anti-gambling crusaders who tried and failed to rouse public conscience against it.

In 1956 the Reverend A. Crichton Barr said at Scots Church, Melbourne: 'Australia has the dishonour to lead the world in gambling. Australia is the gamblers' paradise, the treasure island of the reckless fellows who know there is no more gullible person than the get-rich-quick man.'

This is very strong language and Mr Crichton Barr did not produce comparative figures from sufficient countries to prove his claim, but it is true that Australians have always been mighty gamblers, always looking upon the liberty to gamble as a right, even as the American looks upon the right to bear arms. The Collingwood Tote

The founder of the Collingwood Tote was John Wren, and in many ways he was something of a wonder himself. He did not smoke, he did not drink and he had a tenacity of purpose that was awesome to behold. John Wren was born in Ballarat Street, Collingwood, in 1871. He started work wheeling firewood at a woodyard and his next job was at a boot factory. One of his favourite sayings was: 'Believe me. I knew what it was to feel the pinch in my youth. I had very little to do to feel the pitch in my reach.'

Wren worked at Whybourn's boot plant, and his future did not seem brilliant. In Collingwood they had their fathers into the factories. In New South Wales, in 1880, he was barely twenty when he received a hot tip regarding Carbine in the Melbourne Cup. He mentored Henry Valley to such a great person, because he was carrying to cook. Yet Carbine did win, and Wren backed him with, in today's money, he won £1,867, which would be at least £30,000. He then started the Collingwood Tote, which became the most notorious illegal betting house the country has seen. He rented premises at 3 Sackville Street, Collingwood. In those days the Tote was an honour, and looked a good honest Collingwood citizen from the rear.

The Worrall Affair

In 1906, Detective Sergeant David O'Donnell was forty-seven years old. Big Dave everyone called him. He had a formidable, bushy dark beard and he was formidable by nature. 'I do not cut, shuffle, or deal with betting men,' he said. 'I never made a bet in my life.' He had no doubts about his own ability as a police officer. His career in charge of the detective force on the Richmond squad had been to investigate frauds, forgeries, embezzlements and such. He had served in Egypt and one posting in Colombo.

His speciality was to clean up the gambling rings, but perhaps it was just a move from one more respectable form of gambling to another. So Big Dave did not like gamblers, and they did not like him. At the Royal Commission it was told how the Tote people kept the police under surveillance.

Surveys Australian gambling culture from the colonial era to the 1950s, centring on the saga of John Wren's illegal Collingwood Tote — its extraordinary double-walled woodyard fortress, the police occupation that failed to shut it, and the anti-gambling crusaders who tried and failed to rouse public conscience against it.
