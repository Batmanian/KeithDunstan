The Rowing Passion

24 min read.

First published 1973

Traces Australian rowing from Edward Trickett's 1876 world championship and Henry Searle's death and record-breaking funeral, through the Hanlan-Beach sculling rivalry, to Bobby Pearce, Mervyn Wood and the showmanship of Stuart Mackenzie at Henley.

Australians possess their own vast, self-contained continent, an area as large as the United States, but always they have preferred to cling to the coastline. Perhaps the early settlers were even frightened to stray too far from the lands of their origin.

The big settlements were by the rivers and landlocked harbours, so it was quite natural that the settlers should be keen about water sports. There were organized rowing events on Sydney Harbour as early as 1805. Inter-colonial rowing with fours began in 1863, and with eights in 1877. But rowing is particularly interesting because it produced Australia's first undisputed world champion, and thus the first great effusion of national euphoria, overcoming a deep feeling of antipodean, colonial inferiority. The feeling created at this time has probably never been equalled since, not even when we were almost minting gold at the 1956 Olympic Games.

The first great hero was twenty-five-year-old Edward Trickett, a quarryman, born at Greenwich, Parramatta. At six feet three and a half inches, he was the biggest sculler Sydney had ever seen, and a Pitt Street hotelier, James Punch, was so impressed with him that he decided to take him to England to tackle the world's best. The world champion was Joseph H. Sadler of Teddington, a mere five feet ten inches and at thirty-six years, quite possibly over the hill. Mr Punch saw Sadler's backers and they fixed a stake of £400 for the aquatic championship of the world which was to take place on 27 June 1876.

Back in Sydney it was agony waiting for the result, the mail steamer had to arrive at Port Adelaide from Suez, then the result had to be telegraphed to Sydney from Glenelg. It took over three weeks to get the news through. The Sydney Morning Herald reported that on the morning of 18 July the first sentence in every-body's mouth was, 'Have you heard the result?' There were crowds outside the telegraph office and more crowds at Punch's Hotel, waiting. At 2.15 p.m. the telegram came through. Trickett had won easily by four lengths. The Herald said at first people would not believe it. The news was too good to be true. It seemed incredible that a man born and bred in New South Wales should go home to England and beat the champion of the Thames. Local citizens put pale blue ribbons in their button holes, bunting went up on city buildings, and at Mr Punch's hotel the manager erected great transparencies illuminating the portraits of Trickett and James Punch, side by side.

The next restless wait was for the grand return. The hero was due to disembark from the vessel Zealandia on 8 November. 'Seldom has Sydney been in such a state of excitement,' said the Herald. The crowd began to gather at Circular Quay early and as the day progressed this developed into 'an immense throng'. All was ready. Punch's was decorated with flags, cornstalks, blue ribbons and a pair of sculls.

But the Zealandia did not arrive until the next morning, and so that everybody could see Edward Trickett it was decided that he would not come ashore until 8 p.m. By that time there were twenty-five thousand people at Circular Quay, several brass bands, and the firemen of Sydney equipped with blazing torches. The crew of the Zealandia insisted on rowing Trickett ashore themselves. The ship's boat was decorated with blue lights, and it was steered personally by the master, Captain Ferris.

The crowd was so thick that at first Trickett could not find a place to step on to Princes Stairs. Two horse-drawn gigs were waiting for Trickett and James Punch. However the crowd took away the horses because young men wanted to pull the gigs themselves. The Herald said, 'here were arms strong and willing—too glad to accord homage to Australian pluck and strength.' Every high viewing spot was filled and the crowd so thick the procession virtually had to fight its way up Pitt Street.

Such a crowd rarely, if ever, has been seen in Sydney. From the City Bank to the Victoria Theatre and in King Street from the Café de Paris to Courvoisiers, there was a dense crowd which stopped all traffic, surged like the waves of a sea and looked strange and weird-like in the colour of lights thrown from several places in the streets.

Immediately there was a public subscription for Trickett and he received a purse of £850. So he acquired his own hotel, thus starting a tradition which has continued ever since.

Trickett, the following year, was challenged by the Australian champion, Michael Rush, who had conquered him on the Clarence in 1873. Trickett used the new American sliding seat, and thrashed Rush by six lengths. This was most astonishing to Rush. He was so confident he hadn't even bothered with a sliding seat.

For his really big challenge, Trickett went back to London to meet a super confident little Canadian, Edward Hanlan. Against the six feet or more of Trickett, he was only five feet eight and a half inches and ten and a half stone. There seemed to be no cause for worry. The challenge was set for £500 and the English were a little humiliated that the world championship was to be waged in the very home of rowing with no Englishman involved.

The race took place on 15 November 1880. Trickett held his own, but he drifted further and further behind. For Hanlan it was all too easy. He was so far ahead he started playing pranks. First he rowed with one hand, then with the other. He took his cap off and waved to friends on the bank. Once he stopped

altogether and washed his face in the water. One newspaper described his pranks as risky and in bad taste. He won by three lengths, but if he wished he could have won by half a mile.

The Australian's humiliation was terrible. Trickett's life as a hotel-keeper had been too much. He was out of training and he had gone through an agonizing weight-reduction programme to meet Hanlan. There was a letter to the Sydney Morning Herald which accused Australians of being bad losers. 'The infatuated Trickett admirers are now heaping coals of fire on his head, charging him with turpitude of the basest kind.'

Something had to be done about Hanlan. Sydney produced a hefty bushman named Elias Laycock, and challenged Hanlan for £1,000 a side. The race was on the Thames in freezing weather in February 1881. Hanlan went through all his old tricks, one-armed rowing, cap-waving and he won as if Laycock were pulling an anchor. He even had one special little trick of standing on his head in the shell. He toured the world in triumph and won so well that some critics began to wonder whether Hanlan used some special, mysterious propulsive force.

Hanlan arrived in Sydney in March 1884. Earlier the Australasian had said that the doings of scullers obviously were of far greater concern than those of Bismarck and the Czar of Russia combined. Melbourne Punch referred to the whole thing as Hanlan-mania, the worship of muscle. The Governor, the Premier and the Lord Mayor all bowed before him. What would happen if a great intellect like Herbert Spencer came to Australia? Now if Herbert Spencer comes, what further can we do?

What higher patronage bestow on men of fame, Than that we freely showered on a simple rowing man Just to advertise his money-making game.

First Hanlan had another race against Elias Laycock. The Sydney Echo almost pleaded for a win. It said that to plenty of people a great artist or a great writer or preacher would never be worthy to be compared with a champion sculler, and an honest victory on the Nepean would make Laycock the most popular man in New South Wales.

The race was on 22 May 1884. Late on 21 May, Mr F. B. Suttor, M.L.A., moved in the Assembly that the House should adjourn until 7 p.m. the following day. Seeing that the Hanlan-Laycock race was tomorrow there would be no hope of getting a quorum anyway. Mr Stuart, M.L.A., was shocked:

I trust the House will not accede to the suggestion? With the enormous amount of highly important work which we have before us it would not be creditable to us as a legislative body to adjourn for the purpose of attending a boat-race. I feel sure there will be no lack of members to form a quorum.

Mr Stuart was so wrong. He was beaten twenty-one votes to sixteen and they all went to the boat-race. The Legislative Council also adjourned because it could not get a quorum.

Hanlan again made a fool of Laycock with another easy win. The situation now was desperate. There was a new champion, a man tougher than Laycock, called William Beach, a stolid, taciturn, thirty-year-old Sydney blacksmith. He had come to Australia from London when he was two years old.

Already he had beaten Laycock, but his chances on the betting market of tossing Hanlan were no better than five to one. The Sportsman described it as 'his dainty menu'. For breakfast he had two oranges, two chops, then stewed kidney and bread. For dinner he had roast mutton and roast turkey, carrots, turnips, cauliflower with butter sauce, a large slice of plum pudding, also custard pudding with champagne sauce. Then to wash it down he had a glass of stout, plus a glass of stout and champagne mixed.

The race took place on 16 August 1884. Hanlan, the little man, announced that they would row on the ebb tide. Big Bill Beach was too smart for that one. 'We'll row on the making tide,' said he. Even so Hanlan had no self-doubts, and he announced: 'I can beat that big blacksmith.'

At the start of the three and a quarter mile course on the Parramatta River, Beach took off his shirt, threw it into the river and set himself for the start. 'Why take off your shirt?', said Hanlan, 'you'll need it soon.' Beach replied: 'I'll make you take off your shirt Hanlan, you won't have time for monkey tricks today.'

Hanlan had a good start and built up an early lead, and at the half way mark he was four lengths in front. The Sydney Echo reported that amongst the crowd of a hundred thousand people along the bank there was deep despondency. The comments were: 'It's the old story.' 'He plays with him.' 'Only a procession' and the odds increased to ten to one on Hanlan. But then Beach began to close the gap and steadily he gained, and gained.

When they drew alongside each other Hanlan made a frantic effort to clash oars and foul Beach. The Sydney Echo reporter, aboard the vessel Tomki, devoted page after page to this race, and had he been describing the final stages of the Battle of Waterloo he could not have been more triumphant:

Their oars clash; and something approaching a groan is heard or felt through all the multitudes who witness it. Anger and pain are blending, when Hanlan is seen to put up his hand and Beach to become suddenly disentangled and to steal away. What will Hanlan do? He starts again; but Beach is right away. Will he overtake him? There is silence for a moment and then as the space between the boats increases, as it becomes apparent that the Australian man really wins, such a scene on board the Tomki as has rarely been witnessed. A shout first then a yell: 'Beach wins! Beach wins!' Incredible! But true. Some hats are tossed in air, and blown away no man cares whither. Boots and fists are driven through others. Men leap from the deck in a frenzy of joyful excitement, clasp hands, hug each other. But the rowers, it is no more the eagle labouring through the air which the swallow divides without effort. The eagle is ahead, and going with magnificent strength and speed; hopelessly behind comes the poor swallow, with a fluttered motion and evident symptoms of distress. Very quickly the altered condition of affairs is perceived. Ship after ship takes up the cheering. Wave upon wave, the ever gathering volume of sound rolls on! There are a hundred thousand throats straining! There is a roar as when a crowd rejoices over a great deliverance rather than as when a crowd shouts over a race well won. The cliffs echo it, the trees vibrate it, the gale lifts and rolls it down the harbour. Louder and louder:—it grows as the winner approaches the flag, which dropping, gives the signal for every steam-pipe of the fleet to give vent to such voice as that which greets the new year's birth! Upward and away goes the storm of sound over the roofs and above the steeples of the city!

To all those who have any difficulty in understanding the extravagant exultation attending that winning, it will be necessary to say; turn back the leaves of memory a year or two; remember how Sydney kept awake all one night awaiting the arrival of a certain telegram which, coming, sent a sigh and a shudder through the city such as might follow on the loss of some object long cherished and dearly beloved. Hanlan took us into captivity that day, Beach delivered us on Saturday.

Hanlan did not take his loss well. According to one report he burst into tears. He swore he had been fouled by a river vessel and demanded a re-run. On the other hand Beach was fêted on all sides as the hero of the colony. On 24 March there was a public banquet in his honour in the Town Hall, and on 29 March in Melbourne the Victorian Rowing Association entertained him at Young and Jackson's Hotel and presented him with an illuminated address. He graciously gave Melbourne an opportunity to observe his prowess. Late in the evening just as the workers were leaving their offices, alone and unchallenged he rowed down the Yarra and thousands lined the banks to watch him.

Beach raced against Hanlan once more the following March, and this time he proved the first win was no fluke by completely outclassing the Canadian. Beach was now such a celebrity that he was looked to for advice on all matters, even medical problems. For example, on 13 October 1886 an advertisement appeared in the Sportsman with a picture of Beach looking very strong, very Victorian, complete with his walrus moustache, and below was the message:

Gentlemen—Feeling unwell during my training for the second contest with Edward Hanlan of Toronto, for the championship of the world, my trainer purchased for me Warner's Safe Cure and Safe Pills, and I was agreeably astonished at the great benefit which followed their use.

—William Beach, Champion Sculler of the World.

Hanlan was desperate to get his title back and he issued challenges from all points of the globe. He tried to get Beach to row on the Thames in the winter. Frigid weather particularly suited the Toronto man, but no, Beach said it was Sydney or nowhere. So they set the date for 26 November 1887 on the Nepean River. The publicity build-up was even greater than before and Hanlan was rather more careful—no stout and champagne parties this time. The race took place in torrential rain. The conditions were appalling. Thousands of ladies had their voluminous gowns ruined. But the Sydney Morning Herald said what did this matter. You see, Beach had won, so all was life and vivacity.

All apprehended what true pleasure was, and a long lifetime of ecstacy was crowded into a single moment that Beach passed the post two lengths ahead of his opponent and was once more indisputably champion sculler of the world.

Yet this was a hard race. At the finish Beach looked very pale, and he was by far the more distressed of the rowers. He said after the race: 'Well, Hanlan, you know me now. I hope you are satisfied.' Beach promptly announced his retirement and being an extremely formal gentleman he said, 'I think I have done all that should be expected of me and I intend to retire on the laurels I have fairly and honourably won.'

But even at that Hanlan did not give up. He returned the following year and although the title was no longer at stake, he still had an ego problem to settle. Beach came out of retirement, until 1888. As George Blaikie wrote in his book Skeletons of Australia's Strange Past, Beach won so easily that had he been so inclined, he could have rowed with either hand, washed his face or perhaps even stood on his head.

Beach bequeathed his title to his trainer, Peter Kemp, who successfully defended it against all comers, including Hanlan, until the arrival of young Henry Ernest Searle, who broke all the previous records on the Parramatta, until he became the fastest sculler the world had ever seen. Trickett, Beach, Kemp, were all forgotten. Harry Searle was the new king.

After beating all possible opponents in Australia he went to London to race against another Canadian, William J. Connor, for £1,000 and the world championship. Sydney waited anxiously for the result. It came through in the early morning of 10 September 1889. The Sydney Echo brought out a special edition. Searle had won with 'ridiculous ease' by an incredible twelve lengths.

So after the race Searle sailed for home aboard the Orient liner, Austral, to collect what was by now the tradition—his Caesar's triumph. The ship stopped at Colombo where Searle went ashore and played in a cricket match.

Later he became ill and first it was thought he had sunstroke, but as the ship neared Australia it was clear he was suffering from something far more serious—he had typhoid. He went into hospital in Adelaide with the warning 'no visitors'. He made some progress and was shifted to a sanitorium in Melbourne. The newspapers printed daily health reports and he seemed to be on his way to a rapid recovery, until on 8 December they printed the real truth; the world champion was in a 'grave condition', his recovery was unlikely, and already he had made it clear he would never row again. On 10 December 1889, at the age of twenty-three, he died.

Australia was overwhelmed. Had Queen Victoria on a Royal Tour dropped dead at Circular Quay, the nation could not have been more shocked. Young Searle had been the symbol of the all-powerful, Australian super-sport. The Sydney Morning Herald said:

When men like Searle pass from us we are compelled to stop and say 'Here was a man who expressed the forces of his time—the finely trained and concentrated power which commands within the area of physical rivalry.' In that he did us honour, for he was of us, and had the strength of our soil, of our atmosphere, of ourselves, to some degree within him. And all that power is now inert and forceless 'broken all to pieces under the levin and scoria of disease.'

Melbourne Punch was mystified. It couldn't believe that this young, beautiful life had been so cruelly plucked away. 'Truly there is a mystery in life—dark, close, inscrutable. Searle the champion sculler, after crossing the world to snatch triumph, has been carried back to Sydney a corpse.' But why should such a magnificent physical specimen die like this? Punch had the answer:

The best trained athletes are notoriously the most susceptible to the contraction of diseases. Their very courses of training fine them too much. Hence it must be that this piece of pick humanity, the champion sculler, took the poison, absorbed it and now lies in his grave mourned by all the continent.

One wonders whether anyone in Australia ever had a funeral to compare with Harry Searle; it was actually a vast inter-colonial

courage, his generosity, and his devotion to his parents. Then there was a special message to be read from the Governor of Victoria and his Lady, the Earl and Countess of Hopetoun. They gave 'assurance of deep sympathy and regret at Searle's untimely death'. As the train moved off all heads were bared.

Sydney and Melbourne, antagonists over Federation and nearly every other matter, came together over this bereavement. The Sydney Morning Herald did something unprecedented—it gave Melbourne a compliment.

It is pleasing to see that Melbourne paid such a hearty if sad tribute to the memory of the sculler who came from New South Wales. The test and we shall always be grateful to the sister city for her good offices on this occasion.

As the train journeyed to Sydney there were further ceremonies all along the way. The Mayors of Benalla and Goulburn turned out to pay tribute. Then a newspaper in Sydney described the actual funeral as one of the 'most imposing and remarkable events ever witnessed in Australia'. It took place on 14 December and began from the house of Searle's trainer, Neil Matterson, at Union Street, Pyrmont. It began at 3 p.m. and took two and a half hours to reach St Andrew's Cathedral. The streets were jammed all along the route and most spectators waited several hours to see the cortège. Flags on public buildings were half mast, places of business were closed, many houses had symbols of mourning on their front doors, and thousands of people wore black ribbon or Searle's old rowing colours.

As the cortège moved through the streets bells tolled and the Young Australian Band of a hundred performers played the Dead March of Saul. The procession moved at slower than walking pace. In the place of honour were fellow scullers, Beach, Richard Green, Elias Laycock, Trickett, James Stanbury and many others. The Mayor was there, as were city aldermen and members of parliament.

Four horses drew the hearse, which was draped with white sashes intertwined with blue ribbons and the top was covered with floral wreaths.

The Sydney Morning Herald said:

Near the railway station and all through George Street to the hill as St Andrew's Cathedral, the citizens were massed in serried ranks. The spectacle was an astonishing one—Sydney never saw such a sight before, not even when the contingent departed for the Soudan. The whole space of the street from Christ Church up to the Town Hall was black with people. In addition to filling the roadway and the sidewalks, they were on the balconies and the roofs of houses, while thousands looked down upon the unusual scene from the windows of the residences on the other side of the thoroughfare.

Finally the cortège went down to Circular Quay where the coffin went aboard the steamer Thetis, which sailed to Searle's home town of Grafton. As the Thetis went off the band played In the Sweet By-and-Bye and ships in the cove were black with spectators. The Sydney Morning Herald estimated that a hundred and seventy thousand people attended the funeral.

No other sculler ever again scored such adulation, although Australia did continue to produce a line of world champions; James Stanbury, George and Charles Towns and J. Paddon. More recently the interest has switched to the amateurs—Bobby Pearce, Merv Wood and Stuart Mackenzie. Both Pearce and Wood had the early embarrassment of being barred from the Henley Royal Regatta because they were not 'gentlemen'. It was a race for amateurs and by tradition an amateur did not engage in trade. Indeed Henley's Patron, Prince Albert, had ruled that anyone who was 'by trade or employment for wages, a mechanic, artisan or labourer' was ineligible.

Harry Gordon in Young Men in a Hurry recalls the case of J. B. Kelly, the father of Princess Grace of Monaco, who was barred from competing at Henley in the Grand Challenge Sculls in 1920. Legend had it that Kelly did not make it because of his association with 'trade', but actually the committee ruled he belonged to professionalism. Kelly took this as a frightful slur on the American working man. He won the Olympic gold medal that year, and, charged with a fine Irish fury, he mailed his perspiration-soaked cap to King George V, which the poor King found a little startling. He had only ever been to Henley once.

Bobby Pearce was born in Sydney and his grandfather and father were both scullers of repute over twenty years. Bobby won two gold medals and was never defeated either as an amateur or a professional.

In 1928 on the Sloten Canal in Holland he broke the 2,000-metre Olympic record held by J. B. Kelly, and he won his gold medal with such ease that he slowed down to avoid crashing into a mother duck and her brood. He even had to 'shoo' loudly to get mother duck to move on. After this he thought he would be eligible to row in the Diamond Sculls, but the exclusive English rowing fraternity would not have him; he was a carpenter.

He returned to Sydney because of the depression. In 1930 he couldn't even get a job at his old carpentering trade, and at one stage he was reduced to picking up scraps of paper. Lord Dewar, the whisky distiller of Canada, heard about this and offered him a job as a whisky salesman. Curiously enough this made him eligible for Henley—whisky presumably was respectable—he wasn't a labourer any more. So he went to London in 1931 and won the Diamond Sculls by six lengths.

He won his second Olympic Gold medal at the 1932 Games in Los Angeles. Now there was nothing else to achieve, he had done everything, so the following year he turned professional. 'It is not the lure of the prize money that is leading me into the pro ranks,' he said. 'There is something in my blood which demands that I should enter into further competition in an attempt to attain the last remaining title open to me.' So he took on Ted Phelps of England, the reigning world professional champion. They raced on Lake Ontario and more than two hundred thousand people lined the shores.

The race was for £1,000 with a guarantee of £500 for Pearce or lose, and he took it far more seriously than he had ever done as an amateur. The race was over three miles and before he had covered four hundred yards it was all over. When Pearce finished Phelps was just a speck in the distance way behind, and he had won by a quarter of a mile. Although he had adopted Canada as his country, Pearce was now a legend in Australia—he was one of the unconquerables in the class of Phar Lap, Bradman and Walter Lindrum, and so he dominated professional rowing until he retired at the beginning of the Second World War.

Mervyn Wood's case was only slightly different from that of Bobby Pearce. He was a policeman, and a member of the New South Wales police crew that represented Australia at the 1936 Berlin Olympics. This crew would have rowed at Henley, but policemen were as non-U as artisan-labourers, so they were not eligible as gentleman-amateurs. They were just twelve months too early as the ruling changed the following year and Henley moved into the twentieth century.

However Wood did not become a top-class sculler until much later, and by the time he had a chance to compete in the London Olympics of 1948, at thirty-two, he was, perhaps, past his prime. Yet he won a gold medal that year, and a silver medal in the 1952 Olympics.

One of his best achievements was the defence of the Philadelphia Challenge Cup in Philadelphia in 1950. He raced against John Kelly, brother of Princess Grace, and son of J. B. Kelly, the hugely rich brick manufacturer, who had caused such a stir with King George back in 1920. Kelly brought up his son to be a rower, to win Olympic medals, and high on the prestigious list was the Philadelphia Cup. John Kelly owned nine very expensive, beautiful, lightweight shells. He raced in a grass-green cap and he had his own cheer squad which followed him about in a specially hired bus.

On the eve of the race the Kellys were so confident that they organized a Victory ball and J. B. Kelly gave the speech of the night. In gay terms he sympathized with Wood; it was too bad that he was going to lose, but then he would find that the trip had returned him an investment, for a wise man always made capital of his failures.

Wood, however, won the race with ease. But by 1956 he was thirty-nine and there was another man on the scene, Stuart Mackenzie, aged nineteen, six feet four inches, fourteen stone, ex-Kings School, Parramatta. He was the son of a prosperous chicken farmer, and Stuart's occupation was 'chicken-sexer'. There were plenty of jokes about that.

Harry Gordon wrote that Mackenzie was an alarmingly confident young man and he caused some disaffection both with officials and newspapermen with his talk. He told pressmen:

I was rowing in the New South Wales King's Cup crew and I got to thinking about Merv Wood. It was obvious that he had just about had it, and nobody else good was coming up. So I got out of the eight and I decided to tackle the old bloke.

And he said that if he didn't beat Wood then he could fall back on the double sculls or throw the discus. He didn't have to do either. He beat Wood easily, and won selection for the 1960 Rome Olympics. But Mackenzie did not win a gold medal there. He was beaten into second place by the Russian Vyacheslav Ivanov, while John Kelly came third to win the bronze medal.

Then Mackenzie went to London. In the modern era chicken-sexers were most acceptable at Henley and he won the Diamond Sculls in 1957, 1958, 1959, 1960, 1961 and 1962. He loved disturbing the ancient traditions of the Royal Henley Regatta and every year he created a stream of well-flavoured stories.

In 1957, before the Diamond Sculls, he had a test row against his arch-rival, Ivanov. The Russians were out on the river bank, equipped with their stop-watches, anxiously waiting to see what the Australian would do. They rowed stroke for stroke then just before the finish Mackenzie had an extraordinary lapse and slumped on his oars. Ivanov roared with delight while Mackenzie said dejectedly: 'I'm ashamed. He was too good for me—too fast.'

This gave the Russian a beautiful feeling of security and undoubtedly he eased on his training a trifle. When it came to the big race Mackenzie had his revenge on Ivanov for that gold medal—he won by four feet. He told the press:

I played Ivanov at chess before we raced and I beat him easily. I knew then that I would lick him on the river because he hasn't a logical or methodical mind.

In 1958, at the Marlow regatta on the Thames, he won the single sculls with considerable aplomb, but a launch full of spectators made it clear that they did not like him very much, giving him a chorus of boos and jeers. Not long afterwards Mackenzie reappeared on the river in a light skiff. He rowed up to the launch and as he drew level he smacked his oar hard on the water, spraying water over everyone aboard. One red-faced gentleman in the reserved enclosure shook his fist at Mackenzie, 'You bounder, Sir,' he shouted.

River bank spectators thought the whole affair a little questionable and some of them shouted: 'Play the game Mackenzie' and 'Go home cobber'. Mackenzie yelled back: 'You taught it to me.'

He was always in trouble, in trouble for rowing to the start of a race in a track suit, in trouble for saying 'Good on yer Dig' to an Earl. At the Rome Olympics he drove officials, pressmen and the general public to desperation by not making up his mind whether or not to row until almost the last minute. In the end he decided 'No', he was suffering from ulcer trouble.

Some loved him, some loathed him for being an exhibitionist. Those who loved him thought that here was a man who put a blast of clean air through the appalling stuffiness of British rowing. Simply, Mackenzie was the dominating sculler in the long history of the Henley Royal Regatta and as the Herald put it: 'The whole trouble is that he keeps on winning.' Australia has not produced his like since, and even Edward Hanlan would have been impressed.
