Introduction

3 min read.

First published 1973

Explains that 'Sports' grew out of a 1969 La Trobe Library exhibition on Victoria's sporting past, and sets out Dunstan's aim to show that sport, not war or politics, is the true key to understanding the Australian character.

Australia is not the only country that is sport crazy. The Canadians have their passion for ice hockey, the Japanese and the Americans adore baseball, the South Americans are frenzied over soccer and the passion in England for the horse runs deep, but few countries have such a complete all-round sport mania as Australia.

Few countries have such a consuming determination to conquer at sport; and what other country devotes so much energy to thinking, talking about and living sport? Our television almost exists through sport and the sporting sections of our newspapers are the biggest in the world, particularly during the Melburnian winter, sport seems to swamp and stifle all other creative activities.

I do not hate sport, indeed I am as passionately fond of it as most Australians, but the purpose of this book is to show that sport is a national obsession.

The inspiration for it came from an exhibition entitled 'Sporting Life in Victoria', staged in the autumn of 1969 at the La Trobe Library in Melbourne. This exhibition was beautifully researched and presented, and I was fascinated to note that the Australian passion was not something which began in the 1940s or the 1950s, but that even a hundred years ago our tiny population was producing some of the world's best sporting crowds.

I decided to follow the thoughts of the exhibition a little further. So that the book would not become too unwieldy I had no chapters on golf, bowls, or even that old-time gentlemanly passion beloved by Prince Philip, polo. There had to be deletions, but if one wants to study golf, bowls, surfing, tennis, swimming and other sports like cricket, football, boxing, athletics, cycling and racing, the sports of the majority, I believe I have not written about with the aim of piling historical fact upon historical act. No, I have tried to illustrate great moments of passion, such as the deaths of Harry Searle, the boxer, just to illustrate that followed the departure of Carbine, or the emotional scenes mean deeply moved—and I have never have we been so moved by anything else. Never have we been so moved by our work, our music or our literature, and I mean perhaps the passion we have spent on sport explains the dull history of our politics.

Sport as an activity has been incredibly under-rated by our historians and in most important works it is barely mentioned. Perhaps I have hardly bruised the surface in this book, but if one wants to understand Australians, I believe it is important to study their age-old mania for games. This mania does not necessarily mean playing them—absurdly most Australians over the age of thirty prefer to stand by and look on.
