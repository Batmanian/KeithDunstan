The Swimming Passion

28 min read.

First published 1973

Traces the rise of Australian swimming from the invention of the crawl and Fanny Durack's fight to compete at the 1912 Olympics, through Andrew 'Boy' Charlton's teenage duel with Arne Borg, to the punishing modern training regimes behind Dawn Fraser, Murray Rose and Shane Gould.

Do Australians really have webbed feet? Until the First World War cricket, rowing and prize fighting were the prime methods by which Australians struggled to get noticed. Then came the other sports—the tennis racket wielders and the swimmers. At tennis they were good enough, but swimming they tackled with such single-minded determination, an outsider could be forgiven for thinking they invented the pastime.

Even that is partly true. They have had a profound effect on modern speed swimming, particularly with the invention of the Australian crawl. Forbes Carlile, the great modern coach, has done much research into the origin of the stroke. In the early days, when swimmers began to raise their arms out of the water, it seemed very odd. They believed a stroke that called for a massive output of energy was good for sprinting, but for nothing else. A swimmer would be gravely unwise to attempt to sustain it for more than a hundred yards. Swimmers had yet to perfect breathing systems and they tended to bore through the water, head down, taking only periodic huge gulps of air. Forbes Carlile gives two possible origins for the name 'crawl'.

In 1898 Alick Wickham, whose father had a schooner trading in the Solomon Islands, came to Sydney for his schooling. He was keen on swimming and he entered the 66 yards under-ten handicap at the Bronte pool. Not only did he have a remarkable speed but he also had a remarkable over-arm style. George Farmer, a swimming coach of the day, said excitedly: 'Look at this kid crawling'.

Perhaps this was the origin of the crawl. On the other hand there was Dick Cavill, who won the New South Wales championship on 31 December 1899. He was fifteen years old and he had a spectacular style—head down, flailing arms. Later he was known in England as Splash Dick—one saw a lot of splash and very little Cavill. At least until the twenties lanes were unknown, and Splash Dick tended to wander like a rudder. So much so that a rival competitor complained that young Cavill was 'crawling all over me'.

There were six famous Cavill brothers, the sons of Fred Cavill, himself a great swimmer. He came to Australia in 1879 and established swimming baths at Lavender Bay in Sydney Harbour. The New South Wales Swimming Association in its report for 1905 stated that Dick's brother, Arthur 'Tums' Cavill, was the first Australian swimmer to race using the Australian crawl. Later he froze to death trying to swim across Seattle Harbour in 1914.

So many names could be mentioned. There was Ted Lane, Australia's first swimming gold medal winner. He won the 200 metres freestyle at the Paris Olympics in 1900. Time: 2 minutes 25.2 seconds. He used the double over-arm style, the fore-runner of the two-beat crawl. Fanny Durack, a great exponent of the two-beat crawl, was our next gold medal winner, but she had to overcome far greater obstacles than mere rivals in the water.

During the Edwardian era, most councils considered it proper to check this new swimming craze by segregating the sexes. There was something frightening in the idea of male and female frolicking in the water together, legs and arms totally bare; heaven knows what might transpire. For example, at Williamstown in 1912 there was a division down the centre of the beach. The girls had to bathe on the western side and boys on the eastern side.

At that time ladies wore complex neck to knee swimming costumes. Those used for racing were far too immodest for the general gaze. The Ladies Amateur Swimming Association of New South Wales controlled women's swimming, and they had a firm rule; members were forbidden to compete in the same carnivals as men or even to appear if men were among the spectators.

Fanny Durack, the twenty-one-year-old daughter of a Sydney hotel-keeper, was recording such extraordinary times that she was an obvious choice for the 1912 Olympic Games in Stockholm. The officials of the Olympic selection committee had a problem—how could they see her swim, how could the general public anywhere see her swim, if she was forbidden to perform before males. An angry battle took place involving swimming clubs all over Australia. Finally the progressives won, and the association agreed to rescind the ruling that no member might compete before a male audience.

The president of the Ladies Association, Miss Rose Scott, was so ashamed of the association's immodest conduct, that she resigned her post and walked out of the room the moment the voting was announced.

The male officials were not keen on sending a woman swimmer anyway. There was only one event open to women swimmers at Stockholm, the 100 metres freestyle, possibly one too many. So the ladies started their own fund. Mrs Hugh D. McIntosh, wife of the famous Sydney entrepreneur, the man who staged the first world title fights in Australia, launched the fund. She gave five guineas, and money came in from all over the country. So two girls went, Fanny Durack and her greatest rival, Mina Wylie.

Fanny Durack did not let the ladies down. She set a new world record, clipping it by nearly seven seconds. This race was in fresh water, a long straight course with no lanes and no turns. Mina Wylie triumphed also. She started badly and was swimming last with only twenty metres to go, then she let loose an amazing finish to swim into second place.

But maybe the first of the nationally famous swimming heroes was Frank Beaurepaire. In 1908 he was the Victorian champion at all distances from 300 yards to 880. In the national championships of that year he won the 440, the 880 and the mile. He was the logical man to go to London for the Olympic Games.

In 1908 there was no co-ordinated Olympic committee for sending athletes to the Games. The Beaurepaire enthusiasts did set up an organization, but the swimming season had finished. Football was under way, and a Melburnian public never likes anything to interfere with its most serious passion. The response to the fund can only be described as fragile, and in the end most of the money came from Sol Green, the famous bookmaker and horse owner.

Frank Beaurepaire was only seventeen and he must have been the poorest Olympic candidate ever to leave Australia. He went at bottom rates, steerage, and with only £16 to spend. Sol Green made a special trip to the wharf to see the captain so that Beaurepaire could be let out of steerage captivity and train on the first class promenade deck.

He arrived in London on 16 May 1908, with little money and a third-class return ticket. Nobody was there to meet him and for a month he was too shy to look up any swimming officials. So he stayed with relatives, and as the City Baths were too expensive, he trained at Highgate Ponds. Here the water, even at the height of summer, was only 57°F.

Having no trainer and no advice, he chose as his first competitive start, the 15-mile swim in the Thames. J. M. Rowan in his story on Frank Beaurepaire quotes a London description of the race:

The stroke used by the Australian looked so exhausting that every moment one expected to see him give up, but no, mile after mile he struggled on. He led at Kew Bridge, but he strove like a man struggling against fate. Just before Hammersmith Bridge after some miles of superhuman effort the inevitable came—the effort was too much for mortal man, suddenly and dramatically he collapsed.

He swam blindly in circles—he was helplessly beaten and sinking. The attendant sprang from the boat fully clothed and rescued the drowning man and Jarvis went on to win in 3 hours 36 minutes and 24 seconds.

The few who had backed him in Australia did not receive the news of this event with much enthusiasm. Beaurepaire did not wish to be caught that way again. Next he tackled the 220 yards in the English national championships at Nottingham. He told the curator at the Baths what he thought of English conditions—that they were far too cold for competitive swimming and he wished something could be done about it.

The curator was very kind and he heated the water to 82°F. There was so much steam rising in the cold air that the spectators could not see the racing, and the competitors were in a state of collapse from the heat. It was fine for Beaurepaire, he won in 2 minutes 37¾ seconds.

In the Olympic Games he came second to the English champion, Henry Taylor, in the 400 metres. Taylor's time was 5 minutes 36¾ seconds, eight seconds slower than Beaurepaire's best. This was hardly surprising, considering the pool. It was just a hole dug in the athletic arena. There was no filtration and no chlorination. It was impossible to see more than six inches ahead in the mulligatawny water, impossible to see one's rivals. Otto Schiff of Germany protested that Beaurepaire bored him in the semi-final. Taylor also won the 1500 metres and Beaurepaire was third.

By this time Beaurepaire was acclimatized and he was just beginning to find form. Until now Taylor had been the champion, but no longer. Beaurepaire beat him wherever they met in Europe. So the seventeen-year-old returned moderately triumphant. He went back to Wesley College, and two years later his backers were keen to send him to an intermediate Olympic Games which were to take place in Athens. The Chief Justice, Sir John Madden, became president of the appeal committee, the Lord Mayor was the chairman, and there was a public meeting at the Town Hall to get the appeal under way.

Because of political problems, the 1910 Athens Games never took place, but as the money had been raised the committee agreed to send Beaurepaire on tour anyway. This time the young man, at nineteen, was elevated to a second-class ticket, and it was the greatest tour he ever made. He had forty-one starts, forty-one wins, set world records at a complete range of distances, won fourteen medals, five cups and fifteen other prizes. He returned home to an extraordinary reception at the Melbourne Town Hall in the presence of such dignitaries as the acting Prime Minister, Mr Billy Hughes, the State Premier, Sir John Murray, and the Lord Mayor Councillor Davey.

The Mayor, in proposing Frank Beaurepaire's health, said there must be something in the climate of Australia to produce such an extraordinary variety of champions, there were Madame Melba, Ada Crossley, Victor Trumper, Alfred Deakin and Carbine. Frank Beaurepaire was like Alexander the Great—he had no more worlds to conquer.

Then the Victorian Government did something unprecedented. Sir John Murray decided that the Beaurepaire tour had been of such publicity value to Victoria, he should receive a special publicity grant. Cabinet met and agreed to give him £250, the equivalent of $2,000 to $3,000 in the inflated 1970s. The grant caused a stir in sporting circles. London Truth in its issue of 11 January 1911 wrote:

The grant of £250 by the Victorian Government to F. Beaurepaire is an incontrovertible abuse of the power of expending Government money. How can we expect to maintain our amateur standard if our swimmers are asked to compete against amateurs who are treated in a preferential manner?

That summer Beaurepaire went to Sydney for the national championships. During the third lap he began to flap his arms and was obviously in an exhausted condition. Tom Sealey, who was manager of the Victorian team, tried to lift him out of the water. Beaurepaire fought to go on, but he was on the point of collapse and he had to retire. Sporting heroes must never lose face. A section of the crowd hooted him and shouted 'You mug'.

Next day there was a letter to the newspapers:

I have just returned from the municipal baths and I feel sad. I don't know Beaurepaire from a crow, but I do know he went to Europe and I presume in his travels he has heard the cheers of thousands of foreigners as he touched the end a winner . . . God knows what he must have thought tonight when he heard the yells, hoots, groans and cries of 'You Mug' from thousands of his alleged fellow countrymen.

Doctors advised Frank Beaurepaire to retire for the rest of the season. He did more than that, he retired until 1920, but then he made one of the most remarkable come-backs in the history of sport. In 1920 the champion American swimmer, Norman Ross, was to come to Australia. The war produced no new champions, so the Victorian Amateur Swimming Association persuaded Frank Beaurepaire to come out of retirement. There was a crowd of four thousand at the old St Kilda Baths to see the race over 800 yards. They were level for lap after lap. Then at the last stretch Beaurepaire surged ahead to win by 31 seconds.

He was third in the 1,500 metres at the 1920 Olympic Games in Antwerp and third again at the 1924 Games in Paris, but there was a new and more dangerous rival around, Andrew 'Boy' Charlton, the fifteen-year-old prodigy from Manly. Charlton, like Beaurepaire, used the old-fashion trugeon stroke with an ineffective scissors kick, but he had enormous power in his shoulders.

At the opening of the 1923-4 season the Manly Club asked the Melbourne Club to take part in a series of contests. Beaurepaire had not even started serious training and he was a veteran of thirty-three, in modern swimming terms practically a great-grandfather. Before the race Beaurepaire went to the Spit to have a private trial. This he thought was a very secret affair, but by the time he arrived at the Manly Baths everyone knew how good his trial had been. The old journals described it as a 'never-to-be-forgotten' race. It was over 440 yards and Charlton won it by two yards in the good time of 5 minutes 20¾ seconds.

A few weeks later Arne Borg of Sweden, twenty-two, middle and long distance champion of the world, arrived in Australia. He was tall, blond, bespectacled, rather buck-toothed and he was known as the Electric Flash.

The best man Australia could put up against him was the 'Boy' now aged sixteen, Andrew Charlton, the Manly Flying Fish. The first race was to take place over 440 yards at the Manly Pool on 12 January 1924. Borg was calm about it. 'I expect to beat Charlton easily between 15 and 20 yards. I feel well and could not be in better condition, but will not extend myself unless necessary. I could win the race in 5 minutes 8 seconds but do not expect to swim it faster than 5 minutes 16 seconds, which should enable me to beat Charlton.' When asked if he would attempt to break his world record, Borg said, 'No'.

Borg started well, but after the first ten yards the swimmers were only inches apart, and then Charlton never let him get away. At 220 yards Charlton made a beautiful turn, caught Borg and then they swam stroke for stroke amidst the incredible screaming of the crowd at the old Sydney Domain Pool. At 320 yards Charlton drew ahead, turned four feet ahead of Borg, who again made a great effort, but Charlton went on even faster and finished the winner by twenty yards. The time was 5 minutes 11¾ seconds, and it equalled the world record set by Borg in Stockholm in 1922. Borg graciously put his hand on Charlton's head and called for three cheers.

Sydney's Sunday News described the race in similar terms of joy to the Relief of Mafeking.

Five minutes eleven and fourth fifths of glorious excitement. Another five minutes of joyous abandon. And then Australia has found an idol, an idol such as the world's millions seek—and find—on history's centuries but seldom.

Andrew 'Boy' Charlton, a mere college boy of sixteen summers, an unassuming youth, fulfils the confidence his six million countrymen had placed in him—he shows himself a world champion. This handsome youth, his well-knit frame, endowed with a seemingly inexhaustible supply of rugged stamina, gave us yesterday a day that will go down as perhaps the most memorable in Australia's natatorial history.

The scenes were such as have never been witnessed before at any of our swimming functions. The official estimate of the crowd was 6,500—a bigger gate-money than an Australian swimming function ever previously recorded. Many times this number were unable to gain admission.

The Sunday News reported that after the race Charlton was surrounded by a crush of people, faces all beaming with joy. Two old ladies tried in vain to kiss him. A flapper did it with ease and grace. 'Who's he?' a man with golf sticks asked. 'What?' almost shrieked a lad, according to the News, 'What? Don't you know Boy Charlton? . . . Where you from?'

Five days later Charlton beat Borg again over 220 yards and this was pure ecstasy, the cheering and din at the pool was even greater than after the 440. Still only sixteen Charlton went to the Paris Olympics. He finished third to Johnny Weissmuller in the 400 metres, but he won the 1,500 metres by nearly a lap, and took 34.8 seconds off the world record. Arne Borg was second and Frank Beaurepaire third.

Despite many pleas, Melbourne did not see Boy Charlton until 1928 when he came to a fund raising night at the City Baths for the Amsterdam Olympics. The Herald reported that they really needed to take the roof off to accommodate the crowd . . . .

For nearly five minutes on Saturday night Boy Charlton, the Manly swimmer, stood with bowed head on the starting platform at the City Baths while Melbourne greeted him with a tumultuous outburst of applause. Never in the history of sport in this State has an athlete received such an overwhelming reception. When at last the applause subsided, the young swimming giant, he is now 21, raised his head, unfolded an immense pair of bronze-like arms, and whispered rather than said, 'I thank you.'

Training methods were totally different in 1928. Charlton worked on his sheep property and swam only a few months each summer, perhaps a mile or two each day, to get himself in shape. Borg on the other hand had been upset by Australian defeats and worked hard at his training. So at Amsterdam he reversed the decision and beat Boy Charlton in the 1,500 metres. Weissmuller was still all-powerful in the sprints.

Boy Charlton won his Olympic gold medal in 1924. John Davies won the 200 metres breaststroke. This was the last time competitors could use the butterfly in the breaststroke and Davies, believing he could maintain the same even pace over four laps, won in the last few yards.

After the Olympic Games of 1952 came 'the great revival'. Australia went on to win fourteen gold medals at the 1956 Melbourne Games, when Australian swimmers won every free-style event and took the first three places in both men and women's 100-metre sprints.

Of course, preparation for the big revival began much earlier than 1952. Much of the credit is due to Professor Frank Cotton, who was Professor of Physiology at the University of Sydney. It is an interesting example of Australia's determination to succeed in sport. Professor Cotton put swimmers through heart, lung and blood tests, kept a check of their pulse rates, and made them keep a log of all their swimming. Professor Cotton died in 1955 and so missed the Melbourne triumph of 1956, but planning for it started as early as 1947.

Forbes Carlile was a physiology lecturer with Professor Cotton and he says they were the first to bring science to swimming. They took the heart rate of swimmers after every training swim, and they took the blood count. They made the young swimmers train harder than any swimmers had ever trained and not just in the summer, but all the year round. They even gave them hot baths before competition. They installed a bath at the North Sydney Olympic Pool and heated the water to 110°F. with an old immersion heater taken from the Sydney University. Some said they would turn the swimmers into blobs of jelly giving them this treatment. But Carlile said: 'Would you race a car with a cold engine?'

They tried all sorts of experiments, even hypnotism. Carlile does not talk enthusiastically about this one any more. He believed, back in 1954, that perhaps hypnotherapy would relax swimmers, relieve them of their worries, and enable them to reach their full physical capacity. He would put them into a deep sleep before a race. There was the famous example of Gary Winram who competed in the national 1,650-yard title in 1956. Carlile gave Winram the hypnotic suggestion that he was being chased by a shark. Undoubtedly the shark was not enough because Winram only came second.

Apart from Carlile there were other equally determined coaches—Harry Gallagher, Frank Guthrie, Sam Herford.

Dawn Fraser in Gold Medal Girl tells of her training. She did seven or eight miles every day and in ten years she estimates she swam ten thousand miles. Sometimes she would swim with her legs tied, sometimes she would haul an open petrol drum through the water. She would do a hundred push-ups at a time. Back in the days when she held all the world records from 50 yards to a mile she did two training sessions daily of half a mile apiece.

But there has to be determination. Ken Knox in The Dawn of Swimming said this of Dawn Fraser:

She is a cruel vicious swimmer. She attacks without mercy from the start and keeps up that same relentless attack right through her races. She is a grim, unsmiling destroyer in the water. She is not happy just to win races. She likes to win and humble her opponents. She seems at her happiest in a race when she has completely crushed the opposition and ground them into mediocrity.

But perhaps the most revealing quote comes from Jon Henricks, who won two gold medals in the 1956 Olympics. This was his secret of success:

You see that goddam pool there—well, if you want to get to the top in swimming you stand at one end of it, dive in and start swimming. You do that for three, four or five years and every time you stop swimming your coach bawls you out. You do fast laps and slow laps, you do laps on the kicking board and laps with your feet tied together. You do laps up and down, and up and down until you lose count, and then you start all over again. You go to sleep thinking of the number of miles you've got to swim. You wake up thinking of these tropical waters, but you've got a crazy ear disease from your head shaved to make you look like a zombie so that you will cut down your resistance, to a party and you write back regretting you are unable to attend owing to a prior engagement. That's a lie, of course, the only prior engagement is the pool, going up and down, up and down, then up and down again. You finish going up and down and it's time to do some weight lifting—or maybe go to sleep while your coach goes out playing golf or fishing.

John Henricks' father built a swimming pool right by the bed-room, so that Jon could save an hour's travel every day. However, according to Ken Knox, the coach did not think it a good idea. It destroyed Henrick's fanatical desire to get to the pool to work. Said Harry Gallagher:

A swimmer must be mentally tough as well as physically tough. I never like to see my pupils getting things too easily. The swimmer who has to battle his way to the pool on crowded trams and trains, or on a bicycle, or walk, will battle out a tight finish much better than someone who arrives at the pool in a chauffeur driven Rolls-Royce.

Unquestionably the most intriguing example of the effort involved in creating an Olympic champion was Murray Rose, who won three gold medals at the Melbourne Olympics and one at Rome. Rose, tall, blond, existed on health foods, was almost the copy-book dream of the muscle-building young man. To Australians who had always been brought up on the theory that the only true muscle-building diet was the national dish, steak and eggs, this was a remarkable heresy. Australian boarding schools invariably put their top crews on special steak bicep-building diets. Half raw meat was supposed to build stamina, fire and manliness. Murray Rose never tasted meat or poultry in his life and he became known as the Seaweed Streak. The newspapers even printed Rose diets and Rose recipes. This was one of his daily menus:

BREAKFAST Bowl of sliced pineapple. Bowl of grapes. Irish moss (seaweed) jelly. Goat's milk junket.

LUNCH Large raw salad (including raw carrot, raw beetroot etc.) Egg or cheese dish. Wholemeal pancake.

DINNER Cooked vegetables. Pepper stuffed with soya beans. Millet pudding.

When Murray Rose was training for the 1956 Olympic Games, he went north to Townsville for winter training with the Australian swimming team. His mother took a flat there and cooked all his foods. Supplies were not easy to find there. She had to hunt the district for the goat's milk, and supplies of seaweed and freshly ground sunflower and sesame seeds had to be flown in from the south. Her special millet bread was available at only one shop in Sydney and the cost of getting it to Townsville was seven shillings a loaf.

Mrs Rose had similar problems when the team moved to Melbourne. She still had to prepare his food and she sent it into the Olympic Village on trays. Ian Rose, Murray's father, said it cost $1,600 to get his son to the starting blocks for the Melbourne Games.

Rose went to the United States after the Games. Stanford and Yale universities fought to get his services. Stanford pointed out that the Pacific Ocean kelp they could provide was superior to the second-rate stuff that Yale would get from the Atlantic. Yale fought back and said that they would import the utterly superior carrageen from Ireland, the caviar of kelp.

Rose did not go to either University. He did an arts course at the University of Southern California in Los Angeles. Meanwhile, his father went into an advertising business in New York. His mother then had to travel back and forth to Los Angeles to supervise the diet.

Rose returned to Australia to train for the 1960 Olympics. Once again there was the trail up to Townsville for winter training, once again Mrs Rose had to take a flat nearby. Then, when the Australian team flew off for the Olympic Games both Mr and Mrs Rose were there waiting, a flat arranged in Rome. This time Mr Ian Rose estimated that it cost $8,000 to get his son to the starting blocks.

In 1962 the Sun in Sydney and Speedo Knitting Mills paid his fare back to Australia to compete in the Commonwealth Games at Perth. He won four gold medals, but in 1964 for the Tokyo Games it was a different story. Under Australian Swimming Union Rules every swimmer had to compete in trials in Australia to win selection.

Rose did not do this. He believed his form was good enough to go direct to Tokyo from America and to prove his point, swimming in Los Angeles, he broke the world record for the 1,500 metres. The A.S.U. remained firm, but the Rose case became the greatest cause célèbre since Keith Miller failed to gain selection in the 1949 Test cricket side for South Africa. The Chief Justice of New South Wales, Mr Justice Herron, who was also President of the New South Wales Swimming Association, fought for Rose in Sydney. Mr Nixon, Country Party member for Victoria, appealed to the Prime Minister, Sir Robert Menzies, and asked him in the House of Representatives to intervene on Rose's behalf. The newspapers printed leading article after leading article. The Herald in Melbourne was seldom so stirred and it produced three. In one it said:

This is a strange case of throwing away a winning chance. The episode seems to confirm that Australia, while producing some of the world's leading sports contestants, also produces some of the world's oddest administrators.

But it was all to no avail. Rose did not swim in Tokyo. He went there for an American firm as a sports commentator. Yet there was one compensation, as a veteran competitor he had broken a world record at twenty-five years. Most other records were being broken by thirteen and fifteen-year-olds. This was a comfort to those middle-aged people in their late twenties. There was still hope.

Actually it is the sport which produces the youngest heroes. In 1956 Ilsa Konrads, aged thirteen, held two world records and her brother John at fifteen, held six. But another fine example of parental dedication in training the young swimmer is Shane Gould. Shane was winning her first major races at nine years and at fifteen she won three gold medals at the Munich Olympics and she was acclaimed the greatest woman swimmer the world had seen.

Her mother, Mrs Shirley Gould, has described how it is done. Her book Swimming the Shane Gould Way tells of Shane's practical arrangements of training:

Night before: 1 check school bag. 2 check on school clothes. Clean shoes. 3 Mix container of liquid food. 4 Have a dog biscuit available. 5. Set alarm for 4.20 a.m.

Morning: 1 Take heart rate (count pulse throbs for 30 seconds; double this for HR per minute). 2 Make bed. 3 Quiet dog with biscuit. 4 Drive with coach or parent to pool by 4.45 a.m., and swim under supervision until 7 a.m. 5 Home with father for breakfast, leave on school bus at 8 a.m.

Shane Gould would swim at least three and a half miles a day and up to forty-six miles a week. On some days she would do four hours in the water—two hours in the morning and two at night. She would go to bed at 8 p.m. so that she could be ready for that 4.20 a.m. start.

Mrs Gould wrote:

If the race is an important one for her then she is pretty unsociable. She tries to get her thoughts concentrated on what she is about to do. She ignores people making light chatter, and is very easily irritated by people asking questions. Parents especially will offend her at this time if they ask 'Have you got your socks on?' or some other foolish irrelevancy. At this time she is thoroughly anti-social, completely unable to cheer up a friend who has had a disappointing swim and barely polite to any breathless little autograph hunter.

Yet to really get an idea of the power behind Australian swimming it is important to talk to Forbes Carlile and his wife Ursula. They control Australia's greatest swimming factory and Shane Gould was one of its products. The Carliles have four indoor swimming pools on Sydney's North Shore. They have a staff of sixty, full and part-time, and a payroll of over $1,000 a week. The swimming day starts soon after 4 a.m., and at peak times there are trainers on every lane, to say nothing of anxious stop-watch, poolside parents. He has two thousand boys and girls who train at his pools, keen to be champion swimmers. While in Sydney altogether there could be up to eight thousand.

At Pymble there are hand-lettered signs on the boards to urge on the faithful:

COMPETITIVE SWIMMERS TAKE NOTE

Success seems to be in direct proportion to how intensely your mind can be focused on that which you have to do both before you do it and whilst you are doing it. Most swimmers go along with minimum mental effort . . . They lack purpose.

Do the right thing—what you are told in the training—or stay away.

Miles swum regularly make champions. You build strength and speed while you are fairly fresh. You build endurance by keeping going while you are tired.

There are blackboards too with all the trainees listed—Jill, Betty, Joan, Shirley, Bill, Roger and Alan, detailing the countless miles they have covered, and the charts look like the log books of buses or airliners.

Forbes Carlile competed in the Pentathlon at the Helsinki Olympics and he is still a very lean, athletic looking man with a crew cut, like a U.S. tennis player of the 1940s.

He says North Sydney is the swimming capital because there are more swimming pools per square mile on the affluent North Shore than anywhere else in Australia. Swimming is especially popular with ambitious parents—it is a status symbol to have a child in the school swimming team.

He explained the progressive agony involved in the creation of a champion. Boy Charlton in the 1920s would have done ten miles a week, the swimmers of the 'forties did maybe fifteen to twenty miles, now in the 1970s they do fifty miles.

You see, in the old days a swimmer would get tired and the coach would believe it was a mistake to push him any further. Now the swimmer goes on until he is paralysed with tiredness. Yes, at first there is a falling off of performance because of this, but then the body adapts, gets stronger and is able to cope. It doesn't matter whether they are sprinters or long distance swimmers—they put in the miles.

As for age, if a girl is going to make it she has to be approaching world class by the time she is 13. She will only improve marginally after that. For a boy, he must get there by the time he is 17.

There comes a time when they get fed up, weary of boring up and down, up and down, when they start thinking of the opposite sex, when they see school mates having a good time, eating what they want to eat, going to parties, playing records, sleeping in until lunch time.

We lose a lot of them at this stage. I know many of them who could have gone on to be great champions, but it's not a tragedy really. There has to be a weeding out and unless you have the determination you can't be a champion.

Forbes Carlile insists further that the dedication and discipline is good all round.

I find generally that the top swimmers do better at school. If you have the self discipline to swim then you have the self discipline to do your school work. Shane Gould lost three months in 1972 because of the Olympics yet she got straight As in her School Certificate.

He sees the Australian swimming boom becoming bigger and rosier. Until the 1970s the boom had been practically all in Sydney. There were two major exceptions, both Victorians—Frank Beaurepaire, and John Marshall who broke twenty-nine world records in 1950-51. Now the champions were more evenly divided between Sydney and Brisbane. 'And it's amazing,' he said, 'how many pools there are in the country where kids are using the same training methods we have here. They're going to produce champions.'

One wonders whether the Olympic champions of the future will have to train harder still, put in, say, a hundred miles a week. This hardly seems possible, but Forbes Carlile has demonstrated how they can now start much younger. The Carliles have indoor pools, heated to 95°F., at Pymble, Castlecove, Ryde and Top Ryde. Here they train babies. The babies go into the water long before they can walk. At the door pamphlets are handed out which read:

It is now known that tremendous physical and mental gains in the development of the young child can be made when he is exposed in warm water to a graded exercise and learning process.

These children have a 'head' start on others, and probably stay ahead throughout life. From the age of six or eight weeks, preferably twice weekly, your baby will progress steadily in changes . . . Your child at two years will NOT be swimming the crawl stroke BUT well may be able to save his life in an emergency.

So they progress from the kindergarten classes, to the Beginners, and on to what he calls 'The Tadpoles' or 'Young Olympians'. Who knows, the twelve-year-old Olympic champion of 1985 or 1986 might have been pushed in the water by his mother at six weeks of age.

Apart from gold medals, fame, cheers from one's countrymen, the rewards are not over great. The swimmers get streets and swimming pools named after them. There is a Karen Moras Drive, the Charlton Pool, the Dawn Fraser Pool, and the Michael Wenden Pool. Dawn Fraser had a daffodil and a pink orchid named after her, she was driven in triumph down the straight on Melbourne Cup Day, she lunched with the Queen and Prince Philip and she received more than two hundred letters of proposal, 'from guys who somehow figured that because I could cover 100 metres in the water in about a minute, I was a surefire bet as a housewife'. Then in 1973 a grateful Labor Government paid all her expenses to attend a Swimming Hall of Fame ceremony in Florida, U.S.A. Shane Gould in January 1973, still only fifteen years old, became 'Australian of the Year' as the one who had done most to further the good name of Australia.

No doubt this was important enough, but on 30 September 1972, the officials of the Victorian Football League drove her around the arena at the Melbourne Cricket Ground on Grand Final Day before more than one hundred thousand people. Melburnians look upon their Grand Final as a religious rite, and normally they do not approve of interruptions to their ecclesiastical services. According to the Melbourne Herald she received a Grand Final roar of approval.

Not only was it a great honour for a young female swimmer to receive this from a Melbourne football crowd, but when one considers that she came from Sydney, the honour is almost beyond belief.
