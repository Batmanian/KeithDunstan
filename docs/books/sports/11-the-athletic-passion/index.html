The Athletic Passion

43 min read.

First published 1973

A history of professional pedestrianism and athletics in Australia, from the colonial 'peds' and the great Melbourne-versus-Ireland sprint contests of the 1860s through the careers of Bobby McDonald, Charlie Samuels, Jack Donaldson, Arthur Postle and 'Professor' William Miller, to the Olympic triumphs of Marjorie Jackson, Betty Cuthbert, Herb Elliott and the heartbreaking near-misses of Ron Clarke.

The Athletic Passion

In the old days they were called pedestrians, or more lovingly, peds. The dictionary says 'pedestrian' means 'going, performed on foot, of walking, prosaic, dull.'

Yet prior to 1914, when legs were still being used quite considerably, if one thought of a pedestrian then he was a professional runner, and this ped was rarely prosaic or dull. There were even occasions when the ped was highly acclaimed as in 1887, when the popular Sydney journal Town and Country said rapturously:

If a trial between two horses be a noble sight to witness, surely how much more noble must a trial of speed be between two men. The horse in his brute way, can only struggle and strive for supremacy in his brute unreasoning fashion; while man, the higher and nobler animal by far, will bring his intellect to his aid in order to compass victory.

That was precisely the trouble, the noble humans used their intellect and they tended to be more crooked than the horses, so pedestrianism came in fashionable bursts, drifting from public delight to disgust. It was like the prize ring. Some of the more high-toned journals like the Sydney Morning Herald and the Argus preferred to believe that the sport did not exist. Yet the peds drew the crowd, while amateur athletics had crowds of a capacity similar to those at school sports.

Australians have always liked to bet, and while enjoying their bets on dogs and horses, they found it even more satisfactory to bet on humans. Frequently the races took place as straight out man-to-man contests, similar to prize fights, with bets on the side. The money often was good and the top athletes of the world came to Australia looking for it.

One of the early greats was Tom Cusack of Wangaratta. In north-eastern Victoria, the locals thought him the fastest, the most powerful man alive. The Melbourne champion was Mat Higgins, born in Kilkenny, Ireland, and the two men came together in the summer of 1868 for a series of races for £400. The first event was on a special 150-yard straight track at Goyder's Red House in Northcote, and on the Saturday he brought a crowd of two thousand. Higgins won by eight yards and after they chaired him into the hotel, the sad truth became clear: the timekeepers had suffered from a variety of complaints and there were those who failed to start their watches, while others forgot to stop theirs.

Cusack complained he didn't perform well because of sickness and giddiness before the race. That was hardly surprising said Mr Thomas Dodd:

I was astounded at seeing Cusack take for his tea on Friday afternoon stewed mutton, a bowl of soup, three and a half cups of tea, two spoonfuls of butter in his eggs, and then his toast spread thick with butter. This was not an account for his sickness; he might as well have gone to bed rolling drunk.

The second race was to be 'on a trotted road' and the locale was the Brighton Road near the Elsterwick Hotel. Higgins again won and this time the stop watches did work for the official time was sixteen seconds.

These shocking results were more than Wangaratta could bear. The only thing to do was to get him on the home ground. So Cusack. The Wangaratta Flyer put up these terms: a side bet of £200 each, a straight fee of £40 to Higgins, plus a third of the gate money.

The race was set for Monday 15 June. The man from the Ovens and Murray Advertiser reported that people crowded into Wangaratta from all over the colony, and excitement in the streets that morning was intense. But he was very angry. There were no free passes for the press and entry to the ground cost one shilling, which was expensive enough. Yet worst of all there was no allocation of checks and he had to go out to send telegrams of the results to Beechworth. Getting in and out cost him the terrible sum of five shillings. The betting was three to two on Higgins, but there was still big money on the local boy because he had managed to convince all Wangaratta that Melbourne had not been a true indication of his form.

So the crowd was anti-Higgins, and in the first race they showered him with missiles. Despite the missiles he managed to come from behind and just get ahead with Cusack. There was no nudge whatever for the judge to be blackguarded the way he was, said the Beechworth reporter. The crowd gave Mat Higgins an appalling time and twice as he approached the finish he was knocked to the ground. In the heat of the first race he was beaten by a yard. The last and deciding race over three hundred yards he won easily, but it was full of interest.

He had to cope with obstacles like people falling out of trees. During the race a Mr Jones, the Beechworth chimney sweep, who had been watching from the fork of a tree, fell asleep and dropped fifteen feet on to Mr William Gray, a farmer, of Oxley, then cannoned into Mr Peter Dyring. Mr Gray was very badly hurt and Mr Jones was unconscious. The Beechworth reporter said: 'I saw him engaged in a fight some hours afterward; clearly his neck was not broken.'

As for Mat Higgins, it was a very unpopular win. He was chased by the crowd and had to hide in town in his running clothes.

In 1870 George Coppin, the theatrical producer, brought to Australia a champion sprinter of the world, Frank 'Scurry' Hewitt, born in Limerick, Ireland. He was to be matched against John Gregory Harris, the champion colonial athlete. Harris was twenty-five and five feet eight inches; Harris twenty-four and five feet ten inches. Harris was the perfect physical specimen, the pure local product, born in 1846 in Collins Street, no less, Melbourne's oldest street.

The Australasian was philosophical about this. Many Australians believed that the colonial climate was the more favourable for the most perfect development of the human form. There was also the suggestion that the colonial youth, while bigger and more precocious in his development, might not have the stamina of the product from 'home.'

They arranged to have five races from 100 yards to a 440, and there were all sorts of rumours about their capabilities. Harris' team said he could run 150 yards in fourteen seconds, while Hewitt's backers counter-attacked with the rumour that he had done it in thirteen.

The races were at the Melbourne Cricket Ground and on the Saturday there were twenty thousand people, the biggest crowd since the days of Mr Stephenson's cricketing XI.

There were 'wholesale attempts by the public to climb over the outside' fence and fifteen men climbed on the roof of the stable inside the reserve. Their weight was so great that half the roof collapsed and the men crashed to the ground. The Argus reported concern itself over the fate of the fallen spectators; but the paper did not concern itself over Harris, the local boy, who won the 150 yards by three yards in 15½ seconds. He won the 200 yards by three yards in 20½ and the 440 in a dead heat in 53½ seconds.

They continued on the Monday. His Excellency Viscount Canterbury was there, 'and even the most sanguine of admirers of muscular Christianity could hardly have expected such a crowd,' said the Australasian.

But this day belonged to Hewitt. He won the 100 yards in 10½ seconds and 140 yards in 51½ seconds. Harris was angry. He said the 100 yards was a dead heat and there were all sorts of suggestions about lack of fair play and that one of the umpires was aggressive: 'a well known heavy better . . . a matter not calculated to increase public confidence.'

Nor were the gentlemen of the Melbourne Cricket Club pleased about the affair. This letter to the newspapers has rarely been equalled for M.C.C. disdain:

Amateur pedestrianism and athletic sports are all very well, but the professional element is not wanted. We don't want our ground rushed by rough, our pavilion invaded by members of the ring, trainers etc. The ground was granted for cricket, all cricketers, and although we tolerate amateur pedestrianism it is toleration only.

Why should the dressing room of private members be rendered unbearable by a steaming mob of runners.

A member of the M.C.C.

This had its effect: the next race was in the Friendly Society Gardens which were on the opposite side of the Yarra from the Botanic Gardens. The three hundred yard route was lined with gigs, carts, and drays which acted as grandstands. All the trees were occupied and seven thousand paid to watch. Hewitt won easily with Harris 'toiling hopelessly in the rear.'

The Australasian man shook his head: 'The fact is universally admitted, an honest professional runner has come to be regarded as a human phenomenon.'

Frank Hewitt raced a horse over a hundred yards at the Friendly Society Gardens in 1874. An Arab horse which had repeatedly done the distance in nine seconds was chosen, so the betting was 1/1 on the horse. But the horse was fidgety and got a bad start, and Hewitt just managed to take first place. Another five yards and the horse would have won. This time there were no questions about the integrity of the race.

Victoria was, and still is, the home of professional running. Frank Smith, a theatrical promoter, established the Botany track in the grounds of the Sir Joseph Banks Hotel at Botany, and there was also the Carrington Ground at the edge of Moore Park. The Carrington Handicap was in honour of the handsome young sporting Governor, Lord Carrington, so at last the peds had patrons at the very highest places.

Top peds came to Sydney from all over the world. There was Tom Malone from Ireland, there was L. S. Myers from the United States, 'champion runner of the world', and Harry Hutchins from England, also billed as champion of the world. Comments on the sporting invasion in Town and Country: 'Sydney is the greatest sporting city in the world.' The peds raced almost every weekend at the Carrington or Botany tracks and the bookmakers fielded in fine style, calling the odds over large fields. The huge money went as high as £840 for a single race.

Some of the best peds were Aborigines. There was Bobby McDonald, the first man to try the crouch start. Athletes at the time were not allowed to have any part of the body behind the line. Bobby McDonald would squat almost on his haunches then leap out with a mighty bound.

The greatest sprinter of the day was the Aboriginal, Charlie Samuels. He had a stride of nine feet three inches and recorded such remarkable times as 9⅗ seconds for 100 yards. He was a natural runner, and he could not see the point in training, and for most of his performances he didn't train at all. At his best he could have raced equally with the great Jack Donaldson.

In January 1892, Charlie Samuels had a series of man-to-man races against the great Englishman, Harry Hutchins, who was 5/1 on favourite. Samuels won the first race over a hundred yards in under even time. Already he had done the Botany Handicap by four yards under even, but Town and Country mourned that no one in England would ever believe their records. It was an interesting insight into the local Australian inferiority complex.

The prevailing opinion at home is that we are a lot of numb-skulls. No matter how reliable a record is made in Australia we never see it quoted by an English paper. Strange to say every man who has ever gone from Australia to England has always done well. It is time that we got the credit for being able to do something.

And the journal explained that the English would dream up all sorts of excuses for Harry Hutchins' failure against Charlie Samuels.

Harry Hutchins narrowly won the next race over 150 yards in 14½ seconds, so for the following meeting in a curious burst of confidence, he gave the sable champion three yards start. Charlie not only won in 12½ seconds but his lead was better than three yards.

But professional running couldn't maintain such popularity with races week after week, all handicapped. It was too easy to persuade runners to run dead for weeks on end, or to offer a runner a large share of the bookmaker's take if he were 'stiff' on the day. The bookmakers 'are perfectly unscrupulous,' said one leader writer. 'They don't care a jot who sinks as long as they swim.' So the game sank.

The most extraordinary character at this time was 'Professor' William Miller. He was not a professor in the university sense, but if one considered oneself the ultimate, the greatest teacher and master of an art, then professor was the right title. 'Professor' Miller, of course, was all these things. He always signed himself 'Professor Miller, champion athlete of the world.'

He claimed he was the world champion at boxing, fencing, wrestling, heavy dumb-bell lifting and long distance walking. He came to Australia from Cheshire in 1851 when he was five years old, and after he had travelled the world and shown his enormous gift for 'muscular Christianity', he opened a gymnasium at 216 Swanston Street, Melbourne, and he was the colony's prime example of manliness, physical fitness, strength and clean living. If one required advice on any subject Professor Miller was the man.

He made many trips to the United States to press his fame. In 1879 he walked 102 miles in twenty-four hours. It was interesting how he trained. He would rise at 4 a.m. and go through a routine of his studying physical movement. He came home for a breakfast of stale bread, a scraping of butter, chop or steak underdone, with two cups of tea and sugar. (The 'Professor' was a great believer in stale bread.) He walked around town, socialised a little, then for lunch he had roast beef or mutton, vegetables, a light pudding of tapioca, sago or rice without milk, stewed fruit and a sound lager or Bass Ale.

Another long walk in the afternoon, then at 5 p.m., tea — boiled chicken or turkey, celery, stale bread with a little butter, two boiled eggs, then bed at 9 p.m.

Then when the walk actually started he said:

While on the track my drink consisted of good sherry, well dashed with water, while my food off the track was raw oysters, strong beef tea, celery, calf-foot jelly and a small portion of boiled chicken. Soups or slops of any kind I studiously avoided.

Regularly he put advertisements like this in Melbourne, Sydney, San Francisco and New York journals:

I hereby challenge any two athletes in the world to make a match for £500 a side, a contest in Graeco-Roman wrestling, heavy dumb-bell lifting, foil fencing, broadsword exercise, basket sticks to be used. Athletes accepting this challenge to divide the exercises between them.

I am also prepared to match myself against any athlete in the world for £500 a side at boxing, Graeco-Roman wrestling and heavy dumb-bell lifting, winning of most exercises to be declared the winner.

William Miller, champion athlete of the world.

He did remarkable things. At Cincinnati he raised with one hand a 100 lb dumb-bell twenty times, a 201 lb bell once, a 165 lb bell twice, a 150 lb bell three times. He raised in each hand two dumb-bells of 250 lbs, with both hands 1,550 lbs of solid iron.

Then he did something no athlete had done before. He raised with the right hand a 160 lb dumb-bell to the shoulder, pushed it slowly to straight arm's length above the head and while keeping it in this position raised with his left hand a 60 lb dumb-bell six times. Then he dropped the two dumb-bells to the shoulders at the same time and placed them on the floor.

When he returned from America he repeated his challenge to meet anyone in any form of athletics. Abe Hickin, the fighter, was the only man who would accept. He agreed to meet the 'Professor' for £500, but on his terms, out in the open air with bare fists. 'I wish you Sir, to understand that I do not desire to make an exhibition of myself by a puevile or lackadaisical glove contest.'

The 'Professor' reminded Hickin and his backers that he had always kept within the law, however, he would meet 'any man breathing with gloves on.' So there was no luck with Hickin, but one man who did accept the challenge was Larry Foley, the fighter who thrashed Hickin in a secret bare fist border battle on the Murray in 1879. He was the champion of Australia, the master scientific boxer, and according to Jem Mace, the great English champion, pound for pound the best fighter in the world.

The bout was to take place in Sydney, and the 'Professor' had a grand farewell at St George's Hall. His friends hired the Fitzroy Band just to give the 'good-bye' a touch of style. So the 'Professor' went into training. He did not believe in too much sparring before a fight as it made one stale. Physical fitness was the thing. He put in an hour with the dumb-bells. He then had a swim before his breakfast of steak and chops. He did an eight-mile walk during the morning, spent all afternoon in the gymnasium, then had a bowl of gruel before retiring at 10 p.m.

Larry Foley was now thirty-two and, at ten stone twelve pounds, almost three stone lighter than the 'Professor', but his Sydney backers thought his brilliant speed and boxing skill would be sufficient to finish off the muscle-bound Miller.

Miller, 'Herculean in appearance', according to one paper, was five feet nine inches in height. He had a forty-two inch chest and was now thirty-seven years old.

The promoters tried to get almost every hall in Sydney, including the Town Hall, without success. They were exhausted, oddly enough, 'the harmony wasn't very good.' The stage was slanted, so that the man who took the upper side had an immense advantage. For the first sixteen rounds the 'Professor' slowly gained the upper hand. By round twenty-seven Foley was shaky, by thirty-five he was exhausted and almost defenceless. The 'Professor' was too kind, unwilling to administer the final blow. In the thirty-seventh round he decided it had to be done, he sent a right to Foley's jaw and floored him unconscious. He dropped on to the canvas like a bag of chaff.

At this point all those who had money on Foley rushed the stage and created an uproar. This gave Foley's seconds time to swamp him with water, restore him to consciousness and push him back into the ring. By then Miller could have ended it at any time, but still he was reluctant. Finally in the fortieth round he gave the coup de grâce and Foley fell, helpless, a motionless heap in the middle of the stage.

Again the crowd rushed forward and the stage became mad pandemonium, a swirling mass of people fighting in an utterly un-Marquess of Queensberry fashion. The police intervened and the referee declared the bout a draw. So much for the concert at the Academy of Music.

The bout lasted two hours fifty minutes, and, of course, seeing it was a draw, the backers were able to escape with their debts. The whole affair caused a great stir and little else was discussed in Sydney for a week. The Sydney Morning Herald, which normally ignored fights, this time thundered:

It was just another upheaval of the detestable spirit which gives the inhabitant of another State opportunity to point the finger of scorn. The combatants fought like two honest animals — the one hopelessly brave, the other mercifully tolerant, but the audience from the bookmaker and the patron with thousands on the result, to the larrikin with nothing but his notion of national repute at stake — seemed to lose all natural bearings of fair play. They howled as wild beasts at each blow that told against them. They rushed the stage like hungry dogs when their dignified but whiskered leaders bade them rush in and save the poor bone of victory.

The 'Professor' was cruelly treated in Sydney, but he was feted on his home coming. The Sportsman said that never before in Australia — and indeed in the 'Old Country' — had an encounter attracted such interest, and it was the final settling of the assertion that 'a good big "un" could always beat a good little "un".'

The Fitzroy Band was called out once more. The Naval Reserve came out to give 'attractive drill in the Professor's honour' and there was a special benefit at the Princess Theatre, where there was an attractive programme which included a number of exhibition bouts and the 'Professor' himself, no less, in an exhibition of 'weight lifting.'

Never was there such a hero and he went on a grand exhibition tour of Victoria. He appeared at the Princess Theatre in Bendigo, and the 180 lb dumb-bell that he could raise one head with one hand went on display in the Beehive Exchange so that the sharebrokers could try their strength. None was able to lift it more than three feet.

At this time he was never out of the news, issuing challenges right and left. He would offer to fence anyone in Australia, any choice of weapons. He would offer to battle anyone in Australia at Graeco-Roman wrestling, promising to throw them for five falls in one hour. He even made a series of public challenges to the great John L. Sullivan, heavyweight boxing champion of the world. Sullivan did agree to meet him for $1,000 for a glove contest in San Francisco or New York — the winner to take all four rounds of the house. If Miller could last four rounds he would be the winner.

Sadly for the 'Professor' he broke his ankle wrestling the Scottish champion, Donald Dinnie, who fell on Miller's leg causing such an ugly fracture that the doctors predicted he would never wrestle again. He was out of action for more than a year and his return bout was with the same Donald Dinnie, an athlete whose record was almost as impressive as that of the 'Professor'. He could run the 100 yards in 10½ seconds, jump five feet eleven inches, and lift over his head two dumb-bells — one 128 lbs, the other 112 lbs.

The bout took place at the Bijou Theatre and the box office opened at unheard of prices: stalls six shillings, circle ten shillings, family circle four shillings and upper circle two shillings. The Sportsman was so excited about the affair it asked Mr J. Fraser, the physiognomist, of 76 Bourke Street East, to give his opinions. Physiognomists were most popular; they could reveal one's character by studying physical features. Miller had the larger brain, he said. He was more cosmopolitan and, though not as good at saving, he was a far better man at business. If he had a defect it was heaviness. He was essentially a heavyweight man. He could hug like a bear and crush together the ribs of an ordinary man just as some would squeeze a basket out of shape. Dinnie, on the other hand, was taller, thin-flanked, though heavy about the shoulders and neck. He was not a fighting man; his athletics would be of a more peaceful sort; he was afraid of being hurt; much more afraid than strong men usually are.

Miller weighed in at fourteen stone twelve pounds, Dinnie fourteen stone four pounds. The Sportsman reported that never before had there been such a gathering at a wrestling match. Almost the entire medical fraternity of Collins Street was there, no doubt admiring his mended ankle. The wrestling was Graeco-Roman and mixed, 'catch as catch can above the waist — tripping allowed'. The 'Professor' won the first fall after twelve minutes, and they had a fifteen minute break. Dinnie scored the second fall after seven minutes and then they went for the 'mixed' wrestling. Miller scored two falls and won the match.

During his career he wrestled seventy-two matches, won seventy-five, lost six and drew eleven. In 1887 he went off to the United States again and stayed there for some years before returning to run his gymnasium in Melbourne.

In the all-round athlete class perhaps the only man who could outshine 'Professor' Miller was the famous Reg 'Snowy' Baker. He was born in 1886 and like Victor Trumper, he went to Crown Street State School in Sydney. He was said to be in world class in twenty-nine different sports; he was brilliant as a footballer, cricketer, rower, boxer, wrestler, fencer, swimmer, diver, horseman, gymnast and tower. He was a legend; his fans bought his physical culture course, his 'Snowy' Baker magazine, rubbed his special sportsman's liniment on their limbs and tried desperately to emulate his feats.

At Sydney University he won blues for cricket, football, athletics and rowing. He won forty different swimming titles, he rowed with a crew that won four championship titles, and he represented Australia in two Rugby Tests in Britain in 1904. He was the Australian amateur middleweight boxing champion in 1905 and 1906, and he represented Australia at boxing in the 1904 and 1908 Olympics. In the London Olympics of 1908 he fought four times in the one evening with splendid effect. His third opponent in the semi-final went down after only four punches. In the final he was against J. W. H. T. (Johnny Won't Hit Today) Douglas, who was to be captain of the M.C.C. touring cricket side to Australia. It was not to be a case of J.W.H.T. on this occasion. They fought five extremely closely fought battle for the gold medal, and the referee gave the decision to Douglas, although it was angrily disputed by the crowd.

He was almost as active a businessman as he was an athlete. In 1911 he formed a syndicate, bought the Sydney Stadium from Hugh D. McIntosh and promoted a series of famous fights. The star of many of them was Les Darcy. It was Baker who stripped Darcy of his titles when he slipped off to America secretly in 1916. The departure could always have been used to accuse Baker of turning America against their idol, and breaking his heart.

'Snowy' Baker was part of the emotional scene when Les Darcy's embalmed body came home from the United States. Despite threats to kill him, Snowy Baker insisted on attending the funeral in Maitland and he followed the body all the way to the cemetery. There his friends forcibly stopped him from going inside for they believed some of the more dedicated Darcy fans were bearing pistols. Later 'Snowy' Baker went off to a film career in Hollywood; he became director of the Riviera Country Club and died in Los Angeles in 1953.

Two immortals in Australian athletics, remembered with almost the reverence of Carbine, were Arthur Postle 'The Crimson Flash', and Jack Donaldson 'The Blue Streak'. Arthur Postle was a Queenslander and he was born at Pittsworth, near Toowoomba. He set world records for 50 yards (5.2 seconds), 60 yards (6.1 seconds), 75 yards (7.2 seconds), 80 yards (7½ seconds), and 200 (19 seconds). Some of his greatest exploits were on the West Australian goldfields.

In 1906, after negotiations that went on for ten months, the promoter, Rufe Naylor, managed to persuade the champion sprinter of the world to come to the goldfields for a series of races against the Australian champion, Arthur Postle. Kalgoorlie could hardly believe its good fortune. This was the coup of the century, almost as good as winning the Olympic Games.

The champion had the splendid name Rochfort Beauchamp Day, and he was a true blue Irishman. He did not go straight to Kalgoorlie but spent seven weeks sunning himself at the seaside resort of Bunbury. He declared that he had put in one of the most intensive preparations of his career and he was fit to race for a kingdom.

Days before the big event Kalgoorlie began to fill with visitors and special excursion trains came in from all around the goldfields — from Laverton, Leonora, Kanowna and Coolgardie. On race morning, 5 December 1906, the Kalgoorlie Miner said:

Is it any wonder that for weeks past every adult in the community has been picturing in his mind's eye what is to eventuate on the recreation ground tonight? Before the dawn tomorrow the victor's name will be flashed all over the universe and the greatest duel of the century between two live champions . . . the fastest human beings in the world . . . will be a thing of the past.

The Miner pointed out that this was the greatest athletic contest since the founding of Australia, and the crush was so great at the recreation ground that the Athletic Club had to install an extra turnstile to cope with the crowd. Then, despite roving police and officials, hundreds of people climbed the outside fence into the ground. And, of course, the bookmakers were there. They looked splendid in dinner suits.

In honour of the athletes the Kalgoorlie Model Band played St Patrick's Day Parade for Day and the composition Advance for Postle. The first race was due to start at 8 p.m., but even by 8.30 there was no sign of action. The crowd started a steady infiltration movement closer and closer to the track. This brought on a wild, scrambling, chain reaction; for those who had paid two shillings for special reserved chairs were outraged when they could no longer see, and they moved themselves in front of those who had infiltrated.

The situation became worse and worse. The crowd was actually sitting right on the track, so that it was impossible to run the 75-yard event. Just to add to the confusion there were a dozen stray dogs on the ground, so that the world's fastest humans could easily have found themselves tripping over a kelpie. The police, Sergeant Houlihan and Sergeant Kelso, came out and pleaded with the crowd to move back. No success. They called in four mounted troopers and it looked for a time as if at last they were getting somewhere. But the situation was a nasty one, and the miners were very powerful. Inspector Newland, the officer in charge, pointed out that this was a public ground and legally the crowd was entitled to sit anywhere it liked. He would make no moves that would endanger the lives of his men.

The crowd was alternately groaning and cheering and calling out: 'Come on Naylor — we can't wait here all night.' Yet that was practically what they did. It was very late indeed when Rufe Naylor had an idea: why not get Arthur Postle to ask the crowd to move back? So out came the Crimson Flash himself, clad in his crimson costume. He appealed to them to move. 'I am an Australian and unless they moved an Australian to become a world champion and unless they moved off the track he had no hope of beating the Irishman. This was sort of language they understood and they moved back to a man. The Kalgoorlie Miner reporter was astonished. He said he was an extraordinary performance, a feat which spoke volumes for Postle's popularity.

So Postle in his crimson and Day with the shamrock on his shirt came back for the 75 yards dash. It was Postle's race, he won by two and a half yards in 7½ seconds. As the Miner gently put it, he donkeyicked the Irishman. The cheering could have been heard miles away and for some minutes it was a perfect pandemonium. Handkerchiefs were waved, hats thrown up — some of them being never found again — and almost every member of the crowd was full of excitement.

However, R. B. Day was the favourite for the 100 yards. This was his specialty and he was known as 'a perfect wonder over the distance'. Yet Postle won again, this time by three yards. The crowd picked him up and carried him shoulder high around the ground, while the Kalgoorlie Model Band played Here the Conquering Hero Comes.

There were some angry letters in the newspapers the next day about the treatment of Day. As soon as he failed to win he was spurned and left alone by his fellow Irishman, a shameful affair, since the Australians had always looked after their own kind. The Miner also reflected Day's reactions: 'Drawn features expressed more than words could tell from him how deeply he felt at having the title of world champion wrested from him.'

Arthur Postle was born at Pittsworth near Toowoomba on 8 March 1882. Jack Donaldson was born at Raywood, Victoria, on 16 March 1886, and he was always slightly aggrieved that he did not hang on to make it St Patrick's Day. Their careers were allied — they were the two finest sprinters in the world. Postle usually was the master of the short bursts. From the 100 yards on it was usually Donaldson.

Donaldson's other famous run was on 23 September over 130 yards at the Sydney Sports Ground, and he was defending his title against Charles Holway 'the American Bullet'. Donaldson had not been happy about that Johannesburg experience. He had been suffering from a bad leg 'and besides,' he said, 'it was a strange place. They had the race at the uncanny hour of 10 p.m. and all the glare of the electric light unnerved me.'

Holway had had one victory over Donaldson in Wales, but then Donaldson had beaten him over the 100 yards at Bendigo the previous April. In Sydney the race had a big publicity build-up, and all the talk was that Holway had been improving with every race and he was fitter than ever before.

The Daily Telegraph reported that when he ran out on the ground he looked a picture of strength and vitality — his action was high and his stride tremendous. The betting seemed to favour Holway, but when Donaldson came out it was a different story.

The Telegraph man called him 'The Blue Streak', 'The Kerang Flyer', 'The Carbine of Running' and he did prove to be unbeatable. He beat Holway 'by three strides' and everyone on the entire ground rose to their feet, cheering and shouting. This went on for some minutes, but when the time was announced the cheering broke out again. He was timed by Andrew Kerr, the official clocker for the Australian Jockey Club, and it was like Johannesburg, but on this occasion it was Kerr who would not believe. He had on his watch 12 seconds dead. Since he knew his watch was correct he doubted that the distance was accurate. So he had the track measured with a steel tape and found that it was 132 yards 2 inches, and slightly uphill.

This broke the world record set by W. Johnson in England back in 1867, when he ran it in 12½ seconds. Donaldson's 130 yard record lasted until 1951 when Lloyd La Beach of Panama ran 11.9. Those who follow the Donaldson legend claim he was better than the immortal Jesse Owens, better than any sprinter who ever lived, and had he used modern blocks and run on a smooth cinder track instead of grass, who knows what he might not have achieved. After the First World War he went to the United States and coached athletics at a number of universities. He died in New York aged only forty-seven.

At Stawell he is revered as the greatest of all professional athletes. The athletic club erected a drinking fountain to his memory in 1939. Yet Jack Donaldson never won the Stawell Gift. He was twenty and he had just turned professional when he competed in the Gift at Kerang in 1906. His father, who then ran a hotel in Kerang, had no doubts about young Jack's ability and backed him for £1,000.

When he got down for the start of the 130 yard event he was tense and straining. A horseshoe pitcher behind him threw a shoe ring which rang against another shoe near the stake. Donaldson thought it was the gun and he was off. The judges penalised him a yard and E. W. Thomson beat him into second place by inches. That ring of a horseshoe cost the Donaldson family £1,000. Even so he did have an easy win that year in the 220 yards.

The Stawell Easter Gift has taken place every year since 1879 and the event is an intriguing piece of Australiana. It began in the gold mining days of Stawell and in its heyday it drew twenty-five thousand people — now more like ten thousand. It was almost the Melbourne Cup of athletics. To have been a Stawell Gift winner or even to have been in a Stawell final was enough to gain enormous prestige. The important thing for runners always was to conceal their ability from bookmakers. For months before Easter, athletes would train in secret for the big race. They would go to Stawell days before the sports, hold time-trials away from prying eyes and discuss prospects and tactics with friends behind doors. If one was on to a good thing about a runner, there was always a special term, you were, 'in the secret.'

The Stawell Athletic Club claimed that this was the best-run professional athletic event in the world, and it did manage to survive because there was not too much obvious hanky-panky. In 1918 the first three place getters were suspended because of incorrect performances on entry forms. So there was no prize that year and the suspended runners unsuccessfully took the matter to the Supreme Court. In 1920 there was the case of 'A. Petersen' of Malvern, a runner who was very heavily backed. He finished second to A. G. Cashmore, but someone who saw a photograph of the finalists on the starting line identified him as a former leading New Zealand amateur, named Jack Lindsay. Not only was he the champion of the British Armed Forces, but only a few months before Stawell he had finished second in the Australasian amateur 100 yards title. Lindsay was immediately disqualified for life.

Then there was the year 1933 when Cyril Heath won the Gift, the Sporting Globe reporting that Stawell had all the atmosphere of an Edgar Wallace thriller. Heath won his heat well on the Saturday and he was odds-on favourite for Monday's final. He was just leaving his boarding house on the Sunday morning at 10 a.m. when a man rushed at him and kicked him violently in the groin. Heath retaliated, and so did his father, a former oarsman. He flattened the man with several punches to the jaw, and when he appeared in court later he still had the marks on his face.

Heath's manager, Roy McMinn, applied for police protection and from then on the favourite had a policeman sleeping in his bedroom. It was just like the dramatic events of 1950 when gangsters tried to nobble Phar Lap. Indeed there was a striking relationship between the two events. The same constable who had slept with Phar Lap, slept with Heath.

The grand arrival at the ground had Edgar Wallace qualities. The entrance was surrounded by plain clothes police and Heath arrived in the grand manner, a group which included his father, brother, and trainers, while his mother and sisters walked a few paces behind. Onlookers cheered as he walked on to the ground. Then he entered his own specially erected tent and inside was his police guard. Such 'dramatic events,' said the Sporting Globe, 'are unparalleled in the history of athletics in any part of the world.' Then just as Phar Lap won the 1930 Melbourne Cup with the aid of police protection, Cyril Heath went on to win the 1933 Stawell Gift.

Now, along with the Bendigo Thousand, the Stawell Gift is the last stronghold of professional athletics and it has a vintage turn-of-the-century air about it. In the 1940s the Gift used to attract up to twenty-five thousand people, now the figure is more like ten thousand. Under the trees there is a little ring of ten bookmakers who call the odds on the races. In the old days there used to be thirty, but now too many people prefer to bet on the four-footed athletes and they go to the TAB up the street.

It was a long time before the amateurs of 400 metres could match the triumphs of the old-time professionals and some of the greatest came from women. Winners of track events at the Olympic Games were few. E. H. Flack, a former schoolboy athlete from Melbourne Grammar, won the 800 metres and the 1,500 metres at the first modern Olympiad in Athens in 1896. His wins confused the Greek officials. They could not find an Australian flag and they raised the Austrian flag by mistake.

A. W. Winter set a world record of 50 feet 11¾ inches to win the gold medal for the hop, step and jump at the Paris Olympics of 1924, and John Winter of Western Australia won the high jump gold medal with 6 feet 6 inches in London in 1948. But for public excitement the wins of Marjorie Jackson have probably never been equalled in Australia. Although we had been sending women competitors to the Games since 1928, Marjorie Jackson of Lithgow, N.S.W., was the first to win a gold medal. She won the gold medal for the 100 metres when she equalled the world record of 11.5 seconds, and she broke the world record for the 200 metres with 23.4 seconds to score her second gold medal.

The newspapers reported that on the night of 21 July 1952, more electricity was used in the town of Lithgow than on any other night in its history. Most people in Lithgow sat up waiting for news to come through for the result of the 100 metres. When it did the town went crazy. Everything was used to celebrate Marjorie's victory — ambulance sirens, fire alarms, factory whistles, railway engine whistles, school and church bells, a carillon and thousands of voices. Marjorie Jackson's father worked at the Lithgow Small Arms factory. At 5 p.m. the factory stopped work while workers cheered, sirens roared, and the workers set up a clatter on their shields and hammered pipes with hammers and spanners.

Jack O'Hagan, who in 1950 wrote a hit song to celebrate the triumphs of Don Bradman, did the same thing for Marjorie Jackson. He called it Our Marjorie and these were the words:

Has put the town of Lithgow on the map — And never fails to set the hearts of Aussies in a whirl. She's Marjorie — the Lithgow flier, Our Marjorie — she's a trier, The fastest thing in running at the Olympic Games, Her brilliant wins have won her all the world's acclaim. Our Marjorie, As modest as they make 'em. Our Marjorie!— But records can she break 'em? The Lithgow flash has proved a smash, A champion thru and thru. And Marjorie we're telling you — Ev'ry dinkum Aussie's proud of you.

Jack O'Hagan wrote the song to have it on the market in time for Marjorie Jackson's return from Helsinki. From the time of writing the recording was done in seven days. Jim Gussey recorded it with a twenty-two piece ABC Dance Band at Columbia studios in Sydney, and Parlophone had the record on the market in seven days.

Her title was the 'Lithgow Flyer' or the 'Lithgow Flash' and her homecoming was remarkable. She drove the hundred and fifty miles from Sydney in a new sedan with a police motor-cycle escort. Town after town along the route stopped its activities as residents lined the road to cheer. Then at Lithgow the switched to an open car, which was filled with flowers, rather like a funeral wagon. Apart from this she had a matching escort composed of an Army detachment, and schoolchildren and two bands.

Across the street there were WELCOME MARJORIE banners plus scores of other signs including: 'Welcome home,' 'Marj — You B — wearing a —.' Men athletes carried her shoulder high to the dais which was decked with flowers and there was the Mayor, Alderman Benvon, told the crowd: 'Our Lithgow home girl — our Ambassadors — has made the name of Lithgow and Australia famous throughout the world.'

She flew to Adelaide on a two-day visit in an aircraft specially christened and painted 'The Lithgow Flash.' The Sun News-Pictorial flew her to Melbourne so that the city could run on a traditional sporting triumph. The Premier, Mr McDonald, gave her a reception at Parliament House and the Lord Mayor gave her a reception at the Town Hall. She had a triumphal tour of the city along Bourke, Russell, Collins and Spencer Streets and, according to the Sun, tens of thousands of people turned out to see her, many of them shouting 'Good on you Marj.'

The story for Betty Cuthbert was similar. Betty Cuthbert won the gold medals for the 100 metres, 200 metres and 4 × 100 metres relay at the 1956 Olympic Games. Public delight perhaps reached its greatest peak on the morning of 30 November. The newspapers kept talking of Australia's 'gathering of gold', as if Australia were competing in a mining corporation, were actually in action at the Melbourne Cricket Ground. Sometimes it seemed as if no other nation were competing in the Games. Betty Cuthbert had won the 100 metres final and the Argus almost ignored all other events. It devoted its entire front page to Miss Cuthbert in full colour and the heading was BETTY CUTHBERT — GOLDEN GIRL. Everywhere the paper called her 'The girl with the magic feet', but it was Golden Girl that stuck and she was given so many telegrams addressed to her regular nick-name, she even received telegrams addressed to 'Golden Girl' with no other name given.

When she returned to Sydney with the other New South Wales representatives there was a ticker-tape reception. Seventy thousand people, sometimes crammed ten deep, lining the city streets. Parramatta, her home town, gave a special Betty Cuthbert reception. They cheered her as she went through town perched on a Rolls-Royce. The Cuthbert house was just inside the borders of the Council of Parramatta, so West Ryde, the neighbouring district, claimed her as their personal property also, which meant another reception, another Mayor, another speech, and another Freedom of the City.

The Betty Cuthbert camellia was named in her honour and the name of the main street in the suburb of Ermington was changed to Betty Cuthbert Avenue. But it was not all that easy. The Golden Girl reveals something of the strain of being an Australian sporting star, how the publicity and the fanfare caused nightmares, how she would dream that she was shaking hands with people.

There were few places I could go without people recognising me. Some wanted to touch me and some if I was real, others just feel people looking at me and talking about me. It got to the point where I didn't want to go anywhere, but, if I did, I only couldn't go where I knew there wouldn't be too many people. I finally if I wanted a swim I would go to some little known, abandoned beach well away from the crowds. I didn't have a life of my own any longer but was common property. I realised that being a successful athlete walked hand-in-hand with being a public figure but now I wished it hadn't. I used to pray that I could be left alone.

The strain of it all forced Betty Cuthbert into a temporary retirement, but she made a great come-back with a gold medal for the 400 metres at the 1964 Tokyo Olympics.

The greatest male heroes were the distance men — John Landy, Herb Elliott and Ron Clarke. Landy, a shy thoughtful student, trained at Percy Cerutty's training camp at Portsea and in one year cut his time for the mile from 4.14.6 to 4.02.1, and it seemed certain that he would be the first man in the world to crack the four-minute barrier for the mile. It was almost as much publicised as if there would be an explosion or the heavens would crack when this thing was achieved. Landy's greatest obstacles were lack of talented opposition and poor tracks. Yet luck throughout his career was never on his side. Just before he went overseas to find the right tracks and the opposition he needed, Roger Bannister of England ran 3 minutes 59.4 seconds for the mile at Oxford.

Forty-six days later at Turku in Finland Landy set a new world record, he reduced Bannister's time to 3.57.9 and it was very nearly the biggest Australian sporting story of the decade. For example the Sun News-Pictorial in Melbourne had the story all over the front page; Landy's life story was on the feature page, and he was in the sporting pages. Pictures covered the centre spread and he was in the sports pages as well. There were pictures of Landy at his favourite hobby of mile chasing butterflies. There were pictures showing what a mile looked like — spaced out along Elizabeth Street, Melbourne, it would go from Flinders Street Station and almost reach Victoria Street. This was what Landy could cover in 3.57.9. The previous day the Herald had given similar noble coverage complete with a magic eye series of pictures showing the Landy running style. Then they featured two scientists, Professor R. D. Wright and Professor W. A. Rawlinson, trying to explain how Landy had done this impossible thing. In honour of Landy's record they flew from the Melbourne Town Hall the same flag which had flown there at the Olympic Games in Helsinki.

The other great day in Landy's life was the running of the mile against Roger Bannister at the Vancouver Commonwealth Games of 1954, to give you some idea of the drama of the occasion the English sports writer, Peter Wilson, wrote a 'here-I-am-on-the-eve-of-battle' piece which received much publicity in Australia:

As I write this it is 2 o'clock on Friday morning in Vancouver — Mile-of-the-Century night. It is a doleful, lonely time to be working. Two in the morning is a doleful, lonely time with old friends. And you should be in bed, or out celebrating. You feel very much lonelier when you think that there are two fellows who tomorrow each will run the race of his life against the other man to run a mile under four minutes — for now the pressure is on the other man to run a mile under four minutes since the first cave man decided he couldn't give the straight away to a sabre-tooth tiger.

Landy lost that race by four yards. Bannister ran it in 3.58.8 and Landy did 3.59.6, but Wilson had been curiously prophetic. On the Thursday night Landy could not sleep, so to ease his taut nerves he went out walking in bare feet. He trod on a photographer's bulb and cut himself so badly he needed four stitches in his left instep. He refused to reveal that he had cut his foot, and but for some detective work by a Montreal journalist, Andy O'Brien, nobody would ever have found out about it. Harry Gordon wrote that Landy had the cut, and that it had damaged his performance. He stamped his foot hard on the ground, a gesture which must have hurt him considerably and said: 'You're dreaming boys. There's absolutely nothing wrong with the foot.' Bannister was simply the better man.

Andy O'Brien wrote: 'I don't blame him for telling a fib. In your life time and mine we'll never meet a greater example of high sportsmanship than displayed by John Michael Landy, let's not belittle it now.'

The most successful of Australia's milers was Herb Elliott from Perth, Western Australia. He ran the mile inside four minutes seventeen times. He has credited Percy Cerutty with adding at least 10 per cent to his performance, and he endured the Cerutty 'Stotan' school at Portsea's training camp. A 'Stotan' was a peculiarly Cerutty creation and he used to distribute copies of the Stotan creed:

  1. Realisation that, as Wordsworth the poet says, 'Life is real, life is earnest', which denotes that there is no time for wasteful ideas and pursuits.
  2. In place of wasteful hobbies there commences a period of supervised and systematic physical training, together with instruction in the art of living fully. This replaces previously undirected life.
  3. Swimming will be done all the year round. It is obligatory to swim in the open sea at least once every month. This especially strengthens the will and builds resistance to quitting the task ahead.
  4. The programme implies the cessation of late hours. Amusements, both social and entertaining, should be reduced to a minimum and then only in the nature of relaxation from strenuous work.

This was a typical programme for Stotan at Portsea:

7 a.m. Five mile run before breakfast. 8 a.m. Health food breakfast. Oats, wheat germ, etc. 9 a.m. Swimming and surfing, chores, chopping wood. Noon Training and lectures at Portsea Oval, followed by another swim. 2 p.m. Lunch, fish and fresh fruit. 3 p.m. Siesta. 4 p.m. Weight lifting. 5 p.m. Ten mile run along dirt roads, ending at beach. 7 p.m. Tea and general discussion led by Percy on wide variety of subjects.

Herb Elliott in The Golden Mile said one of the athletes, Bill Stacey, wrote this little piece of verse:

A runner stood at the Pearly Gates, His face was worn and old, He bravely asked the man of late Admission to the fold. 'What have you done?' St Peter said, 'To seek admission here?' 'I trained at Portsea, that was my task, For many and many a year.' Then wide the gates did open, The angels clanged their bell. 'Come in and take a harp,' he said, 'You've had enough of hell.'

As Percy Cerutty has put it, Herb Elliott was remorseless, determined and dominating with 'as fine a disregard for his own feelings or sufferings as one could wish to see.'

At the Olympic Games in Rome, Herb Elliott won the 1500 metres by twenty yards. The Herald described him as 'King Herb' and said that he made the rest of the world's middle-distance runners look like hacks. And with pride it reported comments from overseas newspapers. Daily Mail: 'This eagle of the track. Not a man born has a chance to catch him.' Daily Mail: 'Elliott made his eight rivals look like schoolboys.' The Times: 'When Elliott is at master there are no impudent schoolboys in the classroom.' New York Times: 'Elliott's performance was almost incredible and tremendously thrilling.' His time was 3.55.6.

The Melbourne athlete, Ron Clarke, had all the qualities so much admired by Australians — modesty, determination, a dedication to training and utter self-sacrifice to sport. In his training he ran hundreds of miles a month, month after month. He broke seventeen world records for distances up to 10,000 metres and in one European tour he ran eighteen races in three weeks. Yet success at the games, whether Commonwealth or Olympic, always seemed to elude him. He never won a gold medal — Edinburgh, Tokyo, Mexico — it was always the same. The gold medal for Ron Clarke became a great national desire and at the same time a national agony and disappointment. In almost every interview Clarke had to explain why he hadn't won a medal, why he hadn't done what was expected of him.

His last chance was at the Mexico Olympics in 1968 and it was always billed at Clarke's last chance. The most spectacular picture of the games was of the doctor who wept over Clarke after he had collapsed. The agony of all this came out in the news story.

Australia's Ron Clarke collapsed after finishing a heart-breaking sixth behind Kenya's Naftali Temu in the Olympic 10,000 metres today. Clarke lay like a broken doll on the grass under a blanket for 10 minutes, his arms and legs writhing.

Australian team doctor Brian Corrigan went to kneel to massage Clarke's chest and stroke his head. Mexican doctors gave Clarke oxygen.

All around Australian athletes stood in stunned silence. Their faces showed their disappointment at the failure of Australia's greatest hope for a gold medal. It was a tragic end to Clarke's hopes of gaining at last the Olympic gold that has always eluded him. And it bore out Clarke's fears that mountain men runners from high altitude countries would dominate the distance events at Mexico's 7400 feet.

After 10 minutes of the treatment Clarke sat up, wiped his brow with his left hand and held on to a wire to help himself to his feet. Then he walked slowly off the track, his arm around Dr Corrigan and Australian track coach Ray Weinberg.

World marathon record holder, Derek Clayton, said: 'This is a sad day for all of us and most of the Australians including me, could not stop crying when we saw Ronnie fighting to keep his legs going. Ronnie ran past the limits of endurance. It's as simple as that.'

In the track events at Mexico only Ralph Doubell, 800 metres, and Maureen Caird, 80 metres hurdles, won gold medals. But the search for gold is almost desperately important to us. At the 1972 Games in Munich, in the track events on the field events only Derek Clayton, who finished thirteenth in the marathon, and no Australian woman won a gold medal (for the first time since 1948). The Australian Olympics team manager, Judy Patching, described the performances of Australia's men and women athletes as a flop and a disappointment. The Victorian Amateur Athletics Association asked for a probe into the poor performance by the Australian track and field team and there was even a question asked in the Senate. Senator McAuliffe of Queensland wanted to know why Australia had done so poorly and he urged the appropriate minister to ask the Commonwealth National Fitness Council to investigate Australian performances in the Munich Olympics.

Few Senators ever ask questions why Australians do not win more Nobel Prizes.
