The Boxing Passion

46 min read.

First published 1973

Traces the arc of Australian prize fighting from colonial bare-knuckle battles through the careers of Larry Foley, Griffo, Bob Fitzsimmons, Les Darcy and Jack Johnson's stunning defeat of Tommy Burns at Rushcutters Bay in 1908, to the modern champions Jimmy Carruthers, Lionel Rose and Johnny Famechon.

Boxing or prize fighting is the oldest and one of the most revered of Australian sports. Particularly it was loved in the old mining towns. Normally Kings, Queens, and politicians have the chance to have towns named after them, but Bendigo in Victoria was named after a prize fighter. Unquestionably Victorians believed Bendigo was more noble than any poet or Prime Minister.

Bendigo has been described as one of the most extraordinary characters to ever figure in the prize ring. He was five feet nine and a half inches, eleven stone ten pounds, and a trained acrobat. Three times he fought the great Ben Gaunt who was six feet two and a half inches and varied in weight from fifteen to eighteen stone. The third occasion was in Oxfordshire in 1845 before a crowd of ten thousand. Bendigo kept crouching down so low that Big Ben couldn't hit him. Then Bendigo, with enormous agility, kept dancing in and out, firing blows, and over ninety-three rounds he cut Gaunt to pieces. An achievement which the miners of Bendigo considered worthy of eternal honour.

Prize fighting in Australia actually began with the First Fleet, but the first battle on record took place between John Berringer and Charles Lifton at the Sydney racecourse on 7 January 1814. Curiously the opponents had to run half a mile before fighting. Berringer won, finally, after fifty-six rounds and a battle of two hours. Then there was no such thing as the careful awarding of points. The men fought with bare fists and the battle finished inevitably when one man had been knocked down and could go no more. Fighting was illegal in the Australian colonies until the late 1800s.

Not that it deterred anyone. The sport was followed very closely in the old mining towns and by the money men, sports figures and politicians. Normally Kings, Queens and politicians have the chance to have towns named after them, but according to the miners and the sporting fancy, Bendigo was more noble than any poet or Prime Minister.

Newspapers reacted to the dreadful injustice of these courts, that prize fighting should be banned, and produced enormous leading articles pointing out that it was entirely due to sport. Look at the Englishman, they said, who devoted his energies not to dominoes, or languidly dressing at the Opéra, or forming a liaison with a grisette or a grande dame, or gradually ruining his constitution by drinking large quantities of liquor. No, the Englishman from his childhood was devoted to manly sports, football, cricket and boating.

Bell's Life in Sydney let forth a burst of fury. It wanted to know when 'the Traps' would leave the manly art alone. Nothing they could do would ever stop it, and Mr Bell thundered: 'The excuses are that such contests excite ill-feeling, and may result in serious casualties. Oh! most benign and protective, social act! What a pity it does not more fully embrace all other British national pastimes under its guardian wing!'

'Bosh! Bosh! say we to all those milk and water philanthropists!'

Always they described the fights as the 'valiant', 'gallant', or 'noble mill', and they gave their reports in a curious, jocular manner, perhaps to imply a sense of fun, rather than the violent, blood nature of the sport. The boxers invariably had nicknames like 'Joe Bidders the Snob', 'Deaf Dick', 'Billy the Premier'.

Then the copy was full of euphemisms. Blood was always 'the claret'. One would never refer to a nose; a fighter would get hit in 'the snifter, or the nasal organ', and the police would be 'lynx-eyed myrmidons of the law and handcuffs'.

Only rarely did these journals describe the effect that knuckles could have on a man's face, fought in a long battle of anything up to a hundred and eighty rounds. Although Bell's Life did pass a few comments about the fight between Mat Hardy and Alf McLaren, fought near Lal Lal in July 1868. It lasted for ten hours fifty-five minutes and both fighters failed to come up for the eighty-second round. In the twenty-fifth round Mat had his lip split halfway to the nostril. By the twenty-ninth 'his face was literally chiselled all over; there was not a square inch that was not cut up and carved'. Alf had a dislocated knee-cap and fought for four hours on one leg. In the thirty-third round, 'Alf's mug began to swell. In the thirty-fifth round from blows on the nose Alf was hissing and bleeding like a pig. Then from the fifty-fourth to the eighty-first Alf continued to bleed getting weaker and weaker . . .'

Bell reported, 'Mat's face next day looked a very pretty picture, highly coloured like a coconut, beautifully carved. Constant fomentations had got Alf's head back into shape.'

One would be expected to put aside a whole day for the event and four to five hours for the actual fight was normal. On 2 January 1847, Bill Davis met Bungarabee Jack and fought him for four hours and twenty-two minutes under a fierce sun at Middle Harbour, Sydney. Admittedly neither fighter landed a blow for the first hour, but the fight did go to a hundred and eighty-eight rounds and Bungarabee at the end of that time was a ruin.

The longest bout on record took place at Fiery Creek, near Daylesford, Victoria, on 3 December 1854. Some record books suggest that it is not only an Australian record but a world's record.

Bell's Life reports that rain poured down that day but this did not deter any one. A great flow of traffic poured out from the diggings, a long line of carriages, carts, men on horseback and on foot. The whole population turned out for the fight, including the women. Normally prize fighting was an exclusively male affair, so this was rare. The bout was between Jim Kelly and Jonathan Smith for £400. James Kelly was an Irishman from a famous team of boxing Kellys. He was twenty-four, Smith was from Norwich, thirty-three, and three inches shorter than Kelly.

They set up a ring on a lovely piece of ground four miles from the Fiery Creek Road. The crowd was thick all around, and so the fight went on, both men getting weaker and weaker. After three and a half hours, Smith let go with a powerful left and the blood began to flow. This would have been a slow motion affair, so slow you could have done a series of sandwich eating in the intervals. By the seventy-sixth round they were both exhausted. The crowd got on with it. Kelly taunted his man: 'Are you going to fight? It will never come to anything.' He went with a left, Kelly countered with a right, and to the astonishment of everyone, he faltered, and the battle finished after six and a quarter hours.

At times the police would look at prize fighting with a benign eye, but after a furious report they would move to the attack again. It was always a most delicate matter to have to inform several thousand people that the fight was on, where it would take place, and still keep it secret from the police. Both in Sydney and Melbourne the promoters made great use of boats and they would go to lonely parts of the Bay or Harbour. Sometimes they hired special trains and had their fights by the railway line near Werribee. At a whisper of the approach of the police they would move the train on and fight further down the line. Sometimes they would hire a coach, rattle it round the streets at high speed, and try to lure the police off in the wrong direction.

But how could one get in on the secret? Bell's Life would try to give a hint like this:

'It would be unwise and inexpedient on our part to allude at any length to the quiet little affairs coming off in the course of the next week, since the conservators of the peace are invariably on the qui vive. We can only repeat that in the present state of the Prize Ring it would be the reverse of wise on our part to follow our usual practice in alluding to fights to come. Nevertheless, it will strike any old ring-goer or patron, that by applying at a well-known hostelry in Elizabeth Street he might possibly ferret out the secret.'

Fights usually took place at remote spots at the light of dawn. Devoted spectators would have to walk anything from ten to fifteen miles to get there.

One of the greatest of the early fighters was Joe Kitchen. At ten stone eight pounds, he won over £2,000 in his fights and had the reputation of never being beaten. In one match he fought Dan Timmins at the Great Western Mining field, and his backers devised a remarkable ruse to outwit the Ararat police. They took another fighter named Bill Turner, dressed him in Kitchen's clothes and took him out of the Bull's Head Hotel in a mysterious manner. The police were lying in ambush. A trooper grabbed him: 'Kitchen, you're my prisoner', and carried him to the station house. Meanwhile, having shaken off the police, Kitchen got through the fight in a lonely spot between two hills near the homestead. Timmins might have welcomed intervention by the police, but by the end his eyes were closed, his nose heavily battered, and blood was streaming down his face. The redoubtable Joe Kitchen won in four hours twenty minutes.

Kitchen had many tough and terrible fights. One of his best was against Harry Sallars. They fought for over a hundred and one rounds. The fight was not pretty — Happy Sallars finished with his head 'one mass of bruise' and his face frightfully cut, and Joe's eyes were so closed that he was very nearly blind.

Soon after that, Joe had a fight with Mat Hardy, a clever ten-stoner, which went for a hundred and twenty rounds. They fought on a lonely beach at Portarlington after going there by boat. Mat was knocked out twice, yet his seconds revived him and he got back into battle. It was dark and they stopped fighting when the referee said that the bout should continue next day, but the police had different ideas and charged at the Geelong Central station.

Bell's Life was furious that the great Joe had been arrested. 'It is a matter of notoriety that even in this colony, persons of a certain type take an interest in the prize ring. Such exhibitions as those which took place at Portarlington can only have a beneficial effect.'

One may well ask what would be the ideal diet in order to train a man for the prize ring. Bell's Life in Sydney gave good advice. It is recommended that the fighter should not drink more than one pint of beer a day, no more. He should avoid drinking beer from two different breweries in the same day. He should drink a glass of pure old ale. The food to be used in training is good beef and eggs, all white meats such as veal and lamb; all white fish and poultry are good for nothing. They relax the muscles. Soups, fish, pies, puddings and all such are left to the club house gourmands; they are poison to the boxer.

The real poison continued to be the police, and prize fighting received a serious set-back in the winter of 1867. This time the promoters thought they would be remarkably crafty; they would place it down the Bay on lonely Mud Island, a spot undreamed of by the Traps. They hired two steam boats, the Sophia and the Resolute.

They embarked very early from Queen's Wharf, but as an extra precaution against police interference, the ships made their way separately and each picked up a boxer, one from Williamstown and another from St Kilda. Bell reported that there was the usual camaraderie aboard, the gentlemen were disguised as toughs and the toughs were disguised as gentlemen, but from the jaded appearance of all, it was obvious everyone had been up all night to make it in time for the dawn appearance.

The morning was cold and fine and the captain promised that all passengers would be able to land at Mud Island without getting their feet wet. There was no chance of that. The first steamer went aground with Mud Island still way off across the water and it had to reverse its paddles savagely to get off again.

Then they abandoned that idea and steamed off to an area just opposite Mt Martha. But Port Phillip Bay, which had been amiably placid earlier, had done its chameleon act and now was turning on gale winds. The Sophia lowered a boat which managed only with great difficulty to get through the breakers to the shore. The occupants finished by wading in water up to their waists. The next skiff had all the gear for the ring and only through the skill of the boatmen did the party escape merely with a ducking.

Meanwhile the boatmen aboard the Resolute watched all this and presumed that it must be easy going. It cast off boats and filled them with passengers. The first sank half a mile from shore. Some clung to boats, others started swimming. The good swimmers were dragged out of the water by those arriving from the Sophia. The second boat quickly went over the breakers, spilling its occupants into the water. The troopers galloped to the spot, swam their horses into the surf and managed to save many lives.

Out at sea messages were still going back and forth between Sophia and Resolute on new arrangements for the fight. A young man, at the risk of his life, rowed back to tell them what had happened. He reported that between eight and twelve people had drowned, so the fight was abandoned.

The survivors miserably lit a fire on the lonely beach. Their misery was partially reduced when a case of Hollands gin and cold fowls was washed ashore. Eventually they struggled through the night and, nearly frozen, they reached the hotel at Dromana at a late hour. Bell's Life reported they were far from hospitably received. The Argus finally listed the names of six dead and reported that a subscription list had opened at Tattersalls and principal hotels for widows and orphans. But Bell's Life, which held all the purses and practically controlled all prize fighting, seemed the most grieved. It mourned that this could be the end of the ailing ring.

The fight business was indeed ailing, but the man who helped to revive it was Larry Foley, the man who is usually described as the father of modern boxing in Australia. He trained many of the greats — Griffo, Bob Fitzsimmons, Frank Slavin and Peter Jackson. He wasn't big, only five feet eight inches and at ten stone seven pounds he fought all sizes, in the classic style, bare fists soaked in brine. He lost some fights Marquess of Queensberry style, but he never lost a bare fist encounter.

He reached super-star fame in March 1871. It wasn't many years since the attempted assassination of Prince Alfred, Duke of Edinburgh, by an Irishman, and the mutual loathing between the Catholics and the Orangemen was perhaps the most potent emotion in New South Wales. Larry Foley wasn't exactly an Irishman, but since he was born in New South Wales, he was a good Catholic boy.

Incredibly for that time there was no prize. It was a pure death and glory affair for the cause. It took place at George's River and the crowds left Sydney at 2.30 a.m. to reach the spot. Foley wore green about his waist and Sandy Ross, the Scot, wore a yellow sash. It was described as a desperate encounter which lasted for two and a half hours. The referee declared it a draw when the police intervened. But the exultant Irish said that in another round it was all done, and they claimed victory. The Empire wrote of the diabolical feeling in the community and asked why did men have to fight each other with their fists to prove the goodness of their cause? Both men, it said, were injured.

Naturally the Scots were desperate for a return bout. They even insisted there would be 'no yellow' and 'no green'; it would all be for love of sport and they would make a wager of £100.

They settled on the same area, George's River, Port Hacking, and agreed that both parties would go by a different route to avoid detection. At dawn, in the cold and the rain, Foley's team set out by boat from the Sir Joseph Banks Hotel, their instructions being to go across the Bay to a locality in a line drawn from the hotel across George's River.

They rowed four miles to Towra Point, left the fisherman who had brought them, then walked inland. But there was no sign of preparations for a prize fight. They stumbled on to a bush-hut occupied by an old man. He knew nothing about a fight, but then someone remembered that the bout was to take place near George Hill's residence. The old man knew the place all right, it was fifteen miles away.

The Foley party immediately suspected a plot. They believed they had been put down on the wrong spot deliberately. Larry refused to give up. They had been hemmed in on one side by the threatened fine and on the other by swamps and thick undergrowth. Rain poured down. They walked, they fought their way through the long grass and swamps and stumbled down potholes.

The fight was due to take place between 9.30 and 11 a.m. They got there precisely at 11 a.m., bitterly cold and soaked. Foley, worn out from walking, yet insisted on the fight taking place immediately.

They set up the ring on a small rise in a paddock about three hundred yards from the homestead. The fight was quick, a mere half an hour, sixteen rounds, but Foley actually finished it with one tremendous punch in the first round. From then on Ross just hung on.

The referee said: 'Larry eventually knocked his man out completely, but he was very tolerant all through and let him off several times because of his utter helplessness.'

Soon after the battle the police arrived causing a vast commotion. Some of the crowd hid in the bush, others sprinted out of sight. Foley's fight had been so easy he was actually fully clothed before the police came along, so with a lovely bland astonishment he asked them what they had done to deserve this honour.

'The fighters,' they replied.

Just then George Hill, owner of the property, arrived. He said to the police: 'Look, if you follow that road down there, you will find the fight about six miles off.'

According to the Referee, the very unsophisticated young bobbies went off in that direction.

The story of the battles between Foley and Ross has been immortalized ever since in an Australian folk song sung by keen Irishmen to The Wearin' of the Green.

John Davies hears with great regret the news that's going round

That Sandy Ross has lost the fight at George's River ground.

No more his crowing will be heard, no more his colours seen,

For I think he's had enough this time of Foley and the green.

Oh, the green the colour of the brave, will raise high in the air

And to our enemies we'll show the colour that we'll wear.

As the orange flag has been pulled down, the battle fought out fine,

And Sandy Ross has lost the fight at George's River green.

In 1879 Larry Foley fought a celebrated bout which was one of the last big bare knuckles battles. In 1868 Abe Hickin had settled in Melbourne. He came to Victoria with a fine record as a lightweight and the bout had an extra flavour, a grudge between Sydney and Melbourne. The fight was to be in Melbourne territory and it was decided to have it in the neighbouring bush. Both Hickin and Foley were arrested and placed under bond to keep the peace under pain of a £500 fine.

Foley's supporters steamed out and organized what they believed was a foolproof plan. They would go out the Heads, take their fighters aboard, and have the fight on an uninhabited island. The police beat that one too — maritime experts inspected the ship and declared it unfit for sea-going.

They had another idea. Why not stage it on the Murray, on the dividing lines between the two colonies? Then perhaps they could outwit both police forces. The fans contacted the Victorian Railways, hired a special train and headed for Echuca. It had the potential to be very nearly the most celebrated fight in Victoria's history. Jem Mace of England, former heavyweight champion of the world, and Joe Thompson, the 'Leviathan' punter, travelled with them. The Riverine Herald noted their arrival and observed coldly that the bout had created immense interest in both colonies 'with a certain type of person' and warned that boxers had entered into recognizances for £500 each.

There were careful plans to outwit the police. Word went around that there were two possible sites for the battle. It could take place up the river beyond the Goulburn. Fighters would rendezvous on the other side of the Murray and go there by means of the Barmah Punt. Alternatively it could be on the Perricoota Run, ten miles down the river from Echuca.

The rumour was pushed from pub to pub with such finesse that not only did police head for the Goulburn but a large section of the crowd as well. The Argus suggested later, with some wisdom, that if the police had really wanted to stop the fight then they were 'not over-diligent about it.'

The greatest migration out of Echuca started at 1 a.m. There was a long cavalcade of every conceivable type of horse-drawn vehicle. There were two bridges — one of them was preferred. The crowd decided to take that one. They made their way in the early hours of the morning to Timothy O'Sullivan's Hotel at Wharparilla.

They came to a great camp fire where they sat around and made merry until the dawn. Now the big problem — how to cross the Murray River. The reporter from the Riverine Herald was appalled. He said they had only three small boats 'of amateur construction — surely the crankiest and most perilous ever set eyes on.' Slowly in ones, twos and threes the crowd had to be ferried over. The Herald man was horrified at the price, half a crown each, and horrified at the disastrous damage to clothes. Not only did everyone have their trousers ruined, their clothes were thoroughly soaked as well.

But the site was good. A narrow glade in the midst of heavy belts of red gum timber, situated on one of a labyrinth of points along the river, just below Dead Horse Point. It was beautifully secluded and a perfect natural stadium. The organizers brought out ropes, stakes and constructed a ring, thirty feet by thirty feet.

Foley looked taller, heavier and better muscled than Hickin, but all the betting, six to one and better, was on Hickin. At 6.55 a.m. Sam Waldock, the referee, was in the centre, and they were ready to start fighting. Just then Sergeant Hayes and Senior Constable O'Meara of the Echuca Police arrived on the scene. The Sergeant warned them they were engaged in an illegal act and called on them to stop. Jem Mace replied that they were on New South Wales territory, in an area where the Victorian Police had no jurisdiction and they proposed to proceed. The Sergeant and the Senior Constable knew that they were powerless, so they stopped to watch the fight.

The first round went for twenty-three minutes. Hickin was a charger, a barroom brawler — Foley was cool, a classic scientific boxer. He was hard to hit, he could block almost all of Hickin's dangerous blows, and he landed punches on Hickin's illuminated face with ease. By round two the betting was easy money and from then on it was impossible to lay a bet. At one stage in a driving two-fisted attack, he had Hickin with his back against the ropes.

Joe Thompson called out: 'What are you doing? — trying to kill him!'

It was clear that Hickin was hopelessly outmatched as a boxer, and Foley set out ruthlessly to punish him. The Riverine Herald man wrote that when the blow brought him to his knees, Hickin was bleeding, almost in the sponge. Immediately a wild crowd rushed to congratulate Foley. They took up a collection to show their appreciation of the fight. It was said later that one of those who came to congratulate Foley was a young dark man named Ned Kelly. It might actually have been possible — the Riverine Herald had reported two days earlier that the Kelly Gang had come down the river. 'Sergeant Vaughan of Moama has five white troopers who were hunting for Kelly around Gunbower.' 'The Sergeant is a fine dashing fellow,' said the Riverine Herald, 'and he would care very little for the Kellys if the skulking fellows had but the pluck to show themselves to anyone but people taken at a disadvantage.'

Foley went back to Sydney and loving admirers took up a testimonial. They presented him with a £1,000 and a gold watch.

The old-style bare knuckle fighting came to an end, literally, in 1884. On 17 April at dawn, there was a fight at the back of the Randwick racetrack, the traditional spot for prize ring battles. This was a fight between James Lawson of Sydney and Alex Agar of Melbourne. Agar was twenty-seven and not particularly experienced. He had come to Sydney for the horse-racing, had no money, so decided to try fighting. He wasn't a match for Lawson either in size or strength. There was a crowd of a hundred and twenty people at the fight and it lasted only seven rounds when Lawson gave Agar a mighty bare-fisted blow over the left eye. Agar staggered, fell and lay still on the turf. His seconds could not revive him, so they put him in a hansom cab and drove him to the Sydney Hospital, but he was dead before they arrived.

The police arrested Lawson, charged him with manslaughter and he went to jail for six months. There was such an uproar over this that bare-fisted fighting ceased in New South Wales.

Larry Foley had a close association with Jem Mace and between them they had a big influence in forcing the greater changeover to limited round bouts with boxing gloves under the Marquess of Queensberry Rules. Foley built his own boxing house, the Iron Pot, from iron and glass. It held eight hundred, and only rarely did the police interfere there.

The Marquess of Queensberry himself came to Melbourne in 1888, and the great man even agreed to referee a fight between Jem Burke and Maitland-born Frank Slavin, who in his time was a champion of Australia and England. The bout took place in the Hibernian Hall and the crowd was so immense that the Marquess couldn't get in to referee. The only thing to do was to crawl in through a back window. While he was getting up through the window the Marquess gave his valuable gold watch and chain, and his ticket, to a kind gentleman to hold for him. When he looked around the kind gentleman, plus the ticket, plus the gold watch and chain, had disappeared.

Another distinguished visitor in 1891 was John L. Sullivan, the heavyweight champion of the world. He appeared in a play at Her Majesty's Theatre in Sydney, a melodrama by Duncan B. Harrison called Honest Hearts and Willing Hands. One episode was 'The Contest', a carefully arranged glove fight between the villain, Jack Ashton, and the blacksmith, John Sullivan. The show had already toured America with considerable success and it had earned Sullivan $100,000.

When Sullivan arrived in Melbourne by the Sydney Express there were thousands waiting for him at the station. The crowd gave him three cheers and there were cries of 'Bravo for John L.!' He could barely make his way to the drag which was pulled by six horses.

There were stars and stripes outside the Royal Mail and Criterion Hotels and the footpath and windows of the Eastern Market were filled with people. The Melbourne Athletic Club was decorated with a huge picture of Sullivan above the stairs plus trophies, ribbons and flags. Sullivan, by this time, was three stone over weight and well on his way to being an alcoholic. He explained how careful he was with his drinking. He said his fans would allow him only two bottles of Bass a day. The Sportsman said, 'By stealth he would manage at least a dozen.'

Australians were more interested in seeing him fight than going through a charade on the stage. Frank Slavin did everything possible to taunt him into a fight, and the inducements went up to $20,000, but Sullivan insisted he was forbidden by his contract.

The Sportsman commented:

'Far better work could have been done with that man who in his capacity as an actor proved a failure in more ways than one and should have been manly enough to admit that he did not hit the taste of the Australian public.'

Freed of the old horror of the prize ring, boxing under the influence of Larry Foley, Sydney produced a remarkable series of boxers in world class. Peter Jackson, a black West Indian, learned his trade in Australia and could have become heavyweight champion of the world but for the American colour bar. He died in Roma, Queensland, on 21 July 1901, only forty, and the respect was so profound that the body was brought to Brisbane and buried in the Toowong cemetery. The funeral extended three quarters of a mile and all business houses along the route were closed.

Then there was Albert Griffiths, young 'Griffo', still considered by some as the fastest, the greatest natural boxing talent Australia ever produced. He was born in 1871 and brought up as a young tough in the Sydney 'Rocks' area. In 1890, at Larry Foley's Iron Pot, he beat the American, Billy Murphy, lightweight champion of the world. He did it so effectively and with such skill that Murphy, at the end of the fifteenth round, pulled off his gloves, flung them on the floor and said they had been tampered with. But Griffo took the decision.

So Griffo was the first of the Australian boxing 'immortals'. In 1893 he went off to make his fortune in America and his farewell was tremendous. All his friends from the Rocks 'Push' went to the boat, and it was such an emotional affair that Griffo, because he couldn't bear to leave them, dived overboard.

The stories about Griffo are legendary. He couldn't read or write. He would pretend to read newspapers and telegrams and frequently he held them upside down. Soon after his arrival from Australia he was walking through the streets of New York when he saw a painted calico sign 'GROTTO CAFE'. The words looked vaguely familiar and he paused staring at it. 'What's up, Griff?' said a friend.

'My word,' replied Griffo, 'they 'aven't bin long finding out I'm 'ere.'

Nat Fleischer thought him the fastest thinking, brainless boxer in pugilism. Fleischer recorded how Griffo had no understanding whatever about money. In his early bouts he was unconquerable and he made money so quickly he didn't know what to do with it. 'He was as dumb as an animal,' said Fleischer. His rake-off from a fight with George Lavigne in Chicago was $1,500. When the promoter began to count it off in dollar bills, Griffo stood and watched and became impatient. He raised a hand in protest when the five-hundred mark was reached and said: 'Enough! Wot yer think hi h'am, a bloomink truck? That's enough! Not on yer life, sir! Gimme that stuff. Hi'ts mine!'

The Americans never actually recognized Griffo's title. They said Murphy had no right to take it out of America. So to settle the matter, on 27 August 1894, he fought Jack McAuliffe, who the Americans now billed as the world lightweight champion. Griffo hit the champion where and whenever he wanted to, and Nat Fleischer said that Griffo definitely won the championship that night and every sports writer who saw the fight said the same thing. But the decision went to the American. It was Griffo's first defeat in a hundred and fifteen fights. The robbing of Griffo of his title has gone down in history as one of the great American crimes, like the 'poisoning' of Phar Lap and the death of Les Darcy.

Griffo never trained. He drank every dollar he earned, and when he had done that he had a special trick. He would put a penny on the floor and stand on it. Griffo would not raise his arms and would not move off the penny and he would challenge anyone to hit him on the jaw. His reflexes and his weaving ability were so extraordinary that they never could. Griffo would make his bet, 'drinks for the bar' if he got hit, and he would make that bet even if he had no money in his pocket.

So he deteriorated and from 1895 to 1897 he was being beaten by very ordinary boxers. He died in New York in poverty. He used to stand in a side entrance cadging dimes at the Republic Theatre on 42nd Street. The management would let him do this; it was his personal piece of territory where old fans would come to pay their respects.

For twelve years he lived in the basement room which belonged to a widow of an old friend of his fighting days. He paid no rent and Billy Gallagher, a cabaret man, gave him breakfast and supper through a standing order at a Seventh Avenue restaurant. He lived in poverty, but Nat Fleischer recalls that after his death his friends found that Griffo had been saving. He had a respectable sum in a Broadway bank.

A second immortal was Bob Fitzsimmons. He was born in Cornwall, was one of the most freckled and sandy-haired men who ever entered a prize ring, and he looked one of the most ordinary people alive. When he left Australia he did not even possess the Australian middleweight title. Jim Hall beat him for that on 10 February 1890.

However he went on to achieve more than perhaps any fighter in history. At various times this eleven-stoner, sometimes called the 'Freckled Freak', held the middleweight, light-heavyweight, and heavyweight world titles.

Newspapers in Australia reported Fitzsimmons' every move and every statement, and on 8 January 1895, the Sportsman even broke into verse.

The star of Fitzsimmons has risen
A meteor flaring in sight
Which scatters the clouds that imprison
And curb its impetuous fight.
From the land where the kangaroo races
And leaps with prodigious bound
Comes Robert the ruthless and graces
Our glorious ground.
For Robert with triumph is swelling
He smote a few pugs to the floor
Dan Creedon and Dempsey are telling
The terrible thumping they bore
And Robert they hail as a master
And Jim's in a perilous plight
And Corbett is doomed to disaster
Whenever they fight.

His biggest fight was against Jim Corbett for the heavyweight championship on St Patrick's Day, 1897. It was billed as 'The Fight of the Century'. The fight took place at Carson City, Nevada, and early on Fitzsimmons was in real trouble. Corbett knocked him down in the sixth round for a count of nine, and he had to hang on desperately to finish the round.

But then Fitzsimmons proceeded to out-box Corbett and the end came very suddenly. He crashed Corbett with a left to the stomach, and as he gasped for air, gave him the old Foley right king-hit to the jaw and knocked him out. The boxing boom had a new superhero. Fitz was 'champion of all the champions of the world'. The Referee, the leading sporting newspaper of the day, brought out a special edition with a Fitzsimmons-Corbett supplement. This was spread across four huge broadsheet pages. It was too early for photographs from Nevada, so they had mock-up sketches. There was the romantic sketch of Fitzsimmons knocking out Corbett, Fitzsimmons at the punching bag, Fitzsimmons' fighting stance, and an awe-inspiring sketch of a ferocious Bob titled 'Fitzsimmons fighting face'. English newspapers claimed him as an Englishman, New Zealand papers had him as a New Zealander, but the Sydney Referee insisted he was an Australian because we taught him all he knew.

Later they offered a 'grand illustrated portfolio' giving the entire history of the fight in eighty-one photographs on toned paper — 'a triumph of artistic taste'. Larry Foley himself said: 'No better work of its kind has ever been placed before the public.' The artistic triumph could be bought for one shilling and sixpence.

Fitzsimmons lost his heavyweight title to Jim Jeffries in 1899, but he won the light-heavyweight title in 1903. He had his last fight in 1914 when he was fifty-two years old.

The boom developed and blossomed. John Wren emerged as a fight promoter in Melbourne in 1905. He had made a fortune out of his illegal tote fortress in Collingwood, so that he was able to stage fights for big stakes. But the giant in this field was Hugh D. McIntosh, one of the most spectacular, toughest, go-getting entrepreneurs that Australia ever produced. Already he had made a fortune by the time he was twenty-one. He organized the sale of meat pies on Sydney's beaches, racecourses and sporting grounds. He didn't bake the pies himself but bought them from various bakers, hired the vendors to go to the selling points, and then handled the distribution. His organization expanded with startling speed and by 1903 he was into everything. He promoted bike-racing and he offered £1,000 for a world champion one-mile professional bike race.

He built the huge open air stadium at Rushcutters Bay, and it was then the biggest of its kind in the world. He built it for Australia's first officially recognized world title fight to take place between the heavyweight Canadian, Tommy Burns (real name Noah Brusso), and the Australian Bill Squires. Just three days before the bout McIntosh tested the stadium with a twenty-round bout between Sid Russell and Peter Felix with Burns as the referee.

Then on 24 August 1908, he had the world title bout between Burns and Bill Squires. A crowd of seventeen thousand saw Burns knock out Squires in the thirteenth round.

Hugh D. McIntosh was now supremely confident. In 1908 there had never been a black world champion. The great white champions like John L. Sullivan and Jim Jeffries had always drawn the colour line. A black-white world title fight was as much out of the question in the United States as it would be now in South Africa.

Burns, aged twenty-seven, fifty-two fights with only three losses, was the white world champion. Jack Johnson of Galveston, Texas, sixty-two fights, also with only three defeats, was the black champion of the world. He trailed Burns everywhere trying to draw him into a fight without success. The big negro was said to be an amiable man back home, a devout Methodist, a double bass player — but he was bitter about Burns. He claimed that Burns snubbed him in the London Sporting Club, refused to talk to him, and said that he had 'a yellow streak'.

Hugh D. McIntosh thought about it. He had put up £3,000 for the Burns-Squires match, so a battle between Burns and Jack Johnson was worth surely at least twice that much — £6,000, no, make it £7,000. Burns was to get the £6,000, Johnson £1,000 plus expenses. This was an enormous sum for a fight in 1908, and Burns could not refuse it. So the matter was settled, the fight to take place in Sydney on Boxing Day, 1908.

No fight before or since has attracted such interest. Both boxers, when they arrived in Sydney by train, were mobbed by vast crowds. For Jack Johnson they completely blocked the traffic in George Street.

Great crowds paid just to watch them doing their training at the stadium on Wednesdays and Saturdays, crowds of six to eight thousand. One time several thousand women were there — and it was simply not done for women to watch prize fights.

Seats for the fight were very expensive, ten shillings, £1, £3, £5, but from dawn on, Sydney flocked to Rushcutters Bay. Over twenty thousand crushed into the stadium and according to the Argus reporter, there was an even greater crowd outside. They climbed up telegraph poles, did everything possible to see inside.

There were actually four women in the crowd. Jack London, the author, had come to cover the story for the American newspapers, so Mrs London was there — the first woman to be admitted to a title fight in Australia. As a result of her distinguished presence, several others were allowed in also. One wore a boxer hat in the hope of disguising her sex.

Johnson stepped into the ring wearing a bathrobe. There was a mingling of boos, hoots and very faint applause. His early publicity had not been good. At one stage he said: 'I'm going to the races on Saturday afternoon, sure. Little Tahmmy's going to spend his afternoon in bed.'

When Burns stepped into the ring there was a deafening cheer from twenty thousand throats, which went for five minutes. As the Bulletin put it, they hadn't come so much for a fight, but to see a black contender for the title beaten to his knees and counted out.

As the cheering finished Johnson spat and with magnificent accuracy sent the phlegm between the heads of his seconds and a pressman.

Burns was dressed in an old blue suit and a battered green felt hat. He proceeded to take off these clothes and put them in a suit case.

Johnson then noticed that Burns was wearing bandages above the elbows. He told the referee, H. D. McIntosh, that he wouldn't fight if Burns did not remove the bandages. Burns refused. Hugh McIntosh announced to the crowd the cause of the delay and the crowd set up a vicious hooting. The Argus said it was frightening, like a battle cry. McIntosh then called on Larry Foley to give a ruling. Foley decided that Burns was breaking the rules by wearing the bandages, but why did Johnson worry? They were certainly of no help to Burns.

Suddenly Burns ripped off the bandages and he was cheered once more.

They met in the centre of the ring and it was an awe-inspiring sight. Johnson with his superb build was a black tower of a man, five inches taller and two stone heavier. Burns looked like a little boy in his teens.

'Aaal right Tahmmy,' said Johnson, and giving a grinning display of gold teeth, threw a left to Burns's ribs. Burns, very fast, caught a right at Johnson, which he took on his biceps. Burns sank his left into Johnson's stomach, they clinched and Johnson, breaking loose, swung an uppercut which crashed Burns right under the nose and lifted him off his feet, leaving him in a sitting position on the canvas.

This was the decisive blow of the fight. Burns, famed for his speed and ring-craft, never quite recovered, and from then on Johnson instituted a long process of torture. Burns jumped to his feet and in a dazed fury threw himself at Johnson, but the big black man held him off with ease, throwing lefts and right crosses.

'Ahh, poor little Tahmmy,' he jeered. 'Don't you know how to fight Tahmmy? They said you were a champion.'

Johnson amused himself by stiffening his abdominal muscles and letting Burns punch at them.

At the end of round one Burns was in a very bad way and his attendants rubbed him down with champagne. Johnson sat laughing and talking. 'Water,' he roared, and taking a drink that would have been useful for an elephant, he gargled and ejected a mouthful which splashed over the furious pressmen.

It wasn't until round four that Burns even faintly recovered from the knockdown of the first round. He then hurt Johnson with a right to the jaw, a blow over the heart and a right to the jaw, but even with this it couldn't be described as Burns's round.

'Then,' said the Bulletin writer, 'commenced the most heartrending exhibition of rubbing it in by a man determined to impress a crowd of white trash whose champion he was beating.'

The comments came all the time. For a photographer: 'Get that? I'll give you a good picture.' Then he'd smash Burns in the ribs and on the jaw.

And then: 'Say, little Tahmmy, you're not fighting. You can't? I'll show you how', and then there'd be a crash in the ribs.

In the ninth round: 'Are you going to fight Tahmmy?'

'Yes, you big dog.'

'Sure, but don't cry over it,' replied Johnson. 'I'll teach you something. Look at this, and this, and this.' Every word would be accompanied by another blow.

'Come on leedle Tahmmy,' he would taunt.

Burns's face all puffed on one side and his jaw hung down as if it had been broken.

'Jewel won't know you when you go home, will she?' said Johnson. Jewel was Burns's wife.

The Bulletin said that all through the fight a cinematograph operator, unemotional, monotonously kept winding the handle of his machine.

Then every time Burns returned to his corner his second carefully did his hair. As the commentator said, Burns might be losing his championship, the supremacy of the white race might be disappearing for ever, but at least it was important that he should die with his hair properly parted.

Of course, Johnson could have knocked out Burns at any time after the fourth round, but he wanted to prove something to the stunned silent crowd at Rushcutters Bay.

In the fourteenth round miraculously Burns seemed to make a slight recovery and threw some unhurtful blows at Johnson. This time Johnson went in to kill, he crashed blows at the sagging mouth and puffed cheek, Burns went down for a count of eight. Yet with astonishing courage he got up again. 'Finish him, Jack,' shouted the Johnson seconds.

Burns was helpless, like a condemned prisoner awaiting the headsman. Johnson swept in, bashed him against the ropes and was about to hit him again when a Superintendent of Police stepped in, shouting 'Stop Johnson!', waving his riding crop. McIntosh declared Johnson the winner on points. Johnson waved his arms to the crowd but they did not cheer. As one man said, a few straggling voices were raised but they were mere flecks of sound in an ocean of silence.

Jack Johnson however did not care. He said: 'I shipped him proper and I did it on purpose. I wanted to beat him down bit by bit and show him and the public how much yellow there was in him.'

Jack also doubted if Tommy Burns could do anything. For a £20 wager he was prepared to beat Burns at cycling, running, swimming, tennis, baseball, gold fossicking, bowls, sculling, piano, guitar, fiddle, banjo, car racing, and even archaeology, his hobby.

On the other hand Tommy Burns refused to complain. He had been beaten fair and square, he said. He had sprained his ankle after that knockdown in the first round and this made footwork impossible — but he wasn't making excuses.

The interest all over Australia was as intense as it had been in Sydney. The Argus published a noon-day picture of Collins Street — entirely blocked, cable trams stranded in the centre of the pushing crowd. They were there to watch the posting of round-by-round bulletins.

The next day was Sunday and the Reverend Henry Worrall gave his sermon at the Bendigo Methodist Church. He described it as 'a carnival of savagery and vindictiveness'. Two human brutes had fought with all the savagery and vindictiveness of Bengal tigers. The money raised could have been spent to build a new university or to send four thousand cultured and inspired missionaries to conquer paganism.

Then getting into his stride he said: 'After all the boasting and bragging and betting, those twenty thousand raving white Australians beheld their white champion beaten by the despised black man. Racial hatred has been set on fire. There will be racial reprisals and recriminations. They have by this deed put back the clock of history. God grant that the defeat on Saturday may not be a sullen and solemn prophesy that "Australia is to be outclassed and finally vanquished by these dark-skinned people."'

The third immortal was Les Darcy, who in the Australian folk-lore legend is rated with Carbine, Phar Lap, Ned Kelly and Don Bradman. He was born in Maitland in 1895 and at nineteen he was already a sensation in the district as a boxer.

His first appearance at Rushcutters Bay drew the biggest crowd since the Burns-Johnson fight. There had been some changes. Hugh D. McIntosh had sold out to Snowy Baker, the great all-round athlete, who floated a company in 1914 and established Stadiums Ltd. It wasn't an open air show anymore, having acquired a roof in 1912.

Always one referred to him as the 'Immortal Les Darcy'.

Les Darcy came to town to meet a top American fighter, Fritz Holland. Special trains came down from Newcastle and Maitland and the crowd was so great that not all could get in. Darcy fought superbly, but as a nineteen-year-old he just did not have enough experience to push home his advantages and Holland won on points after twenty rounds. The Maitland crowd was so angry they tried to burn down the stadium by lighting newspapers. Eventually the furious northerners had to be dispersed with fire hoses.

During 1915 and 1916 Darcy had only one loss in twenty-three bouts, and that was against Jeff Smith, and he beat him on the return bout. Although the Americans never recognized the title, Australians devotedly believed that by beating Smith, Darcy was effectively the middleweight champion of the world. He had an extraordinary fascination; every time he fought at Rushcutters Bay he drew a crowd of ten to fifteen thousand people. He was making enough money now to look after his parents, his brothers and sisters. He bought a 1915 model, six-cylinder Buick, one of the most expensive on the market. He toured everywhere in it, going home to Maitland, which was then a hazardous dirt track fit only for drays and wagons.

The awful truth about Les Darcy was this: he was performing with his fists and not his rifle. Actually he tried several times to enlist but he was under twenty-one and his mother refused to give her consent. Meanwhile he was receiving tempting offers from the United States, and here was a chance to put his world middleweight title beyond dispute. On 27 October, secretly and illegally, he left aboard the Heitie Luckenbach, bound for New York. He arrived under the name James Dawson and paid a $25 fine for being without a passport.

When they heard the news that he had flown Australia, newspapers turned on Darcy with extraordinary vindictiveness. Sydney's Sunday Times, now owned by Hugh D. McIntosh, was even tougher than most.

This paper regards with indignation the conduct of any man who bolts from his country at its hour of need. It regards with unspeakable contempt any and every person who connives in any way to such levanting. For a fellow who bolts in such a funk when he begins to deem it possible that his duty may be thrust upon him, there can be nothing but disgust and scorn.

The Argus in Melbourne said:

It is not that he refrained from volunteering that constitutes his obloquy against other men. It is that he ran away. The obloquy which he has incurred will follow him wherever he travels as long as he lives.

Stadiums Ltd stripped him of all his titles, and when the news spread around America Darcy found it impossible to get a fight. He was even branded as a 'slacker' by the Governor of New York.

After one fight in Australia Darcy had had his broken teeth riveted by primitive methods. In Memphis, Tennessee, he began to complain of sore teeth and gums. He came down with a streptococcus throat infection which led to blood poisoning. This affected his heart and he died on 24 May 1917.

That was the official version. Australia decided it was due to maltreatment by the Americans, and that he had died of a broken heart. Father Cody of Maitland said at his funeral:

The doctor said he died of blood-poisoning, the result of a complication of diseases. Those who loved him and knew him best, and I am one of them, say that he died of a broken heart. The pack that howled at him for those few months past had doubtless new and many more insidious snares laid for him and the Almighty in his tender mercy called him to his home before their malice could be further exercised.

The death of Darcy brought on an extraordinary public paroxysm of grief, brought on by the death of a hero, but also accentuated by a national feeling of guilt.

In Memphis, undertakers embalmed Darcy's body. The coffin was magnificent, the outer shell was pure aluminium, upholstered in white satin and inside was a glass case containing the body, dressed in a brown Carmelite habit.

So the remains returned to Australia aboard the American steamer Sonoma. Even at the arrival there was a great crowd waiting but only close relatives were admitted to the wharf.

The aluminium coffin arrived in a great oak box. Adorned with wreaths and a purple sash it went by horse-drawn lorry to the Mortuary Chapel of Wood, Coffill and Co., in George Street. For days Darcy's body lay in state. The top was removed from the aluminium coffin and it was possible to see the body down through the glass case. Francis J. Perry wrote: 'The remains resembled very little of the Les that we knew, the face being so thin and marble-like in appearance, indicating refinement and tenderness rather than pugnacity.'

Estimates on how many people passed through the funeral chapel vary from one to two hundred thousand. The Referee reported that twelve thousand actually went through in the few hours that were available on the first day. They moved through, men, women and children, at the rate of sixty a minute and this stepped up to two hundred a minute before 6 p.m. But on the second day there was a far greater crush. Four police inspectors and fifty policemen controlled the crowd so that they moved in one door and out the other. But the pressure was so great that pushing people broke the plate glass windows of Wood, Coffill and Co., and sometimes the trams had to stop running in George Street. Thousands stayed in the streets after closing time at 10 p.m. and not until the lights went out at 10.30 p.m. did they disperse.

That was Wednesday 27 June. On Thursday morning the body went to Woollhara, which had the biggest congregation for Requiem Mass it had ever known, then or since. The funeral took place in Maitland on the Sunday morning. From every kind of vehicle imaginable, people were making their way in from Sydney, Newcastle and beyond.

The Funeral March was two miles long, and the entire route from the Darcy home, several miles away, was lined with people. Even if one reduced the figure to a hundred thousand it would still be the greatest crowd that had ever gathered in the Northern district.

Before the coffin was lowered into the grave, the Very Reverend Father F. O'Gorman, who had been assisted by nine other priests, said:

This is the first time I have ever spoken in a cemetery, and in all human probability it will be the last. If my feeble voice could reach over this vast continent of people, every hand would go up when I asked, 'Are you glad the remains of Les Darcy are at home, and that the land that gave him birth will hold him forever to her breast?' It will always be a pleasant recollection for the rest of my life that I had something to do in bringing this about. There never has been in Australia a similar demonstration of sympathy and respect. The hand of providence is plainly visible in this affair.

Finally by public subscription a monument to his memory was erected on his grave.

That was the golden age of Australian boxing and passion for the sport never again reached such heights. It almost died in the 1920s. It revived in the thirties with stars like Ambrose Palmer, Fred Henneberry, Ron Richards and Jack Carroll. Vic Patrick drew big crowds in the 1940s, and but for the war years he may have been a world champion.

Dave Sands, a handsome Aboriginal from Kempsey, N.S.W., very nearly made the immortal class. He was Australian middleweight, light-heavyweight and heavyweight champion all at the one time. On 6 September 1949 he knocked out Dick Turpin in the first round to take the British Empire title. Many boxers such as Dick Turpin, Randy Turpin, Ray Robinson, Bobo Olson, believed he was certain to win the world middleweight title. But he was killed driving his truck near Dungog, N.S.W., on 11 August 1952. More than fifteen thousand people turned out in heavy rain for his Sydney funeral.

There is a folksong which is still popular among Aboriginals:

We've had some mighty fighters
In Australia in the past,
They were all good men of the glove game,
Clever, hard and fast,
But there's one mighty fighter
Who took the highest stand,
He's known the world over
As Australia's Davey Sands.

Australia's first official world champion was Jimmy Carruthers. He came from Woolloomooloo and he learned his fighting the hard way. He began to show his great class when he beat the world-rated Aboriginal Elly Bennett on 15 May 1951 at the Sydney Stadium. The most excited person there was Carruthers's four foot ten inch mother, who called herself the paperweight champion of Paddington. She told reporters that she had given him boxing lessons herself so that he could hold off the neighbourhood bullies. She asked the reporters to look at her still scarred knuckles. 'I hit him a bit too hard one night,' she said, 'and these two knuckles swelled up. The doctor told me I had arthritis and I was too ashamed to tell him what really happened.'

Carruthers won the world bantamweight title when he knocked out Vic Toweel of South Africa in two minutes nineteen seconds in the first round. The bout took place in Johannesburg on 15 November 1952. The films showed later that Carruthers threw 147 punches. Toweel threw only one and it missed. Carruthers eventually retired as undefeated champion of the world, though he later staged a disastrous comeback.

Then came two modern immortals — Lionel Rose and Johnny Famechon. Lionel Rose became bantamweight champion of the world when he beat Masahiko 'Fighting' Harada on 27 February 1969. He came home to an extraordinary triumph. He received the M.B.E., at nineteen he became the 'Australian of the Year', and found he couldn't go anywhere, do anything, without being followed by reporters and photographers. He complained he only had to sneeze and it was news. Had Sir Henry Bolte gone to London, and won amnesty from taxes for all Victorians until the end of time, his reception would have been half the size of that received by Lionel Rose when he returned from Tokyo. The Premier, the Lord Mayor, the great of Melbourne were waiting for him at the Town Hall. He was asked to open bridges, to kick off at football matches. The bewildered Rose asked, 'Who really knows why 250,000 people would want to line Melbourne's streets to see a nineteen-year-old boxing champion?'

He had a letter from a Melbourne woman who said her husband had been dying of cancer. He rallied enough to listen to the Tokyo fight and was overjoyed at the decision. The dying man said, 'I'm happy now', and died two days later. There was another story about an eighty-year-old nun at Columbus Convent in Essendon who became so excited as she listened that she fought out the battle with Lionel, blow for blow.

There was one protester in a letter to the Herald. He wrote:

It would appear we are ushering in Australia Day 1969 by bowing down before a new God — the professional fighter. The man who can smash another human being into a state of near-insensibility for an exceedingly large sum of money, is now being hailed as the conquering hero of our country. What an idea to set before the youth of Australia as they are about to enter upon a new scholastic year.

The writer, Herbert Mishael, of Melbourne, spoke of the poor rewards for art, literature, music, science, invention and discovery and added: 'Perhaps one day their leading exponents may hope to attain the measure of favour now being accorded to two bash artists.'

That autumn the committee of the Moomba Festival chose Tito Gobbi to be the King of Moomba, the crowned head of the holiday procession. The Elizabethan Trust Opera Company brought Gobbi to Melbourne for the role of Falstaff. There were hundreds of letters to Melbourne newspapers suggesting the wrong man had been chosen. Melbourne wanted Lionel Rose as King. One letter said: '... are we so ashamed of our Aboriginals that we should choose anyone other than a true Australian as Moomba King?'

Tito Gobbi, very offended, abdicated, and no one could be persuaded to retire and, after much pleading, Moomba had two kings. Gobbi wore the traditional ermine and gold cap while Lionel Rose rode near the front of the cavalcade with his mother and his trainer, Jack Rennie.

Rose defended his title against Takao Sakurai. The Sun News-Pictorial printed his picture on the front page, a supplement, and other pages in between.

Rose lost his title in Los Angeles to Ruben Olivares on 23 August 1969. But he remained a hero. The Sun News-Pictorial in Melbourne published this message: 'Come home with your head high Lionel Rose. Shake off the depression of defeat. Do not feel you have let us down. No one doubts your courage. No one doubts your skill.'

So when he returned home defeated, there were still two thousand fans waiting at Melbourne airport. Little girls and old ladies rushed to kiss him on the cheek.

Johnny Famechon won the featherweight title on 21 January 1969. He came home to a traditional welcome from the airport — along the route there were cries of 'Good on you Johnny', 'You beaut!', 'Well done son.' Famechon explained what it was like to be world champion. 'The telephone didn't stop ringing, night or day. Radio spots, parties, social functions, sporting events, parties of honour ... I went back and found that it was absolutely impossible to cope with it all.'

Famechon lost his title to Vincente Saldívar in Rome on 9 May 1970, but he had made enough money for the rest of his life and like Griffo, Fitzsimmons, Darcy and Rose, he had, of course, acquired immortality. Had he merely been a great poet or musician, few people would have heard of him.
