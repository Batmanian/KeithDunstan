The Pedalling Passion

41 min read.

First published 1973

Traces Australia's craze for the bicycle from its 1880s arrival through the golden age of professional racing at the Melbourne Cricket Ground, profiling the scandals of 'Plugger' Bill Martin, the dignity of 'Major' Taylor, the transcontinental records of Hubert Opperman, and the tragic road death of Russell Mockridge.

The extraordinary passion for the bicycle which swept Australia in the 1880s and 1890s is long forgotten. Yet this is a fascinating piece of social history, an era when a whole nation took to wheels, carried off by a marvellous, joyous craze.

This was the new, easy, cheap transport, a new way of life, a liberating force. Many thought bicycling a sin, a dangerous pastime discovered by the young to irritate their elders. Parsons railed against the bicycle from the pulpit. Cyclists sinned against God by bicycling on Sundays; even worse, they sinned by bicycling when they should have been at church. Then men and women went off into the country together on bicycles and there were terrible fears about this. Heaven knows what took place behind the hedges, and there were worries that the bicycle could cause a total collapse in the nation's morals.

Cycling also was an irritant, a tiresome newcomer which detracted from the old established sports. Geelong Grammar School formed a bicycle club in 1881 and the teachers complained this was a threat to football, rowing and cricket. An even more serious objection was that, 'boys in bicycles are practically out of control of the school'.

There were thirty-two different makes of bicycles on the market and if one went out to a dinner party there was talk of nothing else but Humbers, Raleighs, Rovers, Raglans and Rudge-Whitworths. A Rover would cost £28 10s., a Humber £30, a Rudge-Whitworth £27, a Raglan £26 and a Rapid £25 and £21. But the demand was so enormous that the best makes were simply not to be had—importers could not keep pace.

They were so popular that every firm was trying to get a piece of the action. The boom in Sydney reached its peak in the 'nineties, and by that time there were large bicycle depots in the city. In the suburbs everyone was selling them—tailors, booksellers, piano dealers, music sellers, even druggists. Doctors would advise the use of the bicycle as the one cure-all, the panacea. 'Buy a bicycle', they would say, as the patient left the surgery.

The original bicycle was the old 'hobby horse' which gained a vogue in England and France in the 1830s. The rider literally sat on two wheels and propelled himself along with either foot on the ground. Then in 1861 Pierre Lallamont and Pierre Michaux of Paris fitted pedals to the front wheel of a hobbyhorse. They had seen the possibilities of the cranked axle, like the stone of a grindstone, and so they became 'les inventeurs et propagateurs du vélocipède à pédale'.

This was a dramatic step forward; the rider of a bicycle could now stay erect without having a foot on the ground. A few years later these bicycles caused a sensation in some parts of Queensland. Citizens who knew nothing about velocipedes would see the single tracks along the ground, no footmarks, nothing—just the long, long, mark left by apparently single wheel. How did it get there?

The velocipedes smartly won the name 'boneshakers'; they had wooden wheels, wooden spokes, iron bearings, solid iron tyres, no springs. The front wheel was slightly larger than the rear and the outfit was almost guaranteed to shake the teeth out of a man's head. Yet they were strong, often beautifully made, and could be bounced safely over a kerb. This was hardly surprising for a boneshaker could weigh up to a hundred pounds. Compare this with the fifteen and a half pound models used by racers at the turn of the century.

Velocipedes became the rage of Europe and America, the sensation of the Paris exhibition of 1867. They first appeared in Melbourne in 1868, ridden by Professor Kernot. Later when he became vice-president of the Victorian League of Wheelmen, he told how he rode his boneshaker, his 'wary wobbler', about the streets of East Melbourne. One day he passed John Pascoe Fawkner, Melbourne's founder, who was riding in a carriage. Fawkner looked in astonishment at the 'demonstration of hostility by this novel vehicle'. The Professor said Fawkner died soon afterwards, but whether the novel sight was too much for him and caused his demise he was not prepared to say.

Mr W. A. George, late president of the Sydney Bicycle Club, built one in Bathurst in 1867, the first in New South Wales and probably in Australia. But his boneshaker did not catch on. People thought it a joke.

Melbourne was the first city to find the bicycle irresistible. There was actually a bicycle race on the Melbourne Cricket Ground on 10 July 1869. There was a crowd of 'curious spectators' which various journals estimated at ten to twelve thousand. J. Finlay of Fitzroy and R. Warnock of Emerald Hill fought out a speed test. Finlay covered the last two miles of the race in 7 minutes 19½ seconds to win by a lap. As far as speed tests go, Ron Clarke on foot would have beaten Finlay comfortably by a lap.

Nor was the Australasian impressed: 'Velocipedes have had their trial and the verdict is that as a means of creating sport they are failure ... The universal opinion is that they won't do.' The Australasian was convinced that the single wheel machine was the real answer.

Unicycles, when their management is understood, will show the shine out of bicycles, as I am told that in competent riders they can be worked up to the rate of 40 m.p.h. I suppose the next thing we shall hear of some mountebank running one across the falls of Niagara on a single rope.

The mountebanks of Melbourne were a far greater problem, because the massive, over-weight boneshakers had no brakes and once they started to hurtle down hills the result at the bottom, frequently, must have been awe-inspiring.

The boneshaker reached its peak of popularity in 1872-73, and in 1875 came to Melbourne the revolutionary 'spider' wheel. This bike, an English invention, had an all metal wheel with an enormous number of spokes of single pieces of wire, one end clamped to the rim, the other to the hub.

Messrs Mason and Clarke of South Yarra were the importers and velocipedists from all over Melbourne gathered to inspect them. They thought 'spiders' were flimsy beyond belief. They had this large wheel in front and the small wheel at the rear, which was a stabilizer, nothing more. The velocipedists made bets that the big wheel would collapse and the driver fall through it before it had gone half a mile.

But the penny farthing, of course, was a phenomenal success. Distances of up to fourteen and a half miles were covered in a single hour. On the first day there was great debate as to who would be brave enough to try one. Messrs Mason, Hobson and Bagot took the plunge.

Mr R. C. Bagot, the secretary of the Victoria Racing Club, designer of Flemington racetrack and the Melbourne Cricket Ground, was a bicycling fanatic. He was one of the first to buy a boneshaker and by 1885 he had purchased fourteen bicycles. As soon as a new improved machine came on the market he had to have one.

The new spider, penny farthing, or the ordinary bike, as it was called in the 'eighties, came in various sizes. The enormity of the front wheel depended on the length of one's legs. In the Austral Wheel Race of 1890, Mr Gordon of Gippsland rode the 'tallest machine in the world'. It was sixty-five inches high, while Gordon himself was six feet four inches.

But there were all sorts of cycles apart from spiders. There were quadricycles, tricycles, two-wheelers-abreast, machines with all sorts of curious cranking devices. Some had pedals and hand cranks, and then there were the sociable two-wheelers in which the lady and gentleman sat side-by-side just as they were going to church in a gig.

Then, with all this enthusiasm there had to be clubs. There was an early Boneshaker Club which left little in the way of records. The first important club in Australia was the Melbourne Bicycle Club formed on 1 August 1878.

There were the rules of the M.B.C.:

Annual subscription twelve shillings and sixpence.

That the club uniform consist of myrtle green coat, knickerbockers, stockings and cap, and silver badge. Members are requested to wear the same at Club meetings and when touring. White straw hats with club ribbon (green and gold) to be worn in summer. Club costumiers Alston & Brown Collins Street West. That the uniform and badge be not worn on Sundays, except on runs extending over several days.

Regulations.

  1. The captain, or in his absence the vice-captain, or any member of the committee shall act as leader in every excursion, and his directions shall be obeyed.
  2. That the vice-captain in all runs shall keep the rear, and signal (one whistle) to the leader whenever the tail of the company is getting out-distanced. On hearing the signal the leader shall slacken till he receives a second whistle (two whistles), indicating that they have closed up.
  3. That the strictest order be maintained during all runs.
  4. That when riding in single file, an interval of two yards shall be kept between each rider, and when in double file, three yards. In hilly country these distances should be doubled.
  5. That each member is requested to carry a bell or whistle and to use a lamp when riding at night.

BUGLE CALLS. Attention. The Assembly or Prepare to Mount. Mount. Dismount. Form Single File. Form Twos. Dinner Call.

Attention should be sounded before the Assembly or Dinner Calls, to indicate to riders that they are official.

It was interesting that the club did not want members to wear their badge or their uniforms on Sundays. The Presbyterian General Assembly in Melbourne condemned the bicycle as 'evil' for its desecration of the Sabbath.

The president of the Melbourne Bicycle Club was a Judge of the Supreme Court, Mr Justice Williams. He was a dignified gentleman with mutton-chop whiskers, but there was no limit to his devotion to the bicycle. He would ride his machine on circuit, all around Victoria. The Judge even gave accounts of his travels to the bicycle journals. In 1883 he wrote:

Friday it was blowing a westerly gale, but not raining, though the gale was in my teeth. I was determined to have a ride of some sort, so I mounted my safety at 9.30 and reached Camperdown at 1 o'clock, you can imagine the force of the wind when I tell you I had to work nearly as hard downhill as I have ever had to work up hill. It was a real hard plug the whole way and the much talked of impetus on the Stoney Rises did not fall to my experience. However I rode the distance, 29 miles, without a dismount.

From there he continued his tour to Warrnambool, to Hamilton, to Ararat, to Moyston, to Stawell, to Horsham, to Beaufort. He would be met by officials on the way and he would preside over cases in each town. He rode a 'safety machine', one of the brand new chain-driven machines similar to the modern bicycle. At Hamilton he received a guard of honour from the Hamilton Bicycle Club. There he was met by Mr Farroll, a gentleman six feet four inches, who was riding his sixty inch 'spider ordinary'. 'I on my safety looked like Lilliput beside him on his steed,' wrote the Judge.

The newspapers gave him a very bad time. The Herald said:

That the whole legal profession is scandalized at the burlesquing of the dignity of the judicial bench by Mr Justice Williams going circuit on a bicycle. What a contrast, they say, to the pomp and ceremony attending the entry of the judges in English assize towns, where they ride in splendid equipage and are received by a High Sheriff and other officials, and the Judges attend church in State on the Sunday. Fancy a Judge of the Supreme Court of Victoria entering a large city like Ballarat on a velocipede and being met, not by the sheriff and other functionaries, but by half a dozen boobies on bicycles.

Bicycling News rushed to his defence:

I desire to point out that cycling stands at the very head of athletics in England, and that its votaries are generally of good status. In Great Britain alone there are 300,000 riders, among whom may be mentioned the Prince of Wales, Prince George of Wales, Lord Sherbrooke and two of Her Majesty's judges of the Queen's Bench, as well as a host of noblemen and gentlemen. The judges mentioned frequently do their circuits on bicycles.

The newspapers were continually filled with prodigious feats performed on the bicycle. A. Edward of the Sydney Bicycle Club left Sydney on 23 May 1884, and rode the appalling 532 mile track to Melbourne in eight and a half days. He did the 195 miles from Albury to Melbourne in three days. He had many bad falls during his trip, but he had nothing but praise for his bike, a fifty-two inch Bayless and Thomas 'ordinary'. He did not have to use a spanner on it from the time of starting to the finish.

Cyclists like Mr Edward did so well on the high bike that when the modern safety bikes arrived many people had to be persuaded to ride them. Australian Cycling News said:

To the man who had been in the habit of straddling a 56-inch wheel apparently it is a come down to take to a 36-inch mount. [One] finds out, however, that it is only a matter of getting used to safety and ease. This has been demonstrated by the great feats that have been accomplished in the antipodes by the pygmy machines.

At the beginning women suffered. How were they to get into this craze? It really wasn't proper for a lady to ride astride a bicycle. One writer suggested that while in the United States perhaps the Bloomers and the Mary Walkers might take to 'velocipedestrination', European ladies would prefer propriety to sensation. 'It would no doubt be mighty pleasant to go out velocipeding with your friends, each mounted on her own bicycle, but custom and nature revolt against it.'

Originally there were some side-saddle boneshakers, but they were useful for nothing else but going downhill. The only real alternative was the tricycle, and particularly, the tricycle sociable designed for two. So the journals were full of tales of deserted wives, left at home while their men toured the country on wheels. Cycling poets even put the delicate matter into verse:

Oh! Ladies, dear ladies, be cold as an icicle,
To each man who you fancy is fond of a tricycle,
Be deaf to his tender pathetic appeals
If he cherish a weakness for riding on wheels.

For oh! if you marry, beware of the day
When he'll mount on his wheels and be off and away;
And you'll pine and you'll fret in your bower alone,
And you'll see him afar like a king on his throne.

Then take warning by me and be cold as an icicle,
To a man who is guilty of riding a tricycle,
Except—and indeed, the exception is notable—
He will sell his old 'sulky' and purchase a Sociable.

Ladies riding bicycles was bad enough, but when they took to racing it was quite horrifying. In 1885 the South Australian Bicycle Club held a championship meeting in Adelaide and this included a ladies' tricycle race. The victor was Miss Wills, who won a trophy valued at four guineas. She covered three miles in 9 minutes 43 seconds off a handicap of 395 yards. Even the cycling journals were shocked:

Where would we ask is the man who would care to see his sister, or maybe his mother-in-law, straining her sinews on a tricycle racing before an assemblage of people? What modesty could a lady possess to undertake such a performance? And how edifying to the public mind ...

One way to make bicycling respectable was to stress continually how the Royal Family, how the Royal Ladies, how the inmates of the stately vice-regal houses all took to wheels. In Melbourne the Governor and Lady Brassey were constantly seen on their 'safety' machines. Indeed the whole vice-regal house would turn out, Lord Richard Neville, Captain Neville, R.N., Mrs Neville, Sir Hartley Williams, Commander Collins, all on their bikes.

Frequently Viscount Hampden would be seen riding his bicycle in George Street, Sydney, steering his way down past omnibuses, steam trains, through narrow streets. Melbourne Punch thought him a better rider, actually, than Lord Brassey, who had hit a 'Chinaman's handcart'.

In Brisbane, the Governor, Lord Lamington, cycled, and so did Lady Lamington. Review of Reviews reported that as soon as Lady Lamington took to wheels the ladies of Brisbane swarmed to the various agencies to buy their machines. Lord Lamington's bicycle was so splendid half Brisbane turned out to admire it.

One intriguing piece of information dropped into the social notes was this: Princess Maud of Wales received forty-eight bicycles among her wedding presents. Twenty-four of them came with the message from the manufacturers that should she find this type of frame unsuitable they would be happy to provide a different model.

The advertisements were directed straight at the cycling ladies. 'Messrs George & George beg to inform the cycling public that they are prepared to execute bicycle suits and every description of outfitting and drapery in the latest, most approved style.' Stainer's of Swanston Street, Melbourne, had the Ryde Cycling Skirt, Patent 95043. 'This Skirt, for either Lady's or Gentleman's Bicycle is pronounced by leading riders to be the only Costume which complies with all the requirements of the Lady Cyclist.'

Then there were the special cycling foods. There was 'Cykola', the new cycling beverage. Kola Nut, the cycling nut from West Africa, well-known for its great sustaining power under prolonged exertion. Raisins and peppermints, too, should be used as 'chest and lung clearers'.

Sydney bicycling boomed in the 'eighties, sagged, and then made a great comeback from 1892. In 1895 there was the inaugural meeting of the Sydney Ladies Bicycle Club. The Australian Cycling Annual reported that they had to endure much ridicule, much derision and 'gross insult from ignorant and coarse-minded people'.

However, the lady cyclists were capable of remarkable feats. In October 1899 two women cyclists, Mrs Donald McDonald and Mrs C.A. Birkin, set out to ride from Sydney to Melbourne. There was an accident on the very first day. Mrs Birkin was riding downhill when she went into a bed of sand, fell off and injured her arm. She could not go on and the resolute Mrs McDonald continued alone. She found the country between Goulburn and Yass very hilly, with some particularly long steep climbs. From then on the roads were merely bush tracks, often too rough even for a bicycle, so there were long stretches where she had to walk.

Cycling was much better after she passed Albury and she did the 103 miles from Glenrowan to Kilmore in one day. She covered the entire distance, 577 miles, in seven days four hours, and according to Melbourne Punch it was one of the longest rides ever undertaken by a solitary cyclist.

The pneumatic tyre first reached Melbourne in 1890 and it caused great amusement. The tyres and wheels received such names as 'sausage rolls', 'balloons' and 'steam rollers'. The jump from the half-inch belt of solid rubber to balloons several inches in diameter was too much. But Melburnians quickly found that they had a superb advantage; not only did they give an easy ride, but they did not become caught in the cable tram slot.

The Australian Cyclist estimated that in 1894 there were eight thousand cyclists in Melbourne alone, while Review of Reviews claimed there were seventeen thousand in the entire colony of New South Wales. One journal even suggested that the traffic would become so thick that eventually the city would have to build special tracks underground to preserve lives. The new bicycles were so fast that cyclists were not only killing themselves, they were killing others, and pneumatic tyres with their noiseless approach made them a bigger menace than ever. Melbourne Punch commented:

His speed ranges up to a rate of 20 m.p.h. and it need hardly be said after he knocks down some quiet pedestrian he puts forth his best efforts to 'beat the record' in getting out of sight. The modern bicycle is a deadly Car of Juggernaut with this difference—that the victims are unwilling ones and are sent without warning to become the subjects of Dr Youl's jokes and inquests.

Yet through all the journals there is the over-riding sense of the joy and pleasure that had been created by the bicycle. The amount of bad poetry that was produced was remarkable. Take this one, written for the Melbourne Bicycle Club Easter Tour of 1885:

The wheelman's life is free from care
If roads be smooth and winds are fair;
Then let us mount, the steed so light,
With flashing nickel glist'ning bright.
We'll bowl along Victoria's roads today
With limbs so strong, and hearts so light and gay.
Then let's have a song lads as we pedal on so free
For a jolly band of cycling boys are we.

But my favourite piece of cycling verse is this:

O sweet are sounds of water, and sweet
The distant echoes of welcome feet,
The wild bird's singing, the hum of bees,
The night wind's rustle in summer trees;
But none, to my ear, in sweetness excell
The wee tink-tink of the bicycle bell.

All this passion for cycling naturally produced some extraordinary competition. For nearly thirty years Australia was the home of professional cycling. Here the crowds were the greatest, the prizes the most inviting and some of the world's best cyclists were drawn to compete. The classic event was the Austral. It took place at the Melbourne Cricket Ground and it became the Melbourne Cup of cycling. It even had the same social prestige. The Governor and society would be there, all the best people complete with what they wore were listed in the social papers, just as on Melbourne Cup day.

The first Austral was on 30 January 1886 at the M.C.G. The first prize was a grand piano worth £200 and the race was a handicap over three miles. There were few racing bicycles available in 1886, so that anyone who rode a machine over forty-six pounds in weight, received a handicap of thirty yards in the mile extra. There were a hundred and forty-four entries for the meeting. In those days the low-to-the-ground safety bikes were unpopular, chiefly because of their manoeuvrability. They would zip in and out of their towering competitors who would crash to the ground like tall timber. The New South Wales Union even passed a resolution forbidding the safeties to compete against the high bikes in open races.

For the first four years the Austral was an amateur event, but from 1890 onwards the trophies were in cash, and to the horror of many members of the Melbourne Cricket Club, the bookmakers moved in and the stately ground had all the atmosphere of Caulfield racetrack. So with big cash prizes to be won, the world champions began to arrive. Arthur Zimmerman, 'Zimmie', of New Jersey, champion of the world, undefeated from one mile up to six miles in America, England and France, arrived in 1895. So tremendous was his reception that sixty-five thousand people paid £3,301 to see him at a two-day meeting in Sydney that November.

Yet the most extraordinary of the world champions to race in Australia was 'Plugger' Bill Martin. 'Plugger' was born in Dublin, went to Detroit when he was three, had red hair and an old-fashioned, fiery Irish temperament. He raced everywhere; in the United States, West Indies, South America, Honolulu, South Africa, North West Africa, Spain, Portugal, Italy, Germany, England and New Zealand. Best of all he liked Australia.

'Plugger' was his best known nickname, but he had others: 'The Iron Man', 'The King of the Handicap Kids', 'The World's Marvel', 'The Cast-Iron Boy from Detroit', 'The World's Master Personality', the man you had to see, the big draw at all the carnivals. In races 'Plugger' gave the crowd their money's worth because he was never out of trouble.

The events which the Adelaide Advertiser called the 'disgraceful proceedings', all began in Adelaide during the carnival of October 1896. Alec McDonnell, another American cyclist, was chasing Martin hard on the edge of the track, when he swerved into him and they went against the fence. McDonnell deliberately charged that Martin had knocked him right on the track. We had an inquiry. McDonnell sided with Martin and suspended him for two months.

'Plugger' denied that he ever hit his colleague, but seeing that it was not unusual for him to hit people, his plea was not believed, and the penalty was enhanced. It was further enhanced yet again the following year in Sydney when at a professional meeting a spectator in his brilliant orange racing costume jumped off his bike and chased the man through the crowd and thrashed him. The New South Wales League of Cyclists thought him extremely innocent and exonerated him.

The following year he was back in Adelaide for the 1897 carnival. Still very much about, in the open scratch race when he was leading well, he had a spasm. He waved to the Australian champion, Joe Megson, 'Go on Joe, I'm done'. Megson, who had been disqualified the previous March through 'Plugger', succeeded and won by six lengths. Martin then rode slowly round the ground in a furious demonstration from the punters. Some shouted: 'Fall down Bill'.

The following week the League of South Australia Wheelmen held an inquiry. One of the referees, Mr W.F. Ward, opened up well, and turning to 'Plugger', said:

'I want to ask Darky here something that occurred when I was disqualified for two months — was he given £10 by Alec McDonnell and what was it for?'

Mr Ward: 'It is an absolute lie.'

'Am I to be judged by him?'

'The Chairman: 'Do you object to Ward sitting at this meeting?'

'I do, very much.'

The Chairman, however, ruled that Ward had to stay. Mr R. Cruikshank, one of the referees at Saturday's racing, was the first witness. He said no one alive had ever insulted him the way Martin had insulted him on Saturday. Martin had come over and this conversation followed.

Martin: 'You are nothing but a scoundrel.'

Cruikshank: 'Thank you.'

Martin: 'I intend to insult you. You insulted me on the track and I intend to make it a personal matter to insult you and all the rest of them.'

Other witnesses said that he had called them all 'low scoundrels' and that they always treated him like a dog in Adelaide.

The League fined Martin a total of £4, £1 each for a series of charges. The judges for the carnival felt insulted by the verdict. They thought it inadequate and refused to act for the next day of the meeting on Saturday. Meanwhile 'Plugger' Bill counter-attacked with a letter to the League:

Mr J. Gazard, secretary, L.S.A.W.

Sir, I respectfully ask the League to donate me £25 to defray expenses caused by decisions. If my wish is not fulfilled you may scratch me for every event.

Yours faithfully, W. MARTIN, American Cyclist.

This was a magnificent piece of effrontery, a calculated threat, like Phar Lap scratching himself from the 1930 Melbourne Cup. It seemed that the L.S.A.W. managed to pacify him. 'Plugger's' next move was to distribute handbills around the streets of Adelaide on the Friday:

ATTENTION TO PUBLIC OF SOUTH AUSTRALIA

This is to certify that I will not be competing in the cycle races on the Exhibition Oval on Saturday, October 16, as advertised by others.

W. MARTIN, American Cyclist.

Oh yes, 'Plugger' was bringing a record crowd to the ground for it was he who had engaged for four races. He consulted a lawyer and rode out after his heat in the first class mile on an Electric roadster, equipped with a tool bag, pump and bell. He rode up the track ringing the bell merrily as he went.

The crowd received him with mixed boos and cheers. In the next event, the League Handicap, he appeared without colours or numbers. He stayed hard for a lap, then retired. He scratched from the other events, claiming that he was injured.

The extraordinary story continued in the Adelaide Police Court on the Monday morning. 'Plugger' was on trial. Mr Frank P. Ridley, who had been a member of the Australian League's Record and Racing committee in the McDonnell affair of 1896. Witness after witness appeared. On the previous Wednesday morning Ridley and Martin had been at the track training. Martin came in first to the changing room, fifty or sixty other people followed him. Then, as Ridley described it:

'Gentlemen, I want to ask this man a question and I want you all to hear what he says. You remember McDonnell being here last year?'

'Yes,' Ridley replied.

'He gave you £10, what did he give it to you for?'

Martin did not wait for an answer. He dragged Ridley off a form and proceeded to hit him. He cracked him twenty times with an open hand, very hard. Ridley did not put up any resistance.

'Plugger' asked anyone in the room, asked them if they would object to the same treatment, or whether they had anything to say.

Ridley appeared in the court with two bad black eyes and he said he could still hear ringing in his ears. Mr Paris Nesbit explained that the blows were merely with an open hand.

Counsel for Ridley, Mr Symon, said: 'Yes, with a cyclist's thumb. Let him exhibit his thumb. It's like a kick from a young horse, I believe.'

Ridley denied vigorously that he had been bribed and the court was on his side. Mr James Gordon sentenced 'Plugger' Bill to fourteen days' imprisonment with hard labour.

Had Martin committed murder, or even appeared before a Victorian Football Tribunal, he could have not received more space in the newspapers. Every small detail was covered. A Register reporter interviewed him after the sentence. He found 'Plugger' in the court yard, he was standing there with the diamond stud missing from his shirt front. 'You've lost your stud, Martin,' said the reporter. 'Oh, they've relieved me of those things,' Plugger replied sententiously. 'I'll never race again. I'm going to quit. I intended to do so before. Can't do anything straight on the track now, they seem to think I've made too much money. That's the size of it.'

Martin served thirteen of his fourteen days. He came out on a Saturday because of an Adelaide Jail rule that no prisoner is discharged on the Sabbath. Once again the reporters were waiting for his comments:

'Well, I enjoyed myself down there. It is the most complete rest I have had in years. I was treated the same as the others, but I can't recommend the tucker you have served out to you. You had to imagine that you had enough. Of course I wore a belt and kept pulling it up a notch as the day wore on.'

'Plugger' admitted that he had made £1,000 since he had been in Australia, and he was worth more money than all the Australian cycling leagues put together. The Register commented:

The sentence passed (on Martin) was richly deserved. This man has been taught that bullying and bounce—even from conceited professionals—will not be tolerated by the conductors of South Australia sports.

The South Australian League disqualified him for six months, and various other disqualifications put him out for two years. One journal said: 'Exit William Martin, the best cyclist ever seen in Australia.'

'Plugger' Bill swore he was finished with Australia for good, but he returned in 1899. He suffered for a time from injuries, but he really came good the following year. At the 1900 Austral at the M.C.G. he won eight of nine events on the first day. On the second day he won the ten mile scratch race and on the last he took the mile handicap and the five mile scratch race. His total winnings were £278.

Everyone was ecstatic. 'Plugger' was again the favourite, just like old times, and The Sportsman even printed his glory in verse:

When we went to see the cycling on the Melbourne Cricket Ground,
How we hollered and shouted as The Plugger whistled round.
With his 'trilbies' both a-movin as quickly as you like,
And his 'youthful' hands a-grippin both the handles of his bike.
With his auburn hair a-floatin' like a banner on the breeze
With his quarters up to heaven, and his head between his knees;
With his body all a-wobblin' and his features stern and set—
Well, the Plugger made a picture I never will forget.

The Sportsman said:

Martin must be given the bread, cake, pastry—ay, the whole bakery. For being the best all round man in Australia. Martin and they go again. A new set appears. But Bill is in the lead.

But 'Plugger' was soon back at his old tricks. He was fined £5 for suspicious riding at Charters Towers in Queensland and the Queensland League disqualified him for three months. When the Queensland executive ratified the decision, it said that if the circumstances were as reported then the suspension should have been for life.

Then came the famous Austral Wheel Race of 1901. The Sportsman wrote of it glowingly:

There was never such a burst of excitement on the Cricket Ground, there was never such a stirring crowd, there was never a more perfect day, there was never a better Austral wheel race, there was never so much money taken at the turnstiles, there was never so much money at stake in a cycling race anywhere in the world.

'Plugger' arrived by steamer called the Austral and stayed at the Austral Hotel, so all the omens were beautifully set for the Austral Wheel Race. But he was getting on now in years and it hardly seemed possible for him to triumph yet again. How old was he really? 'Plugger' would never say, but this year he gave hints:

'Wall, I was born in Dublin, went to New York as a boy, an' my father's a U.S. pensioner for fightin' in the Civil War. It's twenty years since I started ridin' in Nebraska. Before I was foot runnin'. I went to the Cape foot runnin' in ...'

The shrewd ones estimated from all this that he was actually forty-two.

The crowd on the final day was thirty thousand and the takings £1,307, a Melbourne Cricket Ground record for any sport. 'Plugger' rode a remarkable race. He won by fifteen yards and he was only the third rider in the history of the race to win from scratch. The Argus reported there was a scene of great excitement as Martin crossed the line.

Tremendous cheers were raised, and there was no question about the popularity of the win. Martin toppled from his machine utterly exhausted and had to be supported by his trainers as he was led up to be introduced to the Lieutenant Governor, Sir John Madden, and the first Prime Minister of the new Commonwealth, Mr Edmund Barton.

The Argus said unquestionably the best man won, but there was much trouble over this race. The payout the ring estimated at between £7,000 and £8,000 and the amount of money wagered a record for any cycling race anywhere. The rumour was that the famous gambler, John Wren, of the Collingwood Tote had plunged on Martin.

The stewards questioned the riding of F. S. Beauchamp on the grounds that he had acted as a pacemaker for Martin. The League suspended him immediately with the recommendation that he be disqualified for life.

The following Thursday seven riders had to appear before an inquiry at the Port Phillip Club Hotel. They came in before the officials one at a time and the tribunal asked each man, did he receive any money in connection with the race; did he try to win and was he concerned in a conspiracy? Every man swore his innocence and the inquiry was inconclusive. Meanwhile 'Plugger's' purse had been withheld and always he was in the background at the Port Phillip. Whenever any of the officials appeared he asked: 'When am I going to get my £400?' Eventually 'Plugger' did get his money, according to The Argus, just as he was about to serve a writ.

Later a journal called the Lone Hand came out bluntly and said the whole race was fixed by John Wren. It gave the version of the settlement as told by 'Plugger' Bill's trainer, 'General' Gordon. The bribes were laid out in piles of notes and sovereigns upon Martin's bed at the hotel. One by one the cyclists were admitted to the bedroom, and Martin, who had a loaded revolver beside him, addressed each successive visitor by name and stated the terms of the contract. The visitor replied 'Quite right, Bill' or words to that effect. Then Bill paid out the money, and solemnly requested the recipient to keep it safe. Everybody was enriched with a heavy burden of cash, 'save poor "General" Gordon, who ultimately sued his employer Martin for £60.

According to the Lone Hand 'Plugger' Bill had nineteen opponents and all had raised a dead heat except Mick Lewis and one or two others.

'Plugger' never revealed how much money he actually made that day but obviously the amount was considerable, for he did say that now he had enough to retire. Yet the lure was too strong. He went to New Zealand, won some races there, and with his Austral friend, Frank Beauchamp, toured America once more. He returned to Australia again in 1902 and actually retired in 1903.

But the reactions to the 1901 Austral were widespread and although the classic is still run today, no one would ever suggest again that here was a rival to the Melbourne Cup. The Argus was filled with angry letters. This was one:

Sir—I was in the grandstand on Saturday, unfortunately accompanied, as it turned out, by a lady. I have never enjoyed myself less. The deafening clamour of the barrackers, the degrading antics of the 'ticktackers', the absence of true sport, thoroughly disgusted me. Sport! More sport could be found in watching a mosquito and far more excitement.

The Argus commented:

We have seen sports rise to extraordinary popularity and fade and die within a few seasons, and the cause has always been the same. Already there has been so much talk about crooked running in connection with these cycling contests that, unless there is drastic interference to minimise the evil, the sport must certainly perish.

The greatest years for visitors were 1902 to 1904, when Americans came pouring in looking for the Australian riches—Major Taylor, Floyd McFarland, N. C. Hopper, Ivor Lawson Hardy, Downing and Orlando Stevens.

The most famous of the group was Marshall Walter 'Major' Taylor. He was not big, only five feet seven inches, but beautifully built, and a remarkable athlete. In 1899 at Philadelphia the Major rode a mile behind a pacing car in one minute nineteen seconds. He came from Worcester, Massachusetts and he was a minister of his local Methodist church. He was such a devoted churchman that when he was offered $30,000 to compete in a series of races on the Sabbath he refused.

Why he was called 'Major' is something of a mystery. When asked in Sydney, he said: 'It was Major to thirty thousand people when I defeated their favourite Jacquelin in Paris and it is Major to the lift boy in my Sydney hotel.'

In 1903 the spectacular Hugh D. McIntosh, pieman, super promoter, inaugurated the Sydney Thousand at the Sydney Cricket Ground. The first prize was £750, £350 better than the Austral, and now the world's richest race. The N.S.W. Cyclist spoke of the affair with wonder, how the Sydney Cricket Ground looked 'splendid', illuminated with hundreds of glittering electric lights. Floyd McFarland and 'Major' Taylor were there, but the 'Major' had a fall and N. C. Hopper won off seventy-five yards handicap.

The following year a protest by O'Brien, who finished second, ended with the disqualification of several riders, including L. Corbett, the winner. Corbett took legal action to get his prize money, but O'Brien, the defendant, won the case. This was virtually the end of the Sydney Thousand.

Major Taylor had two tours of Australia, 1902-03 and 1903-04, and he won a great many races, but his parting words were far from kind:

'Haven't you people got eyes? I'll tell you that if racing as I have seen it allowed to go on, you will see the end. Teaming or combining, call it what you like, is rampant here. I wonder how many of your riders are paid to pull and not to win. What do they get? Gambling has an honest rider against such a man no odds. Take the Sydney Thousand—that was the worst race I have ever competed in and, owing to the teaming I never had a chance. When I mentioned this point to a racing official he remarked, "Oh we never tolerate the same man winning all the time."'

H. Cugnet in his cycling history wrote that nobody heeded Major's warning. The public became fed up with cycle-racing and rival sports took over. Racing tracks closed in every State and by 1906 it was virtually all over.

There were valiant attempts to revive the interest at the Austral. For the Austral of 1908, Messrs John Rinaldo and Christopher Sebphe went aloft and reached a height of five thousand feet. Rinaldo came down by parachute and landed in a garden in the Fitzroy Gardens. Mr Sebphe came down with the balloon and landed on the roof of a house in Queen's Parade, Fitzroy.

For the Austral of 1909, Miss Mollie Mostyn, the American lady aeronaut and Captain Penfold, the Australian balloonist, would go aloft by balloon, and descend to the ground by parachute. Indeed Captain Penfold would perform his sensational act of parachuting down riding a bicycle. Regrettably the balloon did not attain sufficient height and ended on top of a fence in Vere Street, Collingwood. The sensational act, thus, was of a different variety. Captain Penfold used his bicycle to ride back to the Melbourne Cricket Ground.

What else was there to do? In 1910 the Austral committee hired Mr G. Cugnet to take off from the Melbourne Cricket Ground in a Blériot aeroplane; an extraordinarily hazardous idea. On the day the weather was so bad, Mr Cugnet was fearful of making the ascent until the wind dropped. That didn't happen until about 7 p.m. when almost all the Austral crowds had gone home. Mr Cugnet did get off the ground. He had to bank violently to miss the scoreboard, whereupon he dived into the tennis court and his Blériot was hideously wrecked. That was the end of the aerobatics on the Melbourne Cricket Ground.

Never again was cycling capable of being a greater drawcard than football or cricket. It had lost its chance, but the sport did continue to produce a remarkable number of world champions. There was Bob Spears, Alfred Goullet, Alf Grenda, Jackie Clark and Reg McNamara, the iron man. He won a six-day event in New York in 1926 despite three broken ribs. By the time he gave it away in 1936 he had fractured his skull twice, broken his jaw three times, broken his collarbone six times. This helped him to win nigh on half a million dollars.

In terms of sporting passion perhaps the names we remember best are Hubert Opperman, Sid Patterson and Russell Mockridge.

In 1929 the French sporting journal L'Auto conducted a poll to discover the most popular sportsman in Europe. More than one and a half million votes were cast and the Australian cycling idol, Hubert Opperman, convincingly beat France's own tennis champion, Henri Cochet.

'Oppy won because the public admires and appreciates his courage, his perfect loyalty and his eternal smile,' L'Auto said.

There is the possibility that the eternal smile was there because he did not know what the French were saying to him. Alf Stumbles, an old Malvern Star colleague, says there was one particular reason why Oppy became so popular with the French: he learned to relieve himself while actually riding his bike. In a marathon like the Paris-Brest-Paris race it was vital not to lose a minute. So Oppy didn't—he openly urinated while free-wheeling. 'As far as I know,' says Alf Stumbles, 'he was the first cyclist to learn to do the trick and it made front page news. The sophisticated French loved him for it.'

Opperman was born in May 1904, the son of an amateur cycling champion. He was working as a clerk in the Navy Department. He asked for leave to compete in the Launceston-Hobart 126-mile road race. Leave was refused, Opperman resigned and so began his professional career.

He set records. He rode 466 miles unpaced in twenty-four hours. He did a thousand miles, motor paced in a little over twenty-eight hours. He rode the length of Scotland and England from Land's End to John O'Groats and took four hours off the record. He won the French classic, the Bol d'Or, and the 726-mile Paris-Brest-Paris marathon. He did that entire distance non-stop in forty-nine hours.

On 19 September 1934 there was a special dinner in his honour at the Oriental Hotel and Prime Minister, Mr Joe Lyons, proposed the toast. Lyons said:

'I am proud to do him honour. I suppose I am just as much a fan as anyone at listening in to sport on the other side of the world. I used to get my youngsters to get up in turn to give me the latest news of Opperman and they were just as keen supporters of him as I was. All the kiddies of Australia knew him as a hero.'

Opperman's most spectacular ride came in November 1937. He set out to break Billy Read's record ride from Fremantle to Sydney. Billy Read covered the 2,751 miles in eighteen days, eighteen hours. Oppy rode a Malvern Star and, of course, Malvern Star sponsored him. They put out a handsome sheet which listed the exact time and hour that Billy Read had arrived in every town across the continent. Then there was a blank column in which one could write down the second that Oppy arrived there. One also received a map of the route and the instructions were: 'Mark Oppy's progress on this record-breaking ride by filling in between the black lines with a coloured pencil.' The sheet told us 'Hubert Opperman (Oppy) is the greatest and most renowned rider the world has known'.

He started by dipping his bike in the waters of the Indian Ocean, then from Fremantle he rode 268 miles in the first day. The road through Western Australia at this time was shocking. He was frightened of riding at night for fear of losing the track. Over one section of a hundred miles the sand surface was so heavy he was able to average only four and three-quarter miles an hour. When he could not plough through the sand he got off his bicycle and walked. At this stage he averaged only three and a half hours of sleep a night. He described himself as being like a fly walking 'across fly paper.'

Alf Stumbles said Oppy was a man of remarkable integrity. He was determined to ride every inch, so much so that if he finished his ride for the night at the rear of the caravan he would not start the next day from the front. He would insist on going back to the rear.

Bruce Small, the dynamic leader of Malvern Star, played some tricks on him. Alf Stumbles said that on one occasion Oppy had been asleep only twenty minutes, when Bruce Small awakened him. Oppy did not know. He thought he had been asleep for eight hours and mechanically off he pedalled once again.

Undoubtedly the hardest part of the ride was coping with the civic receptions. At Ballarat two thousand people were waiting for him at the City Hall, plus a civic reception. Crowds were waiting for him all the way into Melbourne. At Flemington the crowds were so thick he had to get off his bike. There were ten thousand waiting for him at the Elizabeth Street Post Office and another twenty thousand lined the streets to Parliament House, where the Premier, Mr Dunstan, was waiting to congratulate him.

Several times Opperman fell asleep while riding and crashed off his bike. Even the driver of the caravan that was following him fell asleep at the wheel near Goulburn. It ran across the road and down a bank and was saved from turning over only by a fence. He went from Seymour straight through to Marulan, N.S.W., without a break, twenty-nine and a half hours' continuous riding.

He arrived in Sydney at 10.51 p.m. and over the last twenty miles the traffic was packed tight. He was escorted by hundreds of cars and hundreds of cyclists. The traffic came to a dead standstill along George Street and up to the G.P.O. Here he received his final civic reception. He had crossed the continent in thirteen days, ten hours eleven minutes and broken the record by better than five days.

Famous sportsmen usually get to own hotels. Hubert Opperman's fame helped him to win the Federal seat of Corio. He became both Minister for Immigration, and Minister of Shipping and Transport. He was knighted to become Sir Hubert Opperman and his final appointment was Australian High Commissioner to Malta.

Sid Patterson was born in 1927 and he did not retire until he was forty-one. He had unbelievable stamina and in his day he was the best handicap rider in the world. He rode in more than seventy six-day bike races and won twelve of them, which amounts to fourteen months of pedalling on its own. He did better than 'Plugger' Bill, he won the Austral Wheel Race from scratch in 1962, 1963 and 1964. He was the first man to win it from scratch in forty years of racing.

He was world amateur sprint champion in 1949, and world amateur pursuit champion in 1950. Then when he turned professional he was world pursuit champion in 1952 and 1953.

The feat of winning a world sprint title one year and a pursuit the next was extraordinary. One journalist described it as the equivalent of an athlete winning a hundred metres gold medal one day and the five thousand metres medal the next.

Patterson received huge publicity when he won his world titles. After he took the amateur pursuit title in Liège, Belgium, Hubert Opperman cabled him: 'Warmest congratulations. It's not what you did but the courageous way you did it.' When he returned from winning his sprint title in Copenhagen, Melbourne gave him a civic reception, and the just-retired deputy premier, Mr (later Sir Wilfrid) Kent Hughes, said his win had been worth countless thousands of pounds worth of publicity to Australia. The Government, he said, should put up the money and sponsor many trips by Australian sporting champions.

Russell Mockridge belonged to the same era as Sid Patterson. He was born in 1928 and he went to school at Geelong College, which makes him a rarity indeed, for the public schools were never exactly breeding grounds for cycling champions. They concentrated on the more polite sports like cricket and rowing.

Mockridge had great athletic skills, but he was badly handicapped. His eyesight was so bad he could hardly see across the road, so cycling was one of the few ways in which he could compete. Early in his career he was not keen on road racing, because he thought his bad vision might lead to accidents in which he or his fellows would be injured. That was a prophetic thought.

His climb to fame was very swift. He won two gold medals at the Empire Games in Auckland and he was one of the first selected for the 1952 Olympic Games at Helsinki. At one time he wanted to become a minister and he had a commendable streak of honesty. The Olympic Federation demanded that the athletes sign a bond to remain amateur until two years after the Games. It would be almost impossible to hold any athlete to this bond, but Mockridge refused to sign it. He had no intention of turning professional, but as he said, he did not know what he would be doing in two years' time. So he was dropped from the team. It was one of those matters over which Australians become deeply moved. It was even debated in Parliament in Canberra.

Meanwhile, Mockridge paid his own way to Europe and won the Paris Amateur Grand Prix, then followed this by winning the Open Grand Prix in which he beat the world's top professionals. No amateur had ever done this before and the professionals found it so humiliating that they altered the rules the following year. Amateurs were barred.

Now, of course, Mockridge had to be in the team and the clamour for his inclusion was nation-wide. It was particularly strong from his home town, Geelong. Lithgow worshipped its Marjorie Jackson, the Lithgow Flyer. It was the same in Geelong with Russell Mockridge. With help from the Geelong Mayor, Hubert Opperman, M.H.R., the drive behind him was immense. Geelong put up a bond of £800 and the Olympic officials finally agreed to make the amateur term twelve months instead of two years. So Mockridge was one of the last arrivals in Helsinki.

1952 was Mockridge's golden year. He won the gold medal for the 1,000 metres time trial and the gold for the 2,000 metres tandem. His partner was Lionel Cox, who until four days before had never been on a tandem.

On 12 September 1958, Mockridge left on the 140-miles Tour of Gippsland race. It was to be one of his last rides in Australia before he set out for Europe and hopefully a crack at another Tour de France. He was in brilliant form. A few weeks before he won the Australian Professional Road Cycling Championship for the third year in a row.

At 10 a.m. a bus turned out of Clayton Road, across Dandenong Road. Mockridge hit the bus at high speed, slid underneath and was killed. The story that night took up the front page of The Herald. The whole nation was deeply shocked.

More than ten thousand people came out for the Mockridge funeral. Police estimate that three thousand crowded outside the funeral parlour in St Kilda Road. Thousands more lined the side of the road. A group of five hundred waited at the Clayton Road Crossing in Dandenong Road where the accident had taken place. And six hundred more attended the service at the Crematorium. The funeral cortege was two miles long and it had an escort of seven motor cycle police.

There were also leading articles. The Sun News-Pictorial said:

The late Russell Mockridge was educated at Geelong College whose motto is 'sic itur ad astra'. This means 'Here lies the road to immortality' and old boy Mockridge rode a long way along this road on his bike. He was the best racing cyclist a top cycling country has ever produced and his name is famous in every country of the world where bikes are ridden in competition. His death on Saturday is probably the greatest blow the sport of cycling has ever received.

Even 'Major' Taylor would have been impressed with those lines. He would have decided cycling in Australia was far from dead after all.
