Old Man River

19 min read.

First published 1999

A posting to Los Angeles brings a cast of eccentric Californian cycling companions, before Keith and Marie join a Bikecentennial tour tracing the Mississippi from its source at Lake Itaska to New Orleans, through Hannibal's Mark Twain shrines and Memphis's Elvis souvenir strip.

There was sweet irony in the story of the tandem - within 12 months it was back in California. The Herald & Weekly Times Ltd sent the Dunstans to Los Angeles for three years.

One would have thought that in pollution land, where every garage housed two automobiles, life would have been hell for anyone riding on two wheels. This was not so. There are good biking towns and bad biking towns. Melbourne, Adelaide, Perth and Canberra are all superior cycling towns and so is Los Angeles. Often we were asked: "Were you really brave enough to ride a bike there?" Ah, yes indeed. The streets were wide, people didn't look unkindly upon cyclists, and oddly enough, most of the real traffic was siphoned off to the 1000 km of freeways. L.A. depended for water on its huge aqueducts. There was a bike path which ran along the edge of the concrete aqueduct from the Colorado River 40 km to town. Then there was a bike path which ran along the waterfront from Santa Monica almost to Newport, nigh on 30 km. You might kill a pedestrian or a roller blader occasionally, but it was great biking.

We lived at Calabasas, on the way to Santa Barbara, 30 kilometres from downtown L.A. The local pedallers were friendly. We had been there barely a week before we investigated the neighboring suburb of Westlake, one of the prettiest suburbs we had seen. It was like Canberra only better designed and every street had separated bikeways.

We were on the Jack Taylor tandem pushing quietly into a headwind when there was a bellow behind our ears. "Howya doin? Small, Marvin B. Small's the name. Where ya headin'?" He had a huge handlebar moustache. He was about 58 years old, dressed all in yellow, and Mr Small stood 200 cm tall.

"Ah, sure would appreciate it," he said, "if you folks would let me show you around." That's precisely what he did. He pedalled us to Happy Valley, towards the Santa Monica Mountains and showed us where they filmed the series Gunsmoke. He showed us where Matt Dillon and his mob roamed the ranges. He showed us where they were still filming M.A.S.H., Alan Alda and all.

Marvin was from Alabama and his accent was beyond belief. Actually, we needed an interpreter. He couldn't understand us and we couldn't understand him. His favorite expressions were: "Waaal ah'll be dawged," and "Lawd ha' mercy". Every cyclist he saw he bellowed from 100 metres off "Howya doin'?" - "Where ya headin'". He flew Thunderbolts in the Pacific during World War 2, then he worked for Beechcraft. He flew all over the U.S. in a Beechcraft King Air with his bike in the back. He claimed he had cycled in every State of the union.

We loved Marvin. We went 'bikin'' with him every weekend. There were others, there was Frank Schmidt. Frank owned a Ferrari, a Maserati and a Mercedes. But he had a weight problem, so all those cars stayed in the garage while he sweated on his Schwinn Paramount. There was Bill Wilkinson, who weighed 15 kg more than he ought to weigh. He had a beautiful Cadillac with the label 'Mafia Staff Car' on the back. He would sweat up the Santa Monica mountains swearing: "You idiot Marvin. Only a nut would want to pedal up here."

We joined the Ventura Bicycle Club which had marvellous breakfast rides on Sundays. We would leave Ventura at 8 a.m. then ride 29 km to Montecito, where there was a fantastic restaurant which offered 163 varieties of omelette. Then there was an annual ride from Ventura to Santa Barbara, an 86 km round trip. We went on the ride thinking maybe we would be exclusive on our tandem. There were 1200 bikes, including 110 tandems. The San Fernando Wheelmen had an annual ride which offered a double century or a triple century, all in the one day. This was miles, not kilometres. They would get 200 or more doing the double century and maybe half a dozen on the triple. Marvin always went off on the double century. "You'all'll come along," he would say. We all thought differently.

The Bikecentennial organisation was still in action at Missoula, charting new rides. It announced a new ride, which to us could hardly be more romantic, the Mississippi ride, pedalling from the river's source near the Canadian border all the way down to New Orleans. Ever since the days when I owned a wind-up gramophone I had been dreaming of seeing the Mississippi. Of course, it was the marvellous voice of Paul Robeson, rich as if it came from the bottom of a keg. We had this old record - I Think You'll Do on one side and Old Man River on the other. I played Old Man River until it wore out. The dreaming and the romance was pushed along by Mark Twain, Tom Sawyer and Huckleberry Finn - a whole Walter Mitty dream of river boats, rafts and brown water.

River rafting was not for us. Bikecentennial had worked out an ingenious route which kept close to the river all the way. So in September 1980 we set out with our old friends, Jeff and Pauline Hook and Don and Norma Hartley. Our youngest daughter Sarah flew to the United States to take part in the ride, along with another friend, Tony Orr from Melbourne.

The source of the Mississippi was at Lake Itaska. Henry Rowe Schoolcraft found it in 1832. He had gone there to arbitrate in a war between the Sioux and Chippewa Indians. Pioneers tended to be erudite in those days. He took the Latin words 'Veritas Caput' - true head - and abbreviated them to Itaska.

The lake was surrounded by pines, aspen, birch and oak trees. It was so perfect in its beauty it reminded one of a classic scene on a box of chocolates. Everything was there, except the bow and ribbon across the corner. There was a sign directing us to the head waters of the Mississippi. Here we found water bubbling out of the lake into an exquisite clear stream which tumbled through birch trees and poison ivy.

There was a spot where you could take a running jump which entitled the adventurous to buy a T-shirt which stated: I LEAPT ACROSS THE MISSISSIPPI. Then hand-carved on a tree stump was the message:

Here 1475 ft.
Above
The Ocean
The Mighty
Mississippi
Begins
To Flow
On Its
Winding Way
2582 Miles
To The
Gulf
Of Mexico.

Back in 1832 Henry Rowe Schoolcraft claimed that the area was besieged by "voracious, long-billed and dyspeptic musketoes." He was absolutely accurate. As campers we discovered this at once. Car licence plates in Minnesota boasted it was the land of 10,000 lakes. This was not precisely accurate, there were 15,291 lakes.

It would be reasonable to suggest that every lake spawned a million mozzies which would make a total in Minnesota of 15,291,000,000. Indeed we found Minnesotans took a curious pride in their mozzies. The souvenir shops sold postcards which showed a large multi-colored mosquito with a sting as long as a harpoon, and it was labelled: "The State Bird of Minnesota."

They were good for business. Every shop, every kiosk, even the check-out points at supermarkets, where they sell the chewing gum and razor blades, they all had roll-on, rub-on and spray-on repellent. If nerve tonics were the big confidence trick of the 19th century, undoubtedly insect repellents were the star con trick of the 20th. They were no use. Some "voracious, long-billed and dyspeptic musketoes" had the nerve to sit on the aerosol can while we were spraying.

The happy hour for the mosquito, when he rose like Dracula from his coffin, began at 5 p.m. At some camp grounds a gentleman came round with a smoke machine which looked like a pipe on the end of a lawn mower motor. It asphyxiated everyone while they were having their dinner. It didn't hurt the mosquitoes. It just moved them from one side of the camp to the other. Mysteriously, the mozzies all disappeared around 9 p.m. Either the air was too cold or it was past their dinner hour, but they all returned for another happy hour at 7 a.m.

In 300 kilometres we crossed the Mississippi 14 times. Its color was a superb chocolate and as we progressed further it began to pick up muscle. Minnesota was really just a series of lakes connected by strips of land. We were confused by signs which said: NO HUNTING. NO FISHING. NO RICING.

No ricing?

A ranger told us that wild rice was the caviar of the Lake Country and this was the rice season. It was an occupation as absorbing as fishing and perhaps even as rewarding as digging for truffles. Wild rice was the classic food of the Chippewa Indians. It was permitted on the public lakes, but only according to exact rules. The rules were all designed to preserve the ancient Indian food. Any kind of harvesting machine was banned.

The ranger, Gary Hoeft, said: "First, you are allowed to rice only during the ricing season. We limit the time as we would for duck hunters. Second, you are allowed to do it only between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m. Third, you must use a canoe and paddles are forbidden. You must punt your way along with a long pole so as not to disturb the rushes. Fourth, you must beat the rice into your canoe with a stick and that stick must not be longer than 60 centimetres."

"And what if you break the rules?" we asked.

Ranger Hoeft drew himself up to his full height, which was slightly shorter than a telegraph pole, and said: "Well, I figure we could get real difficult. Maybe fine you $200 and confiscate your canoe."

He told us that wild rice was really not rice at all, but a grass, and the best place to get it was from the Indians. Rice or grass, we thought it delicious. Lake caviar was the perfect name for it. What's more, it cost like caviar. We bought several kilos, and carefully stored it in our panniers, determined to take some home to Australia. What a tale we would tell over dinner parties, how this had been hand thrashed into canoes by the Chippewa Indians. It was never to be. All our wild rice was confiscated at Melbourne Airport by Customs.

The town that really fascinated us was Hannibal, Missouri, 100 kilometres north of St Louis. Here Mark Twain spent his childhood. It was right here that Mark Twain gained the inspiration for the stories about Tom Sawyer and Huckleberry Finn. Inspiration indeed. Here the river was mightier than any we ever saw. As brown as Paul Robeson's arm. You could barely see from one side of the river to the other. Had it been possible to walk across, it would have taken 40 minutes.

I would never have allowed a child of mine to ride a log raft on it, not in a fit. Not only was it large, but it was moody. There were vicious currents that raised waves; trees, boxes, hunks of debris moved past at high speed. A ferryman at St Genevieve a little further to the south told us he had never heard of anyone swimming across the river, but a girl - "I reckon she was about 19" - came down in a canoe the year before, and she went all the way from Minnesota to New Orleans. But this ferryman thought such activities too dangerous for normal folk.

Hannibal was one of those American towns that looked as if it had been designed by Norman Rockwell. Fifty years had passed but Norman Rockwell was still revered. He did covers for the Saturday Evening Post, folksy as a square dance party - pot belly stoves, vintage drug store scenes, main street type houses with high gable roofs and window boxes.

It has always been a class quiz question. What was Mark Twain's real name? The answer, of course, is Samuel Langhorne Clemens. He died in 1910. If he had the opportunity to return to Hannibal he would be considerably surprised. We noted that almost everything in Hannibal was either named after him or characters from his books. There was the Huck Finn Shopping Centre, the Tom and Huck Motel, the Mark Twain Bridge, the Mark Twain Gift Shop, the Mark Twain Bank, the Mark Twain Pet Shop and the Mark Twain Mental Health Centre. Mr Clemens would have appreciated that one.

Then there was Becky Thatcher's Book Shop and Becky Thatcher's Café. Twain fans remember Becky. She was a little blue-eyed creature with yellow hair plaited into long pig tails. Tom fell without firing a shot, the moment he first saw her.

We discovered that Hannibal's big day was July 4, not necessarily the National Day of Independence but Tom Sawyer's Day. On that day there was a raft race. The 13-year-olds had to build their own rafts and race five miles downstream to Hannibal. There was also a frog-jumping contest in honour of the tale The Notorious Jumping Frog of Calaveras County. But the most important of all was the National Fence White Washing Contest. This story concerned the most famous of Tom Sawyer episodes. Aunty Polly told Tom she wanted her fence white-washed. Tom saw a beautiful day ruined, but he was crafty. As his mates wandered by observing him at his grim task, he told them what a thrill it was to have a chance to white-wash. Only one in a thousand was given the responsibility of a job like that. He had been waiting to do it for years. Pretty soon they were paying him hard cash for the privilege of doing him out of it.

We were just a trifle skeptical but we were told the original fence was still there, waiting to be white-washed once again.

We found the Clemens house, lovingly preserved. Sam's old room was around the back, looking convincingly untidy. It had its own entrance which was splendid for sneaking in and out unnoticed, often he would creep back in as late as could be. There was also a Mark Twain Museum which contained a huge mechanical organ he bought in 1904. There was his typewriter. Legend has it he was the first author to write his works on a typewriter. There was his top hat, a white jacket - he was always immaculate in a white suit - and a cotton umbrella. In England he was asked why he carried a cheap cotton umbrella. He replied: "That's the only kind an Englishman wouldn't steal."

At the museum we learned that when Mark Twain was 18 he left Hannibal for ever to become a pilot on a Mississippi steamboat. Which brings one to that other famous quiz question: Why did Clemens choose the name Mark Twain? 'Mark Twain' was the cry of the boatmen as they checked the depth of the river. It meant two fathoms.

We found St Louis and the spot where the mighty Missouri meets the mighty Mississippi. For miles the two rivers just didn't get along. We could see the steel-grey Missouri on one side and the chocolate brown Mississippi on the other. St Louis was the home of the Anheuser-Busch brewery, the biggest in the world. For one who believed that Melburnians were the true beer cognoscenti, this was interesting. It covered 70 city blocks, had 63 buildings and we were told it turned out 22 million barrels of beer every day and nine million cans a day.

The other feature of St Louis was the stainless steel Gateway Arch, 193 metres high, designed by the Finnish-American architect Eero Saarinen, the symbolic gateway to the west. We thought it one of the most beautiful sculptures we had seen.

There was every available weather. For days we rode in heavy rain, almost drowned by semi trailers and tankers as they passed. In the damp we found the rich smell of the pine forests, we could see myriads of wild flowers and occasionally there was the pungent smell of skunk. It was an interesting smell, not nearly as awful as its reputation.

It was autumn and the trees were starting to change colour. The maples, particularly, were startling. Their leaves were as red as traffic lights. Hillsides were ablaze with color. It was important to keep one's eye on the television news. Every night along with the weather, the TV presenters told the best places to find colour. They gave a colour chart of Wisconsin, the red patches indicated where viewers could find leaves in their most brilliant hues.

Every night we set up our tents and camped by the Mississippi which seemed to get bigger and more powerful every kilometre we rode. Back in 1882 Mark Twain bemoaned the dreary, empty docks along the river. Where had all the paddle steamers gone? Where were their colorful skippers, their mates, their crews? "It's woeful. It's melancholy," he said. He knew where they had gone. The steamboats had been killed by the railroads. Samuel Clemens may have thought the river was dying, but it would have been interesting for him to have camped by the river a century later.

We were in a little place called Davenport, Iowa. We were able to camp six metres from the water. Old Man River was moving along silently, relentlessly and the immensity of the water gave one a feeling of serenity, and yes, eternity. The serenity was not to last. We awakened with a start just after 11 p.m. It began far around the bend, heavy drumming noises like the coming of a diesel freight train. Next came the funnel blasts of the approaching ocean liner. These were like the mating calls of a frustrated Hereford bull.

The real sensation came when the monster was right alongside. There were the diesel noises, the whistle noises, the splash of small tidal waves along the shore. Then way up aloft they had searchlights to probe the banks. These were so powerful the beams went right through our tent, in one side and out the other.

As we lay in our sleeping bags we did not realise the magnitude of these vessels. Euphemistically they were called tow boats. Actually they were sophisticated power units worth more than $2 million each, and they could push 40 barges at a time. These barges looked like Brondignagian caterpillars and they could carry as many as 60 25-tonne semi trailers. The load filled with grain or coal would fill 300 railway freight cars. So you can imagine what it was like with 40 of these laced together. Here was a vessel moving down the Mississippi that was longer than the Queen Elizabeth or a nuclear aircraft carrier.

There were 3000 tow boats of various sizes on the Mississippi, all with intriguing names. There was 'Relaxin'', 'Mary Lou', 'Little Bull', 'Gobbler', 'Big Mama', 'Davy Crockett', 'Lola P' and astonishingly enough, we spotted a tow-boat called 'Koala Bear.' As a creature it did not look all that cuddly. We could only discover that it was owned in Alabama.

Jim Morgan, a lock master, at Number 15 Lock, at Rock Island, told us Sam Clemens would have no worries about his river. It was carrying more freight than at any time in its history. There was no way you could send freight cheaper.

Of course, there was the other item, so beloved by Sam, the river boat, the paddle steamer. We found the very thing by the wharf at Memphis, Tennessee. Utterly classic, she had the lofty twin smoke stacks forward and she had the Texas bridge on the upper deck. That is the name for a dinkum paddle steamer bridge, it is as wide as the State of Texas. Then bringing up the rear was a splendid churning sternwheel, the most spectacular of all driving units, the Mixmaster to end all Mixmasters. Around the window on the upper decks there were twiddledly bits of iron, plaited into roses and leaves.

We did not suspect a thing until the engines started. No fuming black smoke poured out of those twin stacks. There was no durk-a-durk-a-durk from the pistons of the steam engine. Oh no, there was the smooth sound of twin diesels. Very likely she was a converted landing barge rescued from Guadalcanal. We looked upstairs and those impressive smoke stacks did not go anywhere, they finished on the mid deck. Furthermore, the stern wheel paddle down aft was just a piece of decoration rolling free.

The other vital Memphis item was Elvis Presley. Indeed it was impossible to overlook him. We went to Beale Street, the famous street where W.C. Handy, the trumpeter, wrote St Louis Blues, Memphis Blues and many others. We didn't see evidence of Mr Handy, but on the corner of Beale Street and Main Street there was a beautiful statue of Elvis in bronze. He was wearing an Indian style shirt open almost to the waist, his shoulders were wide, his hips were slim, just the way everybody wanted to remember him.

We left downtown Memphis on route 61, turned right on a highway called Elvis Presley Boulevard, went two blocks and there was Graceland, the vast Presley mansion. It had grandiose gates which depicted a man playing a guitar. Immediately opposite there was an entire shopping centre devoted to Elvis. It had a sign three or four storeys high, the type you see in Las Vegas, and it shrieked: ELVIS SOUVENIRS - FREE PARKING.

There was the Elvis florist shop, so that you could get Elvis tributes, there was the Elvis custom shirt shop, so that you could get precisely the imprint you desire. There was Karat Korner which specialised in all types of Elvis jewelry. Then there was the Elvis record shop, Elvis books and it was possible to buy such things as the Elvis Cook Book, put together by his uncle, Vester Presley. Actually there was a greater variety of souvenirs than we had seen anywhere, with even such items as a guitar frame depicting the face of Elvis in the centre.

Being near the Mississippi, admiring its strengths and looking at the variety of river vessels was very nearly the greatest experience of a lifetime, but from Memphis on, it was a disappointment. The glorious scenery had gone and worst of all, no longer could we see the river. Here was something we had not realised, levees 10 to 13 metres high lined the banks from St Louis, 2500 kilometres down to the sea. Old Man River might look sluggish and docile enough, but under his quiet exterior there was menace. Some people could still remember the great floods of 1927 when 600,000 people were left homeless and in some areas the river swelled to a width of 130 kilometres. Putting in those levees was a job bigger than building the Great Wall of China.

Our ultimate reward was New Orleans, the end of the trail. The beautiful thing about New Orleans was this - it turned out exactly as expected. The French Quarter looked slightly more French than Paris. It had precisely the right mixture of peeling paint, creaking balconies, verticals not quite vertical and the appropriate number of sidewalk artists.

The cafés and bars had names like Dirty Dan's, Sloppy Joe's and Crazy Shirley's. After dark there was music coming from everywhere, Basin Street Blues...St Louis Blues...When the Saints Come Marchin' In. Over the roads there was Molly's, an Irish bar. It had a coffin, wrapped in the British flag, hanging from the ceiling.

After living with the river for six weeks, after seeing it at its birth, a pure, clear trickle, we were sad about saying good-bye. Now at New Orleans it swept past in an enormous arc, spanned by the world's largest cantilever bridge and we were told the port was the second largest in the United States, second only to New York.

We learned also that there were 14 rivers in the world longer than the Mississippi. The Congo and the Amazon had larger drainage basins, but none matched it for productivity. Its basin had a vital effect on 31 States and it passed through the greatest food creating area in the world.

Finally it emptied into the Gulf of Mexico and if you were aloft in an aeroplane you could still see that famous Mississippi hundreds of kilometres out to sea.
