The Born-Again Biker

24 min read.

First published 1999

Traces Dunstan's own return to cycling during Melbourne's 1970s transport strikes, his time as founding president of the Victorian Bicycle Institute alongside Brian Dixon and Alan Parker, the campaigns for bike paths and the West Gate Bridge ban, and two mishaps that briefly cured him of the habit.

There were 70 bicycles in the bike shed at Francis Brown House, but as soon as we left school none of us looked at our bicycles again. As far as we were concerned, adult legs were not supposed to push pedals. Adult legs were for pushing down accelerators on automobiles. In the 1940s the ultimate dream for a young male was an MG, or if you couldn't afford that, an Austin A40.

In my case I could afford neither. If there were any courting to be done, one either went by public transport or worked very hard on one's parents to borrow the family Buick. Cars in the 1940s were sensible. The Buick did not have the bucket variety, but a broad front seat, capable of holding three people. So it was easy to put an arm around a girlfriend when driving and even better when parking. It always seemed a little forward, immediately, to suggest that you move to the back seat.

The old Malvern Star, complete with Sturmey Archer gears, honker and speedometer, sat on the back veranda beside an old ice chest. It must have been there six or seven years. One day my mother put an advertisement in the newspaper and sold it for five pounds. She did not think the matter worth mentioning and I did not notice its absence.

The year 1972 was a great for strikes. There was a banking strike, a prolonged strike of the oil workers, an electricity strike and at least four strikes involving public transport. Sir Henry Bolte was the Victorian Premier, a man who did not give in easily to strikers. His most famous quote was during the teachers' strike. They had marched on Parliament House with their demands for better wages. The Premier's sympathetic response was this: "They can march up and down until they're bloody well foot sore, for all I care."

Sensible householders worked out a survival kit. Number one was a jerry can of petrol in the garage to cope with the inevitable petrol rationing. Number two was a stock of sausages or chops, so that the barbecue could come into action when the power cuts made the stove useless. Number three was a supply of candles, and maybe one or two good quality kerosene lamps. My mother was brilliantly prepared, she couldn't wait for a strike so that she could illuminate her house with candle power. She had, too, some lovely 19th century kerosene lamps and her living room looked lovely in the soft light.

What would cover a cessation of public transport? Why not a bicycle? It must have been during the second transport strike of 1972. Malvern Star now went under the title of General Accessories and the manager was Bruce Small's old colleague, Alf Stumbles. I called Mr Stumbles and asked if I could borrow a bicycle for the duration of the strike. The name Malvern Star would be mentioned in my daily column 'A Place in the Sun'.

The bicycle was delivered to the office of the Sun News Pictorial in Flinders Street, a Malvern Star Super SL, 10-speed gears. Alf Stumbles explained the marvels of modern technology. Gears had come on since the 1930s. There were two chain wheels at the front, and a sprocket at the back which had five gears. The gear lever on the left side moved the chain from the large sprocket to the smaller and the gear lever on the right moved the chain across the five-gear cluster at the rear. These gave a choice of 10 different gears. "You must always be pedalling when you change gears," said Alf, "otherwise it won't work and the chain could come off."

The next day I set out from Central Park Road, East Malvern, distance to the city just over 12 kilometres. It was the second day of the strike and Sir Henry Bolte had urged motorists to share cars with their colleagues so that everyone could get to work. Even so, cars were solid all the way down Toorak Road, solid all along Alexandra Avenue, crawling at barely five kilometres an hour. The bicycle was the superior machine, moving between the traffic lines at a smooth 15 kmh. It would be hard to underestimate the feeling of superiority and power as one left behind the panting Nissans, Toyotas, Holdens and Fords.

Thirty-five minutes later I was sitting at my desk in the office. Door-to-door this was faster than I had ever done it by train, tram or car. Going home was a revelation, the 10-speed gears really worked, utterly different from the old Sturmey Archer. There was the long climb up from the Yarra, via Williams Road, then Wallace Avenue to Toorak Road. Easy! I thought I was going like Oppy himself. Suddenly, there was a cry from behind: "Aw, move over Grandpa!" A little kid, gearless, moved past on his bike as if I were standing still.

One had to get used to these little humiliations. By the end of the week a new love affair had developed with the bicycle and I didn't take public transport again. The Super Star SL never went back to General Accessories and Alf Stumbles sold it at the going price, $110.

Prior to 1970, the method for getting to work was to walk a mile to Caulfield Railway station then catch a train to work. More often I was driven to the station by a very kindly wife, Marie. The trip to town by train was the most tedious part of the day. The return trip at 6 p.m. had awkward possibilities. Always I was a brilliant sleeper. The world is divided between somnolents and insomniacs. My special skill was to go to sleep in the train, sail past Caulfield, doze through Carnegie, Murrumbeena, Hughesdale and Oakleigh and not wake up until Clayton. This was immensely frustrating for Marie who had been waiting at Caulfield to pick me up.

Getting to work by train had been a chore for both of us, now it had been converted into the most enchanting part of the day. Part of the fun was studying the street directory, working out new and ingenious ways to get to the city. Sometimes I would observe the rich, ride down Irving or St Georges Road, Toorak, follow the Yarra around and take a trip through Como Park. Then there was the ride along the footpath beside the water. The bicycle path was yet to be. In the early morning the river was perfectly still, a stately grey heron often stood by the Henley landing stage and the city over yonder really did look like the pretty pictures put out in the Tourist Bureau calendars. Yet maybe the best fun was exploring the back streets of Prahran and Windsor, past the 19th century weatherboard cottages owned by the Italians and Greeks. Late in the evening the air was redolent with the most marvellous cooking smells.

It has to be admitted it was not entirely easy. Bicycle skills are not acquired immediately over night. It must have been in the first fortnight. My front tyre caught in the tram track in St Kilda Road and I went straight over the handlebars, landing in a heap on the bitumen, suffering a great loss of dignity and a tear in the trousers of a good grey flannel suit. This is a lesson so traumatic a cyclist has to learn it only once.

The other is car doors. Back in the 1950s, as mentioned, there were those splendid bench seats in the front. The thing to do was to alight from your car by moving along the bench seat and out the left hand side. Come the bucket seat, this was impossible except for contortionists. Everybody left the car on the right hand side straight into the stream of traffic. In the 1970s, nine out of 10 people left their cars without looking behind. It was terrifying then and it is terrifying now. Timid cyclists hug the edge of the road close to the parked cars.

Maybe it was in the third or the fourth week of my riding to work. Again it was in Toorak Road. Suddenly the door of a Jaguar opened, I crashed straight into the arms of a lady. We were both considerably surprised. Fortunately I had managed to brake and neither of us was hurt. Again this is a lesson you have to learn only once. The moral is this: bicyclists have a right to their section of the road, and they have to demand it. This is the safest way and the only hope of being seen.

Of course, in the 1970s such behavior was anathema to motorists. The motor car and the people who drove them, believed the car was God and entitled to possess all. A particular object of loathing at the time was the Melbourne Tramway System. Trams moved down the centre of the road at 20 kmh and stopped dead every 200 metres to pick up passengers. So they impeded motor cars, which in 80 per cent of cases had only one occupant. All around Australia there was a campaign, actively encouraged by the newspapers, to get rid of the trams. Only in Melbourne were they saved and chiefly by the very voluble and colorful chairman of the Tramways Board, and Director of the Metropolitan Transportation Committee, Major-General Sir Robert Risson. The theory was if the trams went, buses could take their place. Buses pulled in to the kerb, thus making the way clear for fast movement by private cars. General Risson proved that no other form of transport moved crowds more quickly than the amiable but slow moving trams. So the trams were saved and later Brisbane, and particularly Sydney, came to wish they had a General Risson to save their trams.

Bicycles were another pain in the neck to the motorist. Two or three times a day motorists would wind down their windows and shout out kindly messages like: Why dontcha stay at home? Or more often: Get orf the fuckin' road, ya idiot.

Many of them liked to press the point by driving as close as possible without actually causing homicide. One day in Station Street, Malvern, there was an event which almost made me give it away. A car load of youths rode by shouting abuse. Five minutes later they came back and as they drew close one slammed open the door and tried to wipe me off. I swerved into the gutter, shaken but unhurt.

There were not many cyclists on the road, but one noted immediately there was a rule, always they waved to each other. It was clear that cyclists, like Aborigines, Good Earthers, wild river lovers, Hare Krishnas, Jews, Baptists, Mormons, female lawyers, save-our-treers, and Moslems, suffered terribly from prejudice.

For several years there had been deep worry at the office. Giving up automobiles was very unhealthy. The prosperity of the earth depended on the car. What was good for GMH was good for Australia. So dispensing with the car was like not wearing a tie, growing a beard or saying that you liked Bob Hawke.

At the office this riding a bicycle to work was considered strange, if not ratbag behavior, certainly eccentric. One executive called me aside and asked, tactfully, whether I was in financial trouble. Did I need a loan to buy a car? No, I didn't need a loan and the best place to keep the bicycle was in the office garage, over the road in Exhibition Street. Sir Phillip Jones, chairman of the Herald & Weekly Times Ltd, had a Silver Cloud Rolls-Royce. It was nice to park my bike just beside it.

There were many things they did not understand. When one is forced into a minority group, one becomes progressively more strange. Bicyclists, if they are going to survive, must look vulgar. Bright orange is a good colour, shrieking red tape is splendid, helmets should reflect, be seen in the dark. And if one plans to ride any distance, it is important to wear tight, revealing pants with chamois leather in the crotch. As for wet weather gear, it is vital to look like a Japanese sunset so that bleary-eyed motorists will see you through the windscreen wipers.

The gentlemen at the Herald & Weekly Times Ltd., as they arrived in their Commodores, Volvos, Jaguars and Executive Toyotas, would see the amazing sight of Dunstan doing a complete change out of a Joshua suit of many colours into something more plain. It took at least another 12 years for the bicycle to become completely respectable. In 1985, Rockman's Regency, owned by the former Lord Mayor, Irvin Rockman, was very smart indeed. A lunch group, all interested in bikes, met there. It included Peter Russell-Clarke, the cartoonist chef; Ralph Johnson, managing director of Four'n'Twenty Pies; Geoff Stephenson, secretary of BHP; and Duncan Powers, a partner in Potter Warburg. A favorite gag was to report that my bike had been stolen. Half-way through the dessert, Ralph Johnson said: "Your bike has been stolen."

"Rubbish."

"It's true, look for yourself. It's not there."

I had chained it to a light stanchion in a prominent position right outside the front door and we could survey the scene from the restaurant window. My God, it was true. The bike had gone. They didn't even leave the lock and chain.

I walked up and down and looked in astonishment at the vacant space. The next move was to query the doorman, who was dressed like a Russian General. "You were here. Did you see anyone take my bicycle?"

"I think you had better consult the manager, Sir," he said.

The Manager came out and said crisply: "We had to remove your bicycle. We didn't think it improved the image of our hotel."

"Where is it?"

"We put it down in the basement."

"But...but, I had it chained with a big lock."

"Yes, I am sorry, but we used a pair of bolt cutters to remove it."

At this stage I lost my cool. I said, far from detracting from the image of his beautiful hotel, the bicycle actually enhanced it. In these days of foul air pollution and traffic congestion, it was a symbol of clean air and good environment.

"We had a V.I.P. arriving," he interrupted.

"That bike," I said, "was in a public place. You broke the lock and took it away, committing an act of larceny. I could get a legal opinion on this, but I won't do that. It will be such a good story in tomorrow's newspapers."

"Look, don't do that," said the Manager. "Ah would you like to stay and have a drink with us. It will be all on the house."

"No, I don't ever want to drink here again. Would you please fetch my bicycle and I will leave."

The next day there was a letter of apology hand-delivered to my house and the gift of a book about the hotel business. Irvin Rockman, an old friend, was much more apologetic and had no idea all this had happened. He sent a beautiful new bicycle lock which I am still using today.

The true ratbag is convinced of the correctness of his or her behavior. He or she is the only sane one. The rest of the world are the ratbags. That was the way it seemed to me. The bicycle was so easy, absolutely ideal for getting around the city, go to the bank, go to the State Library, go to Parliament House, just chain it up outside.

Why didn't everyone ride to work? I rode rain or shine. In the winter going home in the dark, I mounted a battery powered headlamp on the handle bars, so big it could have belonged to a semi trailer, plus two large red tail-lights. However, there was one thing I would not do, and that was ride home at night in the rain. No matter how well you are lit, motorists cannot see through whishing windscreen wipers.

The rebirth of the bicycle really began in 1971. There were many reasons; the bicycle was lighter, less expensive. They came equipped with 10-speed gears, and just like it was in the 1890s, for Americans it was trendy. There were reports that Mrs Henry Ford II, rode a bicycle. So too did Mrs Jackie Onassis and the Mayor of New York, John Lindsay, raved about his bike. (1)

There was bicycle hire in Central Park, New York. Sunday was the big day for then it became a bike haven and automobiles were banned. Ah, but there was a fascinating new idea. Every weekend it was possible to see the Detroit-made monsters heading out of town with bicycles strapped over the boot, in much the same fashion as the surfies strapped the boards on to their Holdens in Sydney.

There were 3.7 million bicycles sold in the U.S. in 1960, and mainly they were for children. In 1971 the figure jumped to 8.5 million and now the adults were buying. (2)

In Australia there was not a Mayor Lindsay or a Jackie Onassis to promote biking, but in September 1973, Ken Myer, chairman of the Myer Emporium, went into his own store and bought himself a five-speed Malvern Star. He used it to ride to tennis, then he went with his children on a pedalling excursion around the Melbourne Domain and the Sidney Myer Music Bowl. He suggested also that he might ride it to work from his house in Toorak. Not on a week day, the neurotic fury of automobile drivers around 8 a.m. would be too terrifying, but he might attempt it on a Saturday morning when the traffic was lighter. (3)

However, Brian Dixon really did ride his bike to work. Dixon was the Minister for Youth, Recreation & Sport in the Victorian Government, and very athletic. He had played 258 games for the Melbourne Football Club and 11 for Victoria. There were reports that he had a radio fitted to the handlebars of his bike and he listened to the news on his way to work. (4) The last politician to ride his bike to work was Alfred Deakin who used to pedal to Parliament House from South Yarra at the turn of the century.

Dixon seriously believed that Melbourne needed bicycles and he asked me to become the foundation president of the Victorian Bicycle Institute, which later was to change its name to Bike Victoria. The Institute had no money but went round to all the bicycle dealers. The best contributor was General Accessories, producers of Malvern Star. Our toughest task was to convince the controllers of Melbourne's traffic that there was a role for the bicycle. That was far from easy.

Perhaps our best campaigner was Alan Parker, a traffic expert. Alan was born in England, had a mid country accent and along with that, all the doggedness and determination of a Yorkshire opening batsman. His style was confronting to the point that many a senior public servant had pangs of terror when he was outside demanding admission to their offices. Alan was brilliant at assembling his facts and he was a constant, vitriolic letter writer to the newspapers.

Alan pointed out again and again that Melbourne was flat, the climate was good, the roads were wide, it was one of the best places on earth for bicycles. Why spend millions on freeways, when bicycles were the efficient way to go? He found that 18 bicycles could be parked in the space occupied by one car and 30 bikes could travel in the space occupied by one moving automobile. (5) He had the theory that no Melburnian was more than five kilometres from a railway station. If only we could encourage public transport to carry bicycles or provide good lock-up facilities at railway stations we could improve physical fitness and at the same time solve all the traffic problems.

We went to see the Chief Commissioner of Railways. He was very polite, but as we put our case, his eyes glazed over. Clearly he wanted us elsewhere. The Chairman of the Country Roads Board pointed out that bikes and cars were not a good mix. The chairman of the Board of Works, Alan Croxford was the bluntest of them all. He told us flatly: "There is no place for the bicycle on Victoria's roads. If I had my way I would have them all banned."

We had commuter days and we had anti-smog days when all cyclists were asked to ride their bikes to town. Every year we had a day when we staged a race, car versus bike. It has to be admitted we cheated just a little. The people we used to compete against the cars were not feeble old ladies. On one occasion we chose female road champions and they left the cars far behind.

The first of these contests was in 1976. It was 'Clean Air Day' and all Melbourne workers were asked to cut pollution. The race was from three points in the suburbs and the finish was at the City Square. The cyclists had the advantage - no parking problems. Under the rules, the car drivers had to park their cars first then walk to the Square. The first cyclist to arrive was a Lalor High School student, Martin Golledge, 16. He arrived at 8.47 a.m., just 17 minutes after he left the Coburg Town Hall. The car was three minutes behind and the driver took another 15 minutes to park his car. The next cyclist in was Helen Smith, 30, a typist at the Police Academy. She rode from Chadstone Shopping Centre and beat the car by 10 minutes.

The third was Diana Cunningham, 15, of Lalor High School. She took 42 minutes to reach the the Square from Lalor shopping centre. The car beat her by nine minutes.

We chose our bikers very craftily the following year. Andrew McDowall and Caroline Chapman, magnificent riders, rode a tandem from Toorak to the Square in 14 minutes. They beat the car by four minutes. The other riders came in from Chadstone, Lalor and Footscray. They all won, and two of the bikes were ridden by girls. The margins ranged from two to 14 minutes. (6)

There were protests. In 1978 Melbourne completed the West Gate Bridge, the most expensive in the country, higher above the water and three times longer than the Sydney Harbor Bridge. The fury amongst cyclists knew no bounds. Cyclists were banned. Before the bridge went up there was a ferry. Bikers coming to the city each day from the western suburbs of Williamstown and Altona could use the lower Yarra ferry. Once the bridge was complete the ferry ceased.

Other bridges didn't do this to cyclists. The Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco had a special lane to cater for bike riders. The authorities came up with some remarkable excuses. "We would have made provision for cyclists, but the wind up there is too strong. Bike riders would be in danger." Even stranger, we received the comment: "The approaches to the West Gate Bridge are too steep. Bike riders would never get up there."

This caused even greater fury. We staged a protest ride to the Bridge with the deliberate aim of riding to the top. Brian Dixon, still a State Minister, was one of 2000 who took part. Police barred us at the entrance to the bridge. Alan Parker was at his most eloquent. It was just another example of the attitude to bike riders. "We are treated as dogs," he said. And he pointed out that there were signs on all the freeways: "No cyclists, no dogs." (7)

Cyclists did eventually win the right to ride on the freeways, but not bridges. Even the new Bolte Bridge built for the City Link is verboten to the cyclist, so bike riders either have to make detours of anything up to 20 kilometres or purchase a pair of water wings.

Yet things did improve markedly for cyclists. The Yarra River bicycle path from Princes Bridge to Punt Road cost $19,000, opened in November 1975 and by the end of the following year it had gone right through to the MacRobertson Bridge at Toorak-Burnley. (8) The next move was to put a bicycle path from Port Melbourne around the bay side. The only serious objections came from a pocket of residents in Brighton. There would be visits from motor cyclists, there would be "flirtatious behavior" and there would be desecration of parkland. There were 205 duplicated letters of protest to the Brighton Council. Brian Dixon accused the residents of being "selfish and shortsighted". (9)

Alan Parker was at his eloquent best. He said: "Two hundred and five Brighton residents in the well-off to stinking rich bracket have decided to put their own selfish needs before that of 100,000 children who use bikes in the bayside suburbs." (10) But the Brighton people won their battle. The bicycle path eventually went all the way from Altona to Carrum, but there was still a gap between Bay and Normanby Streets that remained exclusive to the residents. The bikes had to make a small detour.

As the years passed, one noticed that motorists did become more sympathetic and courteous. By the late '70s. I was called a 'fuckin' idiot' only once a month instead of every day. It was interesting. You have noticed, of course, that dog owners invariably resemble their dogs. So indeed is the personality of the driver wedded to the make of car. One learned to be very wary of expensive makes, particularly blue-rinsed females at the wheels of Mercedes. Look out, too, for Porsches and most sports cars. Be careful of cars with names on them, fancy designs down the sides and always expect no quarter from anyone driving a P-plate. And beware, utterly beware, of all taxis. Oddly enough, often the nicest people to the bicycle were the drivers of big trucks.

They didn't really mean it, but many people would say, "Look, it's a great idea this riding a bike to work. I would do it myself if it were not for all traffic. Too dangerous."

Actually, the thicker the traffic, the safer you are. In thick traffic the bike is moving as fast, and often faster than the fossil monster stream. (Forgive that expression, bike riders tend to call cars fossil fuel feeders, or the like.) However, outside on a highway where the speed limit is 80 kmh, or more, that is the time to be scared. It is always wiser to seek out alternate routes. Oh dear, I was never a competent automobile driver and always I felt safer when riding a bike.

None of my friends, none of the wise old hands who have been riding the city traffic for 30 years, have ever been seriously hurt. On the other hand, friends and acquaintances aplenty have been killed or maimed at the wheel of a car. In that time I have just had two mishaps and both helped create a smarter person. The first occurred with those cycling mates, Stephenson, Powers, Russell-Clarke, Johnson and Phil Mouritz, licensee of the Walhalla Lodge Hotel. We met monthly for lunch and at the end of the year we gave a prize for the restaurant of the year. The prize was a silver pie donated by Ralph, managing director of Four'n'Twenty Pies. In 1977 the winner was Café Clichy in Collingwood. I took along a bottle of Veuve Cliquot and as we presented the silver pie to the proprietor, Sigmund Jorgensen, we toasted him in good champagne. The ebullient Sigmund was so delighted he opened another bottle of Veuve Cliquot. Indeed, his delight continued for such a long time, he opened another.

It must have been close on 5 p.m. when I went outside to get on my bike but the wretched thing would not stay still. I went right over and fell on the other side. I tried once more and fell on the other side. The third time I was lucky and managed to pedal off home to South Yarra. The best thing was to have an early night. Next morning I found I couldn't get out of bed. Something terrible had happened to my knees. I didn't ride again for nearly a month. A serious lesson was learned, never touch the widow Cliquot when riding a bicycle.

The other mishap took place in California in 1981. The computer passion was just starting. The world is divided between those who resist gadgets and those can't resist. Alas, I am a gadget person. I read in a bicycle magazine about a marvellous bike computer with a liquid crystal read out. This computer not only told you riding speed, but it gave your maximum speed for the day, the time elapsed since you started riding, your average speed calculated at all times, your trip mileage and total mileage since you bought the thing. It also announced your cadence, the rate at which you were turning the pedals. I sent for it by post and when it arrived I was terribly excited. These days they give you all that information on a gadget the size of a postage stamp. This thing was the size of a cigar box.

I fitted it to my lovely machine and everything worked beautifully. I hardly looked at the scenery, so fascinating was all the information provided by the computer. We were living in Calabasas, a lovely suburb out of Los Angeles on the way to Santa Barbara. There was one marvellous, seriously steep hill two kilometres from our place. I took it to the top of the hill and started racing down, and I watched those liquid crystal figures rise beautifully...40...45...50...55. 58...59... Suddenly I looked up, barely 20 feet in front of me was a great furniture van parked right across the road. It was too late to brake, I threw the bike sideways and went down on the road. The result was two broken ribs and a lacerated face. You see, I wasn't wearing a helmet.

That night I had to fly to Las Vegas to interview a famous Canadian song bird who was about to tour Australia. We met for drinks at five o'clock. I thought I had made a remarkable recovery and in the meantime I hadn't realised what was taking place. The whole side of my face was swollen and turned blue. "I am not going to have you interview me," said the lady. "You should be in hospital."

As for the computer, several weeks later I went touring. There's an old song which says "it never rains in California". It sure did this day. Suddenly the whole face of the computer went blank, no speed, no cadence, no mileage, nothing. I complained to the manufacturers and they were tremendously apologetic. "We will send you another." So a new one arrived and two weeks later it rained again. Once more the screen went blank, no speed, no cadence, nothing.

I took the computer off the bicycle and threw it in the rubbish bin. It was a good feeling. I think I was cured, temporarily.
