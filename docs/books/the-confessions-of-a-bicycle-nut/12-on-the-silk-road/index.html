On the Silk Road

23 min read.

First published 1999

A 22-day bicycle tour through Guangdong Province and Beijing in 1983, cycling laps of Tian'anmen Square on a hired Chinese roadster, filing past Mao Tse Tung's embalmed body in the Chairman Mao Memorial Hall, and buying silk in Guangzhou for a daughter's wedding dress.

The ultimate dream, of course, was to ride a bicycle in China. China was the one place where the bicycle prevailed, where the bicycle was king. The official figure was 700 million bicycles on the roads, and undoubtedly this was a conservative estimate.

In 1983 a Sydney travel agent, Christine Gee, started organising bicycle tours to China. Her inspiration was a Hong Kong-born Chinese, Charles Ng. Charles was an ebullient, high-spirited, bike-riding character, who never stopped talking. He pronounced his name 'Nnnnnn'. Charles dabbled with these adventures for the first time in 1981 by organising bike tours for Americans.

When he suggested the idea, the Chinese were not interested. They couldn't believe he was serious. He was just organising an evil Western spying expedition. Why would affluent Americans leave their Cadillacs, Buicks and Fords, to come to China to ride push bikes around paddy fields?

He worked on the Chinese for a year before they would accept it. He told us later: "You see, the Chinese ride bicycles because they have to. The use them for everything, for carrying the rice, the firewood, even pigs. The truth is, they hate them. If they could get into cars like Westerners they would dump those bikes so fast, Beijing would become like Los Angeles."

We assured Mr Ng that it wasn't only the Chinese who thought we were crazy.

Christine Gee organised a tour for October 1983. We were to meet Charles Ng in Hong Kong, enter China via Portugese Macau and be on the road for 22 days. Most of the riding would be in the great rice bowl around Guangzhou and through Guangdong Province. As a climax we would make a side trip to Beijing by air. We would ride new Taiwanese 10-speed bicycles supplied through Mr Ng.

Oh dear, those Taiwanese bikes! One remembered the dictum of my friend Marvin Small: "Riding someone else's bike, is like using someone else's teeth." The Taiwanese bikes looked good but it took a miracle of maintenance to keep them on the road. Originally we planned to take our tandem, but alas, Marie had had an eye operation for cataracts and it was felt that the bumpy roads of China would hardly be the right post-operative treatment.

I should have stayed home to look after her, but I was too weak to resist the invitation, so off I went. There were 15 on the trip. It included such people as Alex Dean, an engineer, Geoff Haynes, an FM radio station owner in Sydney, Jennifer Blake, a parole officer in Tamworth, and Vikki Davis, a town planner with the Preston Council in Melbourne.

We pedalled to the Chinese border escorted by members of the Macau Bicycle Club, who waved us good-bye with smiles and cheers of good luck. We went through a wonderful towering gate and archway bearing the date 1842. The Chinese immigration officials in their army uniforms, baggy green jackets with red tabs at the collar, were courteous and helpful. We had Charles Ng as our guide and interpreter, but then we noticed there was another character, very plain, very untalkative, who did not leave us for the entire trip. He was representing the Chinese Government. We never actually worked out why he was there, but we suspected he was making sure we were not wicked spies for the West.

It would be hard to underestimate the effect that a group of foreign cyclists has on the Chinese. We were our usual tasteful selves in orange, red and yellow tight-fitting cycling guernseys, some of them sporting all those colours at the one time. Extremely tight bicycle shorts, both male and female. Helmets, cycling shoes, much bare leg, to say nothing of white faces, some of them plastered with zinc cream. White devils indeed. The locals had never seen anything remotely like it. Everywhere we went they rushed to have a look.

Then the bicycles, those Taiwanese 10-speeds! Of the 700 million bikes in China, most of them were the same type, big solid frames, one-speed, weighing maybe 20 kilograms, a classic version of a bicycle being built in Britain, circa 1925. So whenever we stopped for a breather, suddenly a crowd appeared, usually 100 people or more. They would fight to get a front stalls position, get down on their hands and knees and study the gears. Then one of them would start making signs. We would have to give a demonstration of how those gears worked. We could have been that little man ET, demonstrating his space ship.

Although we could not communicate with words, the Chinese were amazingly warm and friendly. They reminded us of the Italians in Rome. Although China is more than 60 per cent mountains, we were lucky. Our terrain was not difficult. Mostly we were on raised roads, like embankments which ran through the endless paddy fields. However, we discovered very quickly, what an illusion it was to think there was no road traffic. There were countless Japanese-made trucks, which honked consistently expecting the thousands of bicyclists to get out of their way. The roads, too, were narrow. We learned to be agile and to move quickly.

Our second stop for the night was in the town of Shiqi. We noticed that every time we turned on our television there was a little sign 'T3' at the bottom left-hand corner of the screen. Charles told us the bad news. T3 was the message that the third typhoon of the year was on its way in from the South China Sea. They didn't give cyclones fancy names in China; T3 was good enough.

The Chinese Government was determined that we should be correctly educated. So our schedule for the next day was to pedal out to see one of the great dams built by Chairman Mao just before the cultural revolution.

At first the rain began very gently, that light misty stuff which we always considered so good for the roses. Then clouds that we associated only with horror movies appeared over the paddy fields and winds began to lay things horizontal. The rain descended fire-hose style. For the first five minutes we congratulated ourselves on the effective quality of our wet weather gear. But soon the damp had worked its way through the trousers, now drops were trickling down the neck, and the saddle had become squelchy. What were trickles quickly became Niagaras. The front wheel sent a jet straight into the boots, so that they appeared to hold more water than even Chairman Mao's dam.

On the way back to our hotel I skidded in the mud and almost landed on a pile of rock by the side of the road. Next morning I noted a disaster. I always rode with a little handlebar bag and in its back pocket I kept my Swiss Army knife. It was gone, no longer there. Perhaps, when I went over on my side in the mud and the rain, my Swiss Army knife jumped out on to the road. This was the same knife that was temporarily confiscated on our way to California, the very knife that had been so admired by the Qantas captain. It was the top of the range for the Swiss Army, with 22 different items. It had a magnifying glass, two cutting blades, four different screw drivers, a file, a pipe scourer, a magnifying glass, a toothpick, eyebrow tweezers, a mini ballpoint pen, a tin opener and even a proper working saw. I loved that knife dearly; it was a real working tool kit. I have wondered many times since, would it have been picked up by a young Chinese boy, a boy who had never owned anything special in his life? I imagined his excitement as he sprinted home with it to his family. Then mother, father and all the family, pulling out the various items, wondering how they worked.

Finally we were so drowned it didn't matter any more. Our wet brakes no longer worked and the descent of hills along muddy roads was exciting indeed. Our Taiwanese bikes did not have mudguards so they sent a stream of mud up and down the back, courtesy of Typhoon 3. The local Chinese passed us in their trucks, as close as possible, so that they could get a better look at these bizarre Westerners. Their big tyres swamped us in a tidal wave. But they were as friendly as ever. They didn't wish us a happy day, but they did open their windows and shout something which sounded like 'Hello'. We tried to gurgle back a similar greeting, but it was not easy for creatures virtually under water.

We stopped at a public shelter by the dam. No longer were the Taiwanese bikes objects of admiration. An elderly Chinese gentleman came up to us. He showed me his Chinese heavyweight bike and he pointed to its equally solid mudguards. He then gestured to my Taiwanese lightweight devoid of mudguards, pointed his index finger and made a circle around his right ear. He was explaining something I already knew, I was a nut.

He was right, of course. Even the meanest of Chinese bikes had mudguards. Some even had ingenious guards made from soft drink cans soldered together. But the Chinese, one noted, never gave up, even in typhoons. They wore superbly practical rain capes, some grey, some blue, some a brilliant pink. Furthermore, they were the only people I had seen anywhere who were capable of riding bicycles while holding an umbrella in one hand.

Chairman Mao's dam was impressive indeed, but what really astonished was something different. Nobody had told us about it. Right in front of the dam was a great amusement park. It looked like something out of Disneyland. It had all the latest rides, a roller coaster that looped the loop, and wild river rafting rides, where the customers dashed down a stream in a cut-away logs. All this was not working because of T3, but incredibly the loudspeaker system was playing Simon and Garfunkel. The song was Sounds of Silence.

The best thing about the ride was this: because we were on bikes, we were intimate with the sights and smells of China and there were plenty of those. We averaged 50 to 80 km a day and we rode into towns rarely, if ever, visited by tourists. The roads were narrow and rough, yet construction was going on everywhere. For hundreds and hundreds of kilometres there was road building. In Australia, you can tour the countryside, admire the 'MEN AT WORK' signs and never catch men actually in the act of working. China was an ant heap of hard work.

One would see an old man in a coolie hat, pushing a primitive wheelbarrow filled high with road metal and just getting there, pushing straining, shuddering up hill. There were girl road workers, hundreds of them, some couldn't be more than 16 or 17, and they wore gloves to protect their hands. They had long poles over their shoulders and they were carrying bitumen in either basket. Yet, after seeing these girls, suddenly we could come across a spanking new Kawasaki front-end loader, as big as a house. We speculated on the price of the equipment, maybe $180,000. What would happen to all those 17-year-old girls when these machines took their place?

The hotels had fascinating little comforts. There would be a Thermos of hot water, a teapot and a can of jasmine tea in the room. There would be slippers under the bed and one establishment provided full size toothbrushes and paste.

We had private bathrooms but the plumbing was always on the verge of a nervous breakdown. One hotel in Zhaoqing was in deep trouble. Hot water was supposed to come on at 5 p.m. but the pumping system expired and there was no water at all. Meanwhile, water was seeping through the roof and in the elegant pink marble foyer there were plastic buckets to catch the drips.

Guangzhou was the capital of the province. In pre-Mao days it was called Canton. Twenty kilometres out of the city we stayed at a remarkable place called Soft Lake. Yes, Soft Lake was beautifully named, it seemed to shimmer in a soft light. Charles Ng told us it used to be the headquarters for Marshal Lin Biao, the former commander-in-chief of the Manchurian strike force. During the agonised period of the Cultural revolution Lin Biao was the Army chief. He could see Mao Tse Tung was slowly disintegrating and inevitably power would fall to the man who was prepared to grasp it. But the plot to kill Mao was exposed and Lin Biao was killed in an 'accident' when he tried to flee in 1971.

We were in his old rooms. The radio man, Geoff Haynes, had the suite actually used by Lin Biao himself. It reminded one of the accommodation the English Raj might have built for themselves in India. The ceilings were actually six metres high. The bathroom had the same dimensions as a squash court. The bath was a mini swimming pool with a long settee at one end, presumably for massage. My bedroom was very large indeed and the bed so grand it conjured an image of Richard Burton and Elizabeth Taylor when they were playing in Cleopatra. That night I awakened at 3 a.m. and I was prepared to swear little feet had just run over my face. Next morning I found a large hole had been eaten out of the side of my pannier bag. There had been a packet of biscuits inside.

We had a rest day in Zhaoqing. It was here we discovered the difficulty of finding anyone who spoke English. I went into the post office to send home some letters. It was important to get some letters home by air mail. The girl at the counter just shook her ahead whenever I said anything. I started waving my arms like a seagull. She laughed and waved her arms back. I was at least half convinced that the letter would go by air mail or seagull.

A full day off, what an opportunity to explore. We were not supposed to go off on our own, but I managed to elude the Chinese Government watchdog and pedalled off into the hills. There was a marvellous feeling of exhilharation, being out there alone taking in a scene so different from anything one could have at home. It was humid and hot. I had consumed all my water and I was becoming desperately thirsty. We had been warned about the water; never, never, never were we to drink any water that had not been boiled. Every morning our bidons were filled with water that been boiled and sometimes it came to us at scalding temperatures.

There were no cafés, no shops, but it would be nice to have a cup of tea. I came across a little place that looked almost like a café. I knocked on the door and an elderly man answered. I tried to indicate that I needed a cup of tea. He took me into a room, sat me down, and five minutes later he arrived with tea. He took a chair also and we had tea out of little cups. There was nothing we could to say to each other, but there was communication just the same. We kept smiling.

What to do now? I had better pay for it. I pulled out some money and that was a big mistake. The old Chinese gentleman was hugely offended and waved away the money. Filled with shame, I got on my bike and pedalled away. I rode for a hour and a half and thought I should be getting close to Zhaoqing, but nothing looked familiar. There was no point in looking at road signs, they were all in Chinese characters. I thought I had the pronunciation of the town correct - 'Zowching' - but nobody understood me. I went into post offices, shops, and even banks to find just someone, somewhere, who spoke English, but without success.

The thought occurred that I could be lost for ever, working or starving as a foreign peasant. So I tried something else. As a trainee pilot in the Air Force, I was told that when lost there was one thing to do. Take a significant landmark and begin a square search, making the square progressively larger until you found some point that you recognised. I did this and, glory be, finally I found something I recognised but it was late in the evening when I returned to the hotel in Zhaoqing.

Yes, the Chinese in provincial China were friendly but another point struck home. Australians looked like a race of comfortable Buddhas, but the Chinese all had flat stomachs, they were as lean as parking meters. How could this be when the food they ate was so superb, so wonderful? We were on the road for three weeks - and it had to be conceded - we visited some strange places, but the food consistently was very nearly the best I had ever eaten. There had always been doubts in my mind as to who were the best cooks, the French, the Italians or the Chinese, but after this ride, the doubts were dispelled. The Chinese were the winners by the length of 16 woks.

The meals usually started with a soup, but soup of such delicacy and fragrance one tended to consume far too much and have three helpings. That was a big mistake, like trying to win the Melbourne Cup in the first three furlongs. We would ask our interpreter what it contained. "Bean curd and fungus," would come the reply.

We had exquisite rice flour rolls, so light they could have been floating in air, we had chicken, fried frog and olive seed, sweet and sour fish, sweet and sour pork, egg dishes like the finest omelettes ever to come out of France. Ah, but the fried rice in the province, generally acknowledged as the rice capital of the world, was something else. It was fried with a delicate subtlety and blend of egg, pork and prawns.

One night we had a meat which clearly had been barbecued. The sauce was perfect and the skin crunchy. I went for another piece and my chop sticks came back with a head, a beak and two mean little eyes. I said to the interpreter: "Hey, what's this?"

"Pigeon," he replied, "very popular in China."

This explained why one saw so few birds in China. They had all been eaten.

Yes, meals were always spectacular and breakfast was no exception. Inevitably it started with soup. Next came marvellous little dumplings. Some had a meat paste inside, some had a red bean combination and some lotus seed. They also produced individual parcels of rice wrapped in lotus leaves.

However, we went to some hotels where clearly they thought: "Ah, these Western barbarians will want a Western breakfast." One morning we received two fried eggs, a piece of Swiss roll and a large pork dumpling all on the one plate.

At the Foshan Hotel, the breakfast diet was particularly interesting. We started with a slice of Swiss roll, followed by two fried eggs and slices of classic supermarket white bread, with apricot jam and no butter. Next we had rice cakes, dumplings, rice noodle rolls as fine as Kleenex, egg and shrimp balls, French toast and items which looked like chocolate eclairs without the chocolate. Triumphantly we finished with soup. Altogether there were 11 courses.

After all this food, the Chinese undoubtedly thought we needed more exercise. They had us out at dawn taking lessons from two masters of Tai Chi, Mr Chan Len Zen and Mr Chan Zan. They stood out in front and with beautiful flowing movements, they expected us to follow. This was easier said than done.

When it was all over, Alex Dean, an engineer with Melbourne's Board of Works pulled out a boomerang and gave an exhibition in the hotel courtyard. A mob of Chinese gathered, amazed. Some of us thought an international incident was pending. That boomerang was sure to go through a hotel window. Not so. Alex was an expert, who made his own boomerangs. Every time, as if by magic, they came back to his hand. At the end of the exercise, he presented boomerangs to the Tai Chi masters. It was an excellent gesture in Australian public relations.

There were times when the countryside was extraordinarily beautiful and times when it was spectacular. We crossed the great Yellow River by ferry. Restless, huge and flood-prone, it reminded us of the mighty Mississippi. In every town there was a People's Cultural Palace. Outside there would be parking for bicycles, not just 20 or 30 bikes, but thousands of them, stacked one against the other and all black. We wondered how they identified their bikes and why they were not stolen.

That's another thing. There is little crime in China and there was never a fear of anything being stolen in our hotel rooms. Was there a good reason for this? Not long before we arrived, 3000 people had been executed for crimes against the State, ranging from armed robbery, excessive gambling, murder and embezzlement. We were not in tourist areas, so local officials had no qualms about placing their posters in post offices and even outside on street poles. They depicted the miscreants being executed. Terrible pictures they were, mostly men, down on their knees being shot through the back of the head.

There were also cartoon posters which pointed out that each family should have only one child. Go beyond that and the family would lose all State benefits. We were told this worked well enough in the cities but in rural areas peasants did not understand. They needed the extra children for work in the fields.

We went into a People's Cultural Palace at Conghua. Inside there was a big roller skating rink, a restaurant with live entertainment and a pool room. There was a theatre where a magician was putting on a splendid performance. Rarely had I seen a lady so beautifully cut in half. There were also Chinese movies, good photography, good directions and a story line leaning heavily on romance.

We flew by Chinese airlines to Beijing and left our Taiwanese bicycles behind. We regretted this bitterly. Surely, above all, we should have an opportunity to ride bikes in the capital, in the greatest bike city on earth? Indeed, we saw bicycles by the million, bikes coming like clouds of locusts. We had already seen them in huge numbers in cities like Guangzhou. The interesting exercise there was trying to cross the road. Some pedestrians become skilled at threading their way through passing cars, but just try doing the same through a hundred thousand bikes. It is impossible.

The official figure for bicycles in Beijing was four million. There were a number of brands, the Five Rams, the Golden Lion, the Flying Pigeon, the Flying Butterfly and a very solid machine simply called Forever. Some brand new models had just started to appear. They stood out incredibly, because these were the first Chinese bikes in a colour other than black and a few rare deluxe machines had three-speed Sturmey Archer gears.

There were no shops in Beijing labelled 'Bike Hire', and at first we received the message that hiring a bike was out of the question. Why would any foreigner want to do such a strange thing? The only answer was to call at every bike shop and ask. At last we had some luck. Mr Sung, our interpreter, found a bike shop which looked so dark and ancient it could have been the place where Bruce Small first discovered his Malvern Star in Glenferrie Road.

Negotiation was tough. The shop owner insisted that we give him our passports, otherwise no bikes. We felt nervous indeed over that one, but we couldn't complain over the price, a dollar a day. A very considerate Chinese lady handed over the machine. Solid? There had to be another word. Monumental would have fitted this bike. Its weight was around 20 kilograms and very likely it saw action in the 1911 uprising or the Boxer Rebellion. The seat was broad and well sprung. The brakes had steel connecting rods and the brake pads pulled up on the rim. Then there was a vast bike rack on the rear, as solid as a helicopter pad. You could see its purpose, it had been built to carry a pig or even a grand piano.

So I started off. It was wobbly, tricky, the long rake to the front forks made steering a matter for concentration. But then I got the hang of it. One rode this bike in a very erect position, almost in the style of an Emperor.

The streets of Beijing were broad and tree-lined. On the main thoroughfares the motor traffic went down the middle and bicycles had their exclusive bike-ways down the side. There was a great deal of motor traffic, unending trucks and buses. There were no privately owned cars. "Too many people in China," said Mr Sung. "If they all owned cars, it would be terrible. Nothing would move."

There were cars, but either they were taxis, cars owned by communes, or limousines used by high officials. The limousines were big, black, formidable. They looked like stretched vintage Holdens and there were black curtains over the rear windows so that we could not see the illustrious people inside.

Yes, there were paths allegedly for bikes, but as they say in America, the traffic came "every which way". Cars came in from the side, buses ploughed in to stops and pedestrians scurried for their lives. In the first five minutes I nearly went to the Chinese land of heavenly peace. A bus pulled in to the kerb and swept me in with it. Just when I thought I had escaped there was more of the bus and more. It was an articulated vehicle, two buses joined in the middle. Like all these buses, it had a loud speaker and the driver was hollering in what could have been Mandarin. If Mr Sung had been available, perhaps he would have interpreted: "Get out of the way, you dumb foreign devil."

Along with 200,000 others, I cycled several times around Tian'anmen Square. This was a tremendous experience. What's more, I was learning to love my Chinese grid. The big saddle was comfortable, it was monumental indeed, but it had its own way of sticking to the road with all the confidence of a Mercedes Benz and I wished I had been able to ride it for the whole experience in China. By comparison, the Taiwanese bikes were toys. The chains broke links, cables snapped. We had a bike where the crank broke away as if it were made of plastic, and derailleurs gave up when they were under any strain. My Chinese had no brand name like 'Forever' but it did feel as if it were there for all time. When I returned the bike the beaming owner returned my passport and later I asked Mr Sung to compliment him on what a splendid machine it was.

The pedal around Tian'anmen Square was done with other things in mind. The ultimate ritual in China was to visit the tomb of Mao Tse Tung. Whenever we mentioned Chairman Mao's mausoleum the word came back: "Not possible."…"Not open."…"Sorry!". Suddenly, for reasons best understood by the Peoples' Republic, we were told we could go through the Chairman Mao Memorial Hall just hours before we left Beijing.

There was an astonishing queue which snaked its way all across the vast Tian'anmen Square. Not just a normal queue, but four deep and almost everyone wearing that drab featureless garb made famous by Mao himself. It was all well organised. Mr Sung formed 16 of us into a little company and we marched towards the queue. Mr Sung had his special permit and an official pointed the way we had to go. We were visiting foreigners and we didn't have to go to the queue's distant end, where, no question, we would have stood all day. We felt guilty as we were signalled in 100 metres from the front.

We inched forward. "No cameras, no bags, be sure to take off your hats and complete silence," warned Mr Sung.

The Mao mausoleum was in the centre of the square, a huge building to house the body of just one man. It had large imposing columns like the Parthenon and it was as big as the National Library in Canberra. On each corner there was a fearful sculpture showing Chinese people, soldiers, peasants, workers, male and female, making grand heroic gestures, arms held high.

We shuffled up the steps, still four abreast and entered the great hall. The first sight was a huge statue of Mao in white marble. Now the queue split, two one side of the statue, two the other, into the next room. Our hats were very correctly off and there was not a sound. We could now pass either side of Mao himself, lying in state. There were four young Chinese with rifles at the ready. Their faces betrayed not a sign of emotion, they could have been embalmed like Mao. There were flowers all around the bier, not flowers that one recognised, but they looked like red lilies.

We were quite unprepared for the sight of Mao. He did not look real. His face was lined, aged, very yellow and heavily made up. He looked like a Madame Tussaud model, but not a good one. This was a model that had been botched. We wondered how many more years the body of Mao would last.

Then everyone had the same thought: "Isn't he small?" He was much smaller than we expected.

No stopping, we were not allowed even to pause. Macabre it was, perhaps horrifying, and yet...and yet, it was a dignified, moving experience.

We left Beijing by aircraft back to Guanqzhou and now it was time for shopping. Glorious hand-made silk blouses were to be had for $15 to $20. We were taken to a silk factory and we looked in wonder at thousands of rolls of pure silk in myriads of colours. It suddenly occurred to me that my daughter Sarah was marrying Peter Cudlipp in six weeks time. If only? Yes, I could. I bought five yards of pure, heavy bridal silk for $65. The same silk would have cost hundreds in Australia.

I was on a bicycle at the time and I wondered how many parents had carried back the wedding dress for their daughter in a bicycle pannier. March 3, 1983 at Trinity College Chapel, Melbourne University was quite a day. Sarah looked very, very beautiful in that Chinese silk dress.
