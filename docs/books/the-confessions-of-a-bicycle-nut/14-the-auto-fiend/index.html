The Auto Fiend

16 min read.

First published 1999

A polemic against Australia's car addiction, from Melbourne's $2 billion freeway spend and France's 1998 one-day Paris car ban to the global rise of rail trails, closing with a science-fiction vision of a Melbourne of 2030 where the petrol has run out and bicycles rule the city again.

At our house we own two cars. There should be none, but we have become addicted like everybody else. There should be a new A.A. - Automobile Anonymous. This would be a counselling organisation which would help the severely addicted. It could counsel those who have an urge to own 300 kmh cars when the speed limit rarely rises above 100 kmh. It would counsel those who have a change of personality as soon as soon as they step into an automobile. At home they are kindly, courteous and good parents, but as soon as they get into a car they become possessed by a strange urgency, where all patience disappears and the ideal method of communication with another human is to raise two fingers in the air.

It would counsel those who feel they need a larger car so that they can impress and dictate and give good advice to those who feel degraded when they actually use public transport. I live by a mainstream road which leads into the big city. The agony begins soon after 7 a.m. It gets earlier every year. Back, circa 1968, the city invented 'clearways' with no parked cars on the way to town between 7 and 8.30 a.m. No parked cars on the other side for the way home between 4.30 and 6 p.m.

It worked for a while. But now my motoring friends find they have to leave home earlier and earlier so that their cars can get a quick run into town. Many of them leave home at 5 a.m. So the agony hour starts at dawn and there is a river of solid metal from traffic light to traffic light. Sometimes I count the number of cars occupied by one person, the ratio varies from one in 10 to one in 15. It can be stated safely that 90 per cent of all cars that drive to the city in Australia have only one occupant. The energy waste can hardly be calculated.

Meanwhile, my city is just completing the expenditure of $2 billion on an amazing circuit of freeways tunnels and bridges, one of the largest projects in the nation's history, to make things more comfortable for the automobile, to make it possible so that we can have more automobiles, to speed up traffic for more automobiles. Meanwhile, the Government is making public transport less attractive by giving us unmanned railway stations and trams and sacking all the tram conductors.

There was a movie titled The Cars that Ate Paris. Actually, cars eat everything. Every new building that is erected must have space for the car. Every new supermarket must have its auto bitumen playground. Those new freeways we are building consume 6.5 hectares per kilometre and when you put in the clover leafs and the service stations it goes up to 10 hectares a kilometre. Nigh on one fifth of a modern city is devoted to the car. (1)

It is so politically rewarding to back the motor car, no Government, Liberal or Labor, Federal or State, has the courage to do anything about it. The automobile is treated as God. Man has worshipped almost everything, the sun, the moon, the sea. In India they have worshipped the cow, and the Romans made a god of the horse. It is merely a modern manifestation of this to turn the car into a god. Alternatively, it could be like a disease, addictive drug, something we can't put down.

It is not absolutely true that no Government anywhere has tried to do something about the motor car. France tried an experiment on 22 March 1998. The Government banned the automobile for a day in 35 French towns and cities. It was an anti-pollution measure and it was enforced in 28 areas of central Paris, including the busy St Germain area, the Marais and Montmartre districts.

The reception varied. For example there was Emilie Hennen, who made her comments from the Brasserie Lipp in Paris while she was having her lunch time coffee. She said: "It is absolute bliss to sit here without breathing in the exhaust or being deafened by the cars and those damn horns. Why can't they do this every day?"

However others were forced into unfamiliar detours to reach their work places. 'What is this idiocy? I'm late and it's all your fault," shouted one Volkswagen driver at a policeman in the central Paris shopping area. The Paris Town Hall reported that traffic was down by 20 per cent in the morning, with a 65 per cent increase in the number of cyclists on the road. At the Town Hall 500 free bicycles were available - in exchange for a set of car keys. Motorists are hard to convince. A survey published to coincide with the campaign showed that only one in four drivers would willingly give up the use of their vehicles. (2)

It is puzzling why so many commuters battle their way into cities by car. Melbourne, for example, has an extraordinarily convenient public transport system. The Melbourne City Council in 1994 published a Transport Strategy Survey. This was the breakdown on how the commuters got to work.

On foot: 13 per cent
Bicycle: 1
Taxi: 1
Car: 31
Car passenger: 9
Train: 25
Tram: 16
Bus: 3
Others: 1

The Council did not explain the mysterious 'Others'. Perps it was by camel or horse, but why do so many put up with the inconvenience of the car? Psychologists have a theory about this. Public transport could get them to town efficiently, often at equivalent speed, at less expense and much less strain. Alas, there is no prestige to be had by coming to work by train, tram or bus. Others would not see your BMW, your Volvo or even your Commodore, which you polished so lovingly on Sunday morning.

The psychologists claim there is even more to it than that. The car driver needs his steel cocoon as protection. There is the fear of others, the fear of multiple contact with other human beings. The automobile is the security blanket, the drive to town is the chance to be alone, away from all other creatures. It is like crawling back into the womb. The electric windows are up, the interior is warm or air conditioned, and the commuter has all his or her dearly loved comforts, radio, stereo, compact disc player, car telephone, even a fax. The car commuter is hermetically sealed from the entire, horrid outside world. (3)

You can ask at what cost. Ivan Illich has been a great commentator on these matters. He estimated that the typical American devotes more than 1600 hours a year to his car. He sits in it while he travels, and while it stands idling. He has to earn the money to pay for it, petrol, tolls, insurance, taxes and tickets. He spends four of his 16 hours on the road or gathering resources to pay for it. The average distance he travels in his car is 12,000 km. So his accomplished speed over his 1600 hours is 7.5 km. He would be better off on a bicycle or a horse and cart. (4)

I am indebted to the popular American writer, Bill Bryson, for these figures published in his book A Walk in the Woods. The average total walking distance of the American adds up to 1.4 miles a week. This is the lot, walking from car to office, walking round the supermarket, up and down the office, through shopping malls, barely 350 yards a day. What's more, the average American gets his first car at 16 and that's the end of leg usage. The story in Australia is not vastly different.

It is not easy to discover the first person killed by the automobile. It has been suggested that Mr H.H. Bliss was the first. On September 23, 1899, he was helping a lady to step down from a tram in New York City. A horseless carriage came by and killed him. Since then 17 million plus have died. This is more than all those killed in death camps in World War 2. It is 18 times more than killed in the Korean war, 17 times more than in Vietnam and 130 times the death toll at Hiroshima. It is more than all the Americans killed in warfare since the birth of the nation. (5)

We try to make the automobile safer by manufacturing front ends that are not solid hunks of steel which immediately crush the occupants of the vehicle. The vehicles collapse progressively and at least those in the front seat are protected by air bags. Manufacturers crash their vehicles experimentally to find ways to make them safer. Yet no Government demands that automobiles be reduced in power, that they consume less fuel or that all engines be governed so that none can exceed the maximum speed limit.

We have campaigns against the destruction of forests, campaigns against uranium mining, campaigns against the destruction of the ozone layer, but campaigns against the automobile are few indeed. In 1979, an artist, Dustin Schuler, tried to make a point in Los Angeles. He went to a used car dealer, bought a 1959 Cadillac, took it out to a paddock and floodlit an eerie scene. He created a six metre nail, hired a cherry picker, hoisted the nail 30 metres into the air, then dropped it on the Cadillac, harpooning it perfectly, like a stick through a cocktail sausage. He kept it on show for six weeks. Huge crowds came to see it, and looked in awe at this ritual murder of an automobile. It was a nice change. At much the same time, another campaigner, Stanley Marsh III of Amarillo Texas, found a plot of land on Route 66, west of town, and buried 10 Cadillacs nose-first into the ground. It was an eerie sight, 10 automobile tombstones, their rear bumpers pointing up to heaven. (6)

Another point that few politicians recognise is this. What will we do for power in the next millennium? The happy thought is that something will turn up, science will solve our untidy problems. Oil will start to run short some time between 2005 and 2010. At the current rate of production of 23.6 billion barrels a year, oil supplies will last only another 43 years. There are few new oil fields and there is little doubt that 90 per cent of the world's oil fields have now been tapped and many of these have been drained. (7)

Consumption is going up all the time. It is soaring in Third World countries, soaring in Asia, soaring in Latin America, and the forecasts are that by 2010 consumption will be up by 60 per cent. In a single year the United States burns more petrol than an Alaskan oil field accumulates in 100,000 years. (8) One can imagine the international agony, the world strife, as we have less and less oil. Those countries still oil rich will be able to demand huge prices, but what of those who have none, India, Japan, and many countries in the European Union.

What are the alternatives? Electricity? Some sort of fuel has to be produced to make electric power. The natural gas will have gone. The timber will have gone. Do we dig deeper and deeper coal mines, adding to the hole in the ozone layer? Do we switch to nuclear energy a move already rejected by many governments? Or do we switch to solar and windpower, at this stage a chancy method for acquiring energy.

It is inevitable that by 2005 the price of a barrel of oil will double from $15 to at least $30, which will have dramatic effects on the use of the car. Cars will become less popular and cities everywhere will have to grasp the bitter truth: there is no place for a private automobile in an over-congested central business district.

We have been through great social changes before. The railway train had its first symptoms of death when Messrs Daimler and Benz invented the motor car, but the real agonies came after World War 2. Railway lines, particularly the smaller feeder lines, closed everywhere. The United States in its heyday boasted 480,000 kilometres of active railway. By the 1980s this had shrunk to 224,000. What to do with all this space? In 1986, an organisation was born in Washington D.C. called the Rails to Trails Conservancy. The Rail Trail was a brilliant idea. This was a track that had been engineered. The grade was splendid, never more than one in 20. Often it was elevated to escape floods, often it went through tunnels and deep cuttings and other times it went across soaring bridges. So enthusiastic people, both Government and private, converted railway tracks for use by bicycles, horses and hikers. The project grew. Now there are over 1000 Rail Trails that cover 16,480 km in 49 States.

Apart from recreation, Americans discovered Rail Trails make sound economic sense. In the old days the railway train brought business to town. Now it was doing it again. Rail Trail users became thirsty, hungry and tired. They needed food, lodging, B & Bs, clothes, shoes, equipment and, when they reached the end of the trail, often they needed someone with a bus to bring them home again.

This started a world movement. In 1999 Canada was building a 15,000 km trail which would link every province and territory. There was a marvellously active organisation in Belgium called Chemins du Rail. There was another in Spain which was converting 7000 kms of abandoned rail. Japan and New Zealand were both interested and an organisation called SUSTRANS was converting 5000 km of rail in the United Kingdom.

Australia, after the United States, is one of the best places in the world for Rail Trails. We have an abundance of disused railway lines, 10,000 kilometres of them. Two to 5000 km are worth converting. But here's the point. They do not get snowed under in winter. We have the ideal climate. Every State has an organisation with Government backing and rail trails are being opened all over. Some were discovered long before the Government or any local authority took an interest. They were good places for hiking or being adventurous on your mountain bike.

Victoria is out in front. It has 60 disused railway lines, 700 kilometres of which are on the way to becoming good trails. By January 1999, 120 kilometres of trail were fully converted. The State Government is spending $1.75 million over three years and it is expecting at least equal sums from local shires. The jewel in the crown is the Warburton-Lilydale trail.

This was everything a trail should be. The Warburton-Lilydale railway line opened in 1901 to serve the local fruit growers and market gardeners. Premier Henry Bolte closed it in 1964 and pulled up the rail lines as quickly as possible so that it wouldn't be opened again.

The trail, 36 kilometres long, was completed just before Christmas 1998. It has been an amazing community effort, much of it due to the Friends of Lilydale-Warburton Trail. There was so much mess to clear: old cars, refrigerators, mattresses, tyres. Weeds abounded.

There are bike trails now in every State of Australia, including a lovely trail in South Australia called the Riesling Trail, five kilometres from Clare to Watervale. Not far, but it could call for extraordinary stamina, you pass nine vineyards, including Tim Adams winery, the famous Jesuit vineyard, Sevenhill Cellars, then on to Stephen John Wines and Crabtree at Watervale. Darwin has a fine rail trail 22 km to Palmerston North with shaded vegetation most of the way. It is used by cyclists and joggers every day.

Tasmania has opened a 477 km trail which runs right down the centre of the State from Devonport in the North to Dover on the south coast. It links forestry roads, fire trails, country roads and a disused railway line. Tasmania's best rail trail runs from Queenstown to Strahan on the west coast. It has gone the full cycle, the old railway is being restored, but the trail will still run alongside. (9)

If railways have withered compared with the last century, what will come next century? The decline of the freeway? In 1978 a movie opened in Los Angeles titled Americathon. It was science fiction. The story took the audience into the Los Angeles of 1998, the year when petrol finally ran dry and not a moving car was to be seen. People lived in their cars and travelled by skateboard, on bicycle or by foot. There was one thing wrong with the movie: it had the date wrong. The year should have been 2030.

Many a time I have gone into a trance and tried to predict a scene when the petrol ran dry. Not in Los Angeles, however, my visions of the Melbourne of 2030 were much more clear. There I was sitting on a bench at the corner of St Kilda and Domain Roads. The air was as pure as if I were on the top of Mount Hotham. There was not the sound of a car or a motor bike or a motor mower. Actually, 30 or 40 university students were trying to mow the Domain with hand mowers.

As I wandered around I gathered a little information. Bicycles now made up 42.5 per cent of journeys into the city. Perhaps not quite as popular, but GMH was doing well with horse-drawn carriages. There was a cheap fibre glass gig known as the Holden Belmont. There was a lightweight Commodore VT Executive made of compressed carbon, but those who really wanted to impress bought the Statesman barouche made of titanium.

I did inquire what had happened to the million or so automobiles that had been on the road. A great many were dumped in Port Phillip Bay as breeding havens for fish. However, many people sought out fancy cars, particularly Rolls-Royces, BMWs and Mercedes. With their leather upholstery they made lovely little cuddle houses for use in the back garden. The smaller cars like the Suzukis, Festivas and Barinas were marvellous for telephone boxes. Teenagers adored them. You could put your little Suzuki anywhere, even in the living room and they could talk in there for hours without anyone hearing.

Some of the later models of Lotus and Ferrari were nearly all glass. You could fill them with water and turn them into an aquarium. Then, when food became expensive, and chooks returned to popularity, everybody wanted those big four wheel drives, the likes of the Patrol, the Pajero and the Land Rover. They made perfect chook houses and tool sheds.

The City made brilliant use of the West Gate Bridge and the lower Yarra bridge and the same thing happened in Sydney with the Harbor Bridge. Undoubtedly they were inspired by the Ponte Vecchio in Florence. Up on the main spans they created shopping malls with shops, pubs, restaurants, marijuana lounges and casinos, all with the best views in Australia. You could tie up your bikes or your horses down by the toll booths.

The freeways were a devil of problem; these huge ribbons of concrete snaking across the countryside were indestructible. They didn't need all that space for bike trails. There were some valiant attempts to cover them with ivy. The City Link tunnels were turned into wine cellars and mushroom farms, but I think the best job was done on the Western Ring Road and the citizens of the western suburbs were thrilled. Victorian Recreation, better known as Vic Rec, but once known as Vic Roads, turned it into a continuing series of tennis courts, roller skating rinks, basketball courts and cricket grounds. They did a particularly good job with the cricket grounds. They covered the concrete with mod grass. It was all very attractive when they had finished. It was simple really. They just changed the name from freeways to freeplays.

Naturally there was an immense boom in train and tram travel with much spreading of routes. The Met Transport system lost $12,000,102,000 and 50 cents in 2008. For the financial year of 2029 there was an actual profit of $177,975,000 and 22 cents, which was more than double that turned in by Crown Casino.

That last year, 2029, was considered a bad year for road accidents. There were 34 dead and 220 injured. Thirty fell off horses, a bicyclist hit a street pole (alcohol-related accident) and three had strokes alighting from hansom cabs. Oddly enough, the RACV and NRMA prospered as never before. They ran marvellous services. If your horse went lame, they galloped out with another to replace it. If your bicycle broke a chain or a spoke, the RACV fellow would pedal out to your assistance.

The disappearance of the motor car caused unemployment for a time, but with the general slowing down of transport there was vast employment to be had in fine old-fashioned industries, like blacksmithing, stabling, coach building and excellent little eating and rest houses appeared every 20 km along the freeplays. Soon everything was better than before.

The Melbourne City Council and all other councils needed a large staff for dung-sweeping. This may sound untidy, but it was great for the gardens. The roses in 2030 were the best in 40 years.
