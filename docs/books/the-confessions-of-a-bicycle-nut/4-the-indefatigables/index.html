The Indefatigables

30 min read.

First published 1999

Chronicles the mania for covering distance on the high-wheeled bicycle, from century-a-day riders and George Burston and H.R. Stokes' 1888 circumnavigation to Arthur Richardson and Donald McKay's competing rides around Australia and Francis Birtles' seven crossings of the continent.

No doubt it was the liberating force of the bicycle that produced the mania for covering distance. It began with the century-in-a-day mania. A century was a 100 miles. Every club would have its formal century days and all those who achieved the wondrous 100 would receive a gold medal and their names would be published in the Australian Cycling News.

In the United States the mania became a national affair. There was an organisation for century riders titled Century Riding Club of America. One character, Teddy Edwards of New York, announced he would ride 100 miles every day for a year. He started out on January 1, 1898 and after a breakfast of clam broth, oatmeal, a rum omelet and two cigars, he took off. He was going well until September 10 when he went down with typhoid fever. He had covered 25,000 miles. (1)

A century was never enough; the true enthusiasts had to do more, much more. In 1888 George Burston and H.R. Stokes rode bicycles around the world. They were the first Australians to attempt it and certainly amongst the first to do it on the old-fashioned high wheeler. They rode in knee breeches, flannel shirts with sleeves rolled up to the elbows and Panama hats. They started their trip in Melbourne on 10 November and covered 62 miles to Tallarook. On the second day there was a ferocious north wind and they reported at Glenrowan that the temperature was 108 degrees Fahrenheit in the shade. However they found Glenrowan interesting. This was where "the fight between police and bushrangers took place a few years ago" and they noted traces of bullet marks on the buildings.

They found it important to begin at dawn...absolutely the best time for travelling "for then the dew is on the ground, the air is scented and cool, birds sing and flit about the bush.. Later on," they said, "the sun's heat is terrible, the wind like a furnace blast and not a sign of animal life visible save sheep and cattle." As for the Hume Highway, at times it disappeared altogether. Just past Cootamundra the road ended abruptly. A stockrider arrived on the scene and told them to cut across the open bush for a mile. This they did, they took a compass bearing, battled through thick scrub, then followed a scarcely definable track for 10 miles. After that the road was good again.

They covered 700 miles, arrived in Sydney on November 17 then caught a boat. Their trip took them to Java, Singapore, Penang, Rangoon, India, Egypt, Palestine, Sicily, Italy, across Europe to England and Scotland, Ireland, then they returned to Australia via USA. They did 10,000 miles in 10-and-a-half months.

They were devoted to their ordinaries. George Burston reported in Calcutta they met a couple who had just ridden 500 miles averaging 50 miles a day on Safeties. They were patronising indeed. What hope did the Australians have of achieving this on high bikes. Burston and Stokes had to prove them wrong. They set out on the Great Trunk Road across India. Their average over five days was 88 miles a day. Then between Allahabad and Cawnpore they did 131 miles in 11 hours, 30 minutes. They felt their point was well taken. Who needs bikes with small wheels?

They arrived home in high spirits, both praising their high wheelers and praising the hospitality they received. "It was of the most lavish kind and made us weather-beaten wheelers feel that where Englishmen resided we could never feel strangers in a strange land and, furthermore, we thank God that, although living under Australian skies, we can claim to be members of the Grand Old Empire." (2)

Ladies too were indefatigable. Every club century ride included a few women. In January 1896 the Misses Benzley of Sunbury rode 130 miles, which the Australian Cyclist claimed was the longest one day distance covered by lady cyclists in Australia. They rode from Melbourne to Castlemaine and back. There had been heavy rain earlier in the week, so the mud was terrible. It took them two hours twenty minutes to negotiate the 12 miles of bog between Sunbury and Gisborne.

The ladies wore rational dress and rode diamond-framed Raleigh bicycles. After setting out as soon as it was light, they reached South Yarra and the G.P.O. at 10.30 p.m. (3)

Perhaps the most remarkable of the early female riders was Mrs E.A. Maddocks. She started bike riding in 1893. She was an important member of the Sydney Bicycle Club and she started the Sydney Ladies Bicycle Club in 1895. She did not wear the rational dress. She believed ladies should look like ladies. There is a photograph of her unsmiling, determined, wearing her long skirt, blouse with puffed sleeves, tight little waist and straw boater on top of her hair.

Mr and Mrs Maddocks bicycled to Melbourne in 1894. They left Sydney on 19 September. Mr Maddocks had been in poor health. His doctor advised him that he needed a thorough change so they decided to ride to Melbourne in "easy stages". It could not have been that easy. They did 570 miles in 10 days. The road was so bad they had to walk many miles. Mrs Maddocks rode a Conqueror fitted with pneumatic tyres. It weighed 36 pounds. Mr Maddocks rode a New Rapid which performed "splendidly". (4)

The following year they pedalled to Brisbane and back and on the New England Highway they averaged 80 miles, or 129 km, a day. Mrs Maddocks' Conqueror was a ladies' bike with the dropped frame.

Aeolus in her "ladies column" had no time for dropped frames. If women wanted to ride well they should ride on diamond frames and wear rational dress. Female bikes were no good. She said: "If cyclists cannot by reason of their costume ride diamond frames they should not go on tour when men are in the party. The average cyclist likes a better pace than the average cycliste on dropped frame can manage. Nothing, I imagine, must be more annoying for a man who is a fairly good rider, than to ride his cycle at a snail's pace the whole day beside a cycliste whether she be his wife, sister or sweetheart". (5)

Mrs Maddocks could hardly be described as slow, nor could Miss Lucy Beyer. Lucy rode from Sydney to Melbourne with her brother Adolph in November 1896. The Australian Cyclist described Lucy as young, tall, fair and remarkably pretty. They set out on 14 November. Lucy was on a 26-pound machine geared to 66. Conditions could not have been worse with rain and headwinds. The road up the Razorback was boulders, clay and loose sand, which the rain converted to glue. Very quickly Lucy had no brakes and she had to back pedal all the way down the other side.

Goulburn, Yass, Gundagai, when it wasn't raining and blowing a headwind, it teemed. Lucy had numerous punctures, broke a chain and walked 14 miles into Germanton. "Any cyclist," said the bicycle journal, "who has had the unpleasant experience of wheeling a machine through mud on a dark night, stopping occasionally to scoop mud out of forks and chains, can sympathise with Mr and Miss Beyer." Yet they did 93 miles before they reached Germanton. Lucy ultimately lost a shoe in the mud and had to walk a mile without it.

They arrived in Melbourne late on Saturday, November 21. Ah, said Lucy, if only the weather had been good, she could have averaged 100 miles a day. (6)

Perhaps Lucy had been inspired by Miss Anne Londonderry of Chicago whom The Wheeler magazine described as "a fair specimen of the go-ahead American young lady". She made a very curious deal. She was to girdle the globe within 15 months and earn $5000 without begging or accepting gratuitous support. Should she succeed she would receive a further $1000. There was also a condition that she was to cover 10,000 miles on a bicycle. Annie actually covered 28,000 miles. She was robbed by highwaymen in France, and while witnessing a battle between the Japanese and Chinese in Gasan, she received a bullet wound in the arm. In Stockton, California, she was run over by a cyclist, and spent five weeks in hospital. She had a bad fall in Gladbrook, Iowa, and rode 175 miles with a broken arm and did not receive proper attention until she arrived in Chicago. She did it all in the required 15 months, but her earnings only came to $4900. She had to raffle her bicycle to make up the rest. (7)

Treks between Sydney and Melbourne, Adelaide and Melbourne, and Sydney to Brisbane, became common. One of the pioneers was James Copland of the Sydney Bicycle Club. He wanted to prove that the tricycle was a machine that could cover long distances over all sorts of roads. He certainly found them between Melbourne and Sydney. He described the Hume Highway: 100 miles, good, 400 miles, middling to fair and 80 miles, utterly unrideable. Some of the worst was the road from Wodonga to Melbourne.

He rode a Cheylesmore Club tricycle, an exact facsimile of the machine that had been presented to His Royal Highness the Prince of Wales. He fitted it with two oil lamps plus a Gladstone bag and tool bag, weight 23 pounds, which he attached to the back curls of the cradle spring.

Tricycles in 1884 were an awesome sight. The rider sat between two huge spoked wheels with a third wheel which could be out front or out behind. There was a curious form of steering, rack and pinion, operated by a handle, and the fixed wheel was chain driven. Indeed the Cheylesmore was a sight to behold. Copland left Sydney from the Royal Hotel in George Street at 9.30 a.m. and he reported that on Monday the 18th he was at Yass. He set out at 4.30 a.m. with lamps shining cheerily and bowling along. He rode into Bowning, but here "unfortunately", a team of horses, feeding at a waggon, bolted on seeing this amazing contraption. They took off, galloping for eight miles before they stopped.

"Therefore," said Copland, "the teamster had to ride 16 miles to get his horses back where they started. He said some pretty things to me."

Just before he arrived in Melbourne there was an odd incident at Craigieburn. "A wood carter, although on the wrong side of the road, keeping on the only rideable track, to which I was entitled, and under the circumstances of mud, wind and rain, decidedly declined to leave. Mild expostulation gradually emerged into fierce argument and culminated in a mild application of Miller and Foley's persuasive art."

He was referring to Professor William Miller and the great Larry Foley. Professor Miller had a gymnasium in Swanston Street, Melbourne, and always signed himself "the champion athlete of the world". He claimed he was the world champion at boxing, fencing, wrestling, dumb-bell lifting and long-distance walking. Larry Foley, was only five feet eight inches in height and weighed a mere 10 stone seven pounds, but he fought all sizes in the classic manner with bare fists soaked in brine. He never lost a bare fist encounter.

Mr Copland was good at understatement and not good at modesty. He did not reveal precisely who was the victor but he did say the journey resumed with the wood carter "no doubt a sadder and wiser man". The trip took 12 days nine hours and he averaged 44 miles a day. There were a great number of letters and telegrams of congratulation, "the most notable being from His Excellency Lord Augustus Loftus, Governor of New South Wales". (8)

James had 18 days in Melbourne, which included a tricycle trip to Ballarat, then rode home again. The celebrated members of the Melbourne Bicycle Club, Bagot and Lee, rode out with him as far as Pentridge and they said good-bye over a bottle of Champagne and biscuits.

He had some troubles. Nine miles from Kyamba he was coming down a long hill at an unsafe speed and suddenly found himself rolling in the dust. The result, a buckled off-side wheel and a broken spoke. He took off the wheel, put it on a log, sprung it back into shape and repaired the spoke with a piece of fishing line. "A splendid job," said he, "which has since commanded universal approbation here in Sydney."

He liked to look after himself. At one stop he spoke of the excellent French claret he consumed. He wired ahead to Picton announcing that he would like hot supper of boiled fowl at 10 p.m. As he rode up, exactly to the minute, he was delighted to find it quite ready. But he was very cross at Camden, he went over the Razorback and arrived at 3.40 a.m. It seemed remarkable to James that not one hotel would open for him. So he took shelter under the verandah of the Camden Inn and camped on some forms. "When the landlord came out in the morning, I'll be bound he was somewhat surprised at the forms of the debris of bread and butter, jam, apple skins, oil spots, loose pieces of paper, etc., which I left as a legacy for not opening it to me."

James seemed to like confrontation. He was riding up Brickfield Hill into Sydney when several boys threw their caps at his wheels. He dismounted, grabbed one of the caps and continued at top speed until out of sight then threw it into a brick cart. James commented "the loss of the cap would ensure its owner a good thrashing and teach him such a lesson as will prevent him indulging in such a dangerous amusement in the future".

His average distance on the way home was 52 miles a day and his longest run, 66 miles. (9)

The road from Adelaide to Melbourne was even worse than the track between Melbourne and Sydney. Frank Toms, an Adelaide racing cyclist described it as execrable. On 26 November, 1893 he left the Adelaide Post Office and established the first record for the route, three days, 23 hours and 45 seconds. At times he tried to follow the rail route, riding on the ballast with awful results. He had 17 falls, one time buckling his wheel and putting his foot through the spokes. (10)

The really extraordinary people, the ultimate masochists, were those who wanted to cross the continent, pedal through the remote areas of the roadless outback, suffering as only explorers the like of Burke & Wills and John McDouall Stuart had suffered. In January 1894, S.M. Plover, an insurance agent with the Mutual Life Association, set out for the South Australian border from Brisbane. He had taken his first lessons on a bicycle only a few months before and rode 10,000 miles until he wore out his cyclometer. He went down through Adavale, Thargomindah and Eulo to Cooper's Creek. He startled the citizens of these areas, who had never before seen a bicycle.

The single track left by his bike caused the greatest astonishment. One West Queenslander came into town "aghast" at what he had seen. A single track like that could only have been caused by a wheelbarrow. But where were the footprints? Was the wheelbarrow being pushed by a ghost? One day Plover rode his bike round and round a clay pan. Local Aborigines came afterwards, very puzzled. What manner of beast had danced this remarkable corroboree? (11).

Percy W. Armstrong was the first of the real overlanders. He was described as one of "West Australia's crack riders". He was born in Melbourne but he started a very successful bicycle business in Perth. In August 1893, with fellow cyclist R. Craig, he started from Croydon in Queensland's far north and rode all the way to Sydney, 2500 miles in 50 days. From there Armstrong continued on alone to Melbourne. He set a new record, covering the 578 miles in four days, three hours, 45 minutes. Considering the roads, this was an unbelievable achievement. He was doing nigh on 140 miles a day. (12)

The first man to cross the continent straight up the middle was Jerome J. Murif. He rode from Adelaide to Darwin in 1897. He couldn't quite explain why he did it. In his autobiography he said he wasn't searching for honours, he didn't expect to find a peerage. There was just "a vague longing to do something which first flattered, then irritated, then oppressed me. In vain I tried argumentatively to push it aside".

He decided, although it was more dangerous, that he would do it alone. He chose an Electra roadster with 28-inch wheels and a diamond frame, 20 teeth on the sprocket and nine on the hub. Weight: 29 pounds. He used to talk to his bicycle almost as if it were human and call it 'Diamond.'

It had one brake, a bell, and he fitted it with tandem tyres in the hope they would be tougher. He carried a spare tube, chain links and rivets, copper wire, a file, spanners, pliers, patches, rubber solution, a long length of cord, a small bottle of matches, a revolver and ammunition. He fitted a pair of luggage carriers on the handlebars. He had a satchel over one shoulder and a quart tin of water under the main cross bar of the frame.

Incredibly, he travelled in striped pyjamas, because he thought this was the best gear for the tropics. He dipped the wheels of his bike in the sea at Glenelg, had the Post Master sign his log as proof of his departure and he was off. Very early he had a feeling of guilt. A good Christian should not ride on Sunday. So he said, he had to decide "which would be the greater sin, not to avail oneself of an inviting breeze or continue cycle-touring on the Sabbath".

He decided definitely that the greater sin would be not to keep riding and his conscience never worried him again.

As he pushed up towards Lake Eyre, he encountered blinding duststorms, loose stones, gibbers so rough he found the only way to progress was by a "Chinaman's trot". He wrote: "As the wind beat wildly into my face I heard it warningly cry, 'Go back! Go back!' and in the lulls it droned and muttered chidingly 'Obstinate fellow! Stupid fellow!' I stooped and in a short-lived sprint exerted all the strength I had to bore through the dust."

The flies and the venomous mosquitoes he found unbearable. The flies were so terrible they got into his eyes so that he could no longer see. But there was a greater danger.. "Water, water, it was never off my mind. To have it," he said, "was my greatest craving, to have plenty of it my chief aim."

He discovered one idea which was to become regular procedure for adventurers more than half a century later. He carried a waterproof sheet for use at night. "Because of the heat from one's body condensation sets in, with the result that the underside of the sheeting becomes wet. This discovery made for myself on arising next morning, turning the waterproof over quickly, I greedily licked up, cat-like, all I could of the precious dew."

He dreamed of water. He saw constant mirages. At times in his imagination he could hear water flowing, rippling over gravelly beds. On at least three occasions he nearly died of thirst. It happened near Goyder Waters, where he struggled over loose sandhills, often carrying his bike. "Fierce sun tormented me from above and blistering sand beneath." His tongue was swollen in his mouth. Eventually he found a well, where he drank and drank and drank. "Until I found myself seized with the most awful cramps I have ever had the satisfaction of getting the better of...for a long time I couldn't move, couldn't stand." Solitary graves were plentiful in the area, he said.

Ah, but his bicycle kept going without mechanical problems. Thorns, three-cornered tacks, he called them, were a constant worry. He wished that he had used two-inch tyres instead of the one inch. However, he deflated them a little to give better purchase over the sand. The burrs became so bad he created a "burr-dissauder" which he fitted over the tyres. He made it out of an old tin match box.

He lived on damper, salt meat, sugar and tea. On his way to Tennant Creek he lost his way, had to return on his tracks and he went without food for three days. He started to get the feeling that the whole of the Northern Territory was composed of flies and ants. When he pulled out a piece of meat instantly it was covered with flies before he could get it to his mouth.

Murif was typical of the bicycle explorers. He had little understanding of the indigenous people. He had been fearful of the Aborigines but they did him no harm, except perhaps mentally. When he saw them, totally naked, he was shocked. It gave him an uncomfortable, uneasy feeling. "Later this passes," he said, "and you look at ungarbed blackfellow as you might apes and monkeys in the zoological gardens."

When he called in at outback stations like the famous Elsey always he was received courteously as if he were a Governor's son, which was amazing. He admitted that he looked a remarkable sight, bearded, dirty, burnt and thin. One leg had gone from his pyjamas, but he did have a spare pair for best when he visited. He had to apologise, however, for his appetite. He said: "It is embarrassing when you eat twice as much as everyone else at the table and still feel hungry."

Murif left Adelaide on 10 March, 1897 and reached Darwin on May 21. There he dipped his bike in four feet of water at a place he called Bicycle Point. He said: "I looked fondly upon the bicycle which had served me so well, pressed gently one of its handles and whispered, Thanks Diamond". (13)

The Australian Cyclist was ecstatic at the news. The editor wrote: "When the cycle was first introduced to Australia little did the enthusiastic owner imagine that by its aid great continents of the world would be crossed with safety. The wildest dream has been realised and more. ...In every direction the cycle is gaining a firm hold. Horses by the thousand are being disposed of, livery stables are closing, and in a few years the cycle, driven by electricity, and manipulated with greater ease and certainty than the quietest of Old Dobbins will still further revolutionise traffic. A slight frame on a pair of wheels, impelled by the feet, enables the operator to laugh at space, catch time, and live a kind of will-o'-the-wisp life that only he who feels it and lives it, knows. ...The ride of Murif across the Continent of Australia has made the hearts of thousands of cyclists throughout the colonies leap with admiration." (14)

The first around Australia adventures took place in 1899. Arthur Richardson went clockwise on his own, starting from Perth. Richardson was the first to pedal the Nullarbor in 1896. The others travelling anti-clockwise and also starting from Perth, were the brothers Frank and Alex White. There was a third in the second party, Donald McKay. He was described as the son of a well-known Queensland squatter. He read about the Whites' proposed ride and asked if he could come too. He could not meet Alex and Frank in Perth, so he waited until they had ridden half way round the continent and met them in Brisbane on 28 July. The trio then set out north and there was a wonderful procession as they headed for Gympie accompanied by 200 members of the Brisbane Cycling Club.

It was in the Northern Territory after they left Anthony Lagoon on 28 September that they ran into trouble. They had 120 miles to ride to Powell's Creek. The police told them there was a firm track all the way but the heat was dreadful and the ride became like an episode in purgatory. The track was hard to find, so stony and rocky often they had to push their bikes. The solder had gone in their canteens, precious water was leaking away. Soon they were in a wretched plight. Frank Clune recorded in his book Last of the Explorers, "All three men were now suffering agonies. They sat by their machines, heads bowed, their tongues rasping in their mouths, their limbs weak from loss of perspiration...It was more than 12 hours since water had passed their lips and all the time they had been biking through a territory more torrid than the Sahara."

Myall blacks, naked, and with spears in their hands, directed them to a water hole. It was black with beetles, but it was enough to keep them alive. They pushed on, but the days following were even worse, and they were pushing their bicycles because they were too weak to ride. Frank White said he felt strong enough and he would ride on alone and seek help.

This he did, but he lost his way and he had gone 96 days without food and water before he reached Anthony's Lagoon. (15) He found help and two Aboriginal stockmen went out to rescue the others. It was a slow business for Donald and Alex. Alex was very weak and pushed his bike while the stockmen carried his gear. When they arrived at Anthony's Lagoon, Frank White came out to greet them and — this was amazing — with him was Arthur Richardson, the clockwise adventurer, the lone cyclist who had ridden from Perth the other way. They swapped notes together, each telling the other what lay ahead. Donald, Alex and Frank, still shocked over what they had been through, drew heart from Arthur Richardson. If he had ridden through the north-west, so could they.

At Darwin they had pre-arranged a shipment of new tyres and spare parts, but when they called at the office of the shipping agent there was bad news. The parcel had missed the boat and there would not be another ship for 25 days. Their tyres were threadbare and the roughest part of the ride was ahead. They searched the shops and there were no tyres, but they managed to find two new inner tubes, three pounds of rubber solution and some patches, so they decided to push on. Near Pine Creek Frank White broke a main crank. There was no way they could repair it. He was out of the ride. Donald and Alex continued, Frank returned to Darwin on his way home.

At Koogabring Station on Elvira River their tyres were in shreds. The only thing to do was to improvise. They found some untanned calf's hide, called green hide. They scraped off the hair, cut it into strips, and soaked them in water. Then they stretched the hide strips and bound them around the rims over the frayed tyres. The wet hide dried, shrank and all this made impressive protection. It managed to get them to Derby, where at last they found their shipment of tyres forwarded on from Darwin. But the hide tyres were not exactly a holiday. Donald scored a puncture near Hall's Creek. It was impossible to remove the tyre for repairs so he had to stop and pump every 20 minutes.

In the North West they had 250 miles of what they described as an "unpathway of scrub, rock and swamp" and three-quarters of it had to be walked. Donald said he became so skin sore with mosquito and ant bites he had to take off his clothes and walk with a piece of mosquito net around his loins. (16)

On 27 January at New Norcia they had another amazing surprise: they met Frank White. He had mended his crank in Darwin, cycled down the centre to Adelaide, across the Nullarbor and now up the coast to join them. So now they were all together again. They arrived in Perth on 5 February. Arthur Richardson had arrived there only two days earlier, the first to bicycle right around Australia and he had done it in 243 days four hours.

Donald McKay wanted to beat Richardson's record. He worked out that it was 3747 miles to Brisbane and he had just 50 days to do it. If he did it non-stop, that was 75 miles a day. It seemed impossible and he was crossing the Nullarbor at the height of summer.

Frank White went as far Adelaide, Alec continued with him to the Victorian border where, weak with dysentery, he could go no further. Donald reached Sydney on 19 March. He had lowered the Perth to Sydney record from 44 days to 40. Then he rode to Brisbane and set another record. He had ridden from Perth to Brisbane in 48 days, four hours, 15 minutes. The previous record was actually held by Frank White, done in 53 days, seven hours.

So Donald McKay achieved his goal; he had beaten Arthur Richardson's round Australia ride by three days. He rode to the Brisbane Post Office accompanied by cheers and hundreds of cyclists. (17)

Arthur Richardson's ride was more remarkable because he did it alone. At a reception in Perth, the Mayor, Alex Forrest MLA, said earlier explorers set out with considerable provisions, attendants and teams of pack horses. Richardson did it on his own, on a bicycle, 10 times as fast just carrying 20 pounds of flour.

Richardson was an engineer, the son of a doctor. He worked on the goldfields around Coolgardie, and became well versed, as he put it, in the art of living like a blackfellow. He bought a bicycle in 1896 and three weeks later announced that he would ride across the Nullarbor via Eucla and Port Augusta to Adelaide. Nobody had done this before and he received plenty of warnings not to attempt anything so foolhardy. However, he did it with a remarkable lack of drama. And this applied to most of his bicycling.

He was skilled at living off the land, catching fish, and eating plants. On his trip around Australia he ran into serious trouble only once, between Wave Hill and Victoria Downs. The country was rough and trackless. He went to the local Aboriginal camp for help and two of them volunteered to show a short cut over the ranges. Richardson wrote:

Only one of them turned up and he was anything but a beauty. He was one of the most evil looking niggers I have seen with a peculiar squint in his eye. I was somewhat distrustful of him. Our provisions were made up of flour, meat, tea, sugar, which I packed into two 50 pound bags. Strapping these bags together by the tops, the boy (all niggers are called boys) carried it across his shoulders. We started off with 100 miles walking in front of us, and no track. The nigger had stated he knew a short track across the hills....

By sundown we tramped 18 miles....I gave the nigger to understand that an attempt to desert meant severe punishment and after tea we commenced cutting grass to make a camp. The boy set to work at the spot where we crossed the creek, while I went lower down. Getting a good armful I started back, and as I rounded the bend there was the boy with my tucker-bags etc. And his own spears, running up the opposite bank. As I was about 80 yards away there was no chance of catching him. I fired, and the ball split against the bank just in front of his nose. With a yell of terror he dropped everything he had, and scrambled into the long grass like a dingo. I never saw him again.

Richardson wrote this in a pamphlet titled The Story of a Remarkable Ride produced by the Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Company. The cover carried a dramatic sketch of Richardson firing his revolver at the fleeing 'boy'. It looked like something out of Ripping Yarns.

Richardson said here he was in a locality as wild and treacherous as any in Australia. The coming days were awful in their loneliness. He had little idea where he was. He travelled over creeks, gorges, one gully after another. At times he thought he was making great headway only to come against a blank wall with an impossible range of sheer cliffs. The blacks, he said, would follow him along the tops of the cliffs for miles and every moment he expected a shower of spears. He presumed they were waiting for him to camp, but at sundown he would boil his billy, then push on in the dark and "slinking through the bush like a dingo, camp away from his bike, no fire, and myriads of mosquitoes to worry him until dawn".

His food ran out. Now he lived on fish, wild fruits, berries, and lily roots which he obtained by groping shallow pools. Yet eventually he found his way:

One fine, clear morning, I clambered to the top of a range, and winding away through the hills beheld the Victoria River. Following its course along a very rough pad, and feeling terribly done up and ill, I reached the station a day or two later. I must have looked a pretty object with my beard half grown, my shoes tied together with pieces of hide, and the soles made of green hide laced on to the uppers. You can imagine how welcome under the circumstances was the hospitality extended to me by Mr Watson, the manager. For ten days, while I suffered terribly from a sort of low fever, he tended and nursed me, and had me fit and well for the journey to Katherine. (18)

The Dunlop publication commented that one would have thought when the ride of 11,500 miles was over Arthur Richardson would have taken a rest. Instead, he immediately volunteered for the Westralian Bush Contingent to go to the Boer War. He accepted the position of Sergeant Major, but "unfortunately, after braving all the risks on the ride around the continent", he fell off his horse during mounted drill and broke his arm. However he still went off with his troop to the Cape.

Many cyclists pedalled their way back and forth across the nation, but perhaps the most indefatigable was Francis Edwin Birtles. He was born in Fitzroy, Melbourne, the son of a bootmaker. He joined the merchant navy at 15, then jumped ship at Cape Town in 1899 in the hope of enlisting in the Australia Militia. This didn't work but he did manage to attach himself to a troop of irregular infantry. Then when the war was over, he became a mounted police officer in the Transvaal. (19) This is how he honed his survival techniques in arid environments. He did 20,000 miles by horseback and crossed the Great Karoo on a bicycle.

In 1912 the journal Lone Hand made the comment that Francis Birtles knew more of Australia than any other man. He had cycled seven times across the continent and twice around it, taking a new route on each occasion. (20)

On 21 August 1912, he rode from Sydney to Brisbane, to Normanton, Darwin, Alice Springs, Adelaide and back to Sydney. He wrote down carefully what adventurers needed for a trip like this. He had a Speedwell bicycle fitted with three-speed gears, free wheel, front hub brake and Dunlop canvas lined tyres. As for his gear, he had fitted to his bike a bronze metal tank, 1.5 gallons. He carried a light waterproof sleeping bag, a Winchester .32 calibre repeating rifle with 200 rounds of ammunition, camera, waterproof canvas bags for flour, tea, sugar. He had a folding double billy can, one for cooking, one for tea; concentrated foods, Bovril, grapenuts, soup tablets, sausages, and chocolate. He had a compass, a barometer, matches in waterproof cases and a cyclometer. Medicines: boracic acid for eye wash, quinine for malaria, and potash for cleaning muddy water and an antidote for bites. Clothing: he had a wide felt hat, flannel singlets, woollen guernsey, woollen racing knickers and long topped boots to help the legs and keep out sand and water. His bike weighed 25 pounds and his gear 85 pounds.

His advice:

The crystal spring of life of the poets would be a sad misnomer, for there isn't any crystal and there isn't any spring, and it is wise, if you can, to filter the liquid through your teeth. It is no uncommon experience to have to drag the carcass of some animal that has jumped into the waterhole and been too far to jump out again. Should you happen to miss the waterhole you get an experience that ought to satisfy you for life.

Headwinds are the overlanders curse. On the dust plains blinding, stifling dust storms blow for days altering the face of the landscape.

No matter what happens, be it rain, dust, missed waterhole, breakdown or sickness itself, the overlander must keep moving for delay may mean death. (21)

Birtles said you couldn't escape the sand, it penetrated everything, every secret crevice and filled your boots until you felt they were encased in molten lead. Nights were made hideous by the howling of wild dogs and demented dingoes. Sleep was impossible through the invasions of ants and mosquitoes. He told the story of the American Colonel who used no nets on his bed:

When I go to bed I am so intoxicated that I don't mind the mosquitoes, and when I awake the mosquitoes are so intoxicated they don't mind me.

Alas, the overland cyclist did not carry this remedy.

He had his adventures. On the road to Gympie he was caught in a bushfire, the hair on his arms, legs and head all were burnt. His tyres, burned by cinders, burst and he had to sprint for his life riding on the rims. He then walked the next 50 miles to Gympie. In the Northern Territory he was caught in the wet season and waded in mud so deep he lost his boots. Several times he was incapacitated with malaria. At Darwin for 48 hours he was "blind, raving, singing and shouting" and he spent 11 days in hospital.

On the way to Newcastle Waters he thought he would get his revenge on the howling dingoes and attacked what he thought were some dingo pups. It was a big mistake. The dogs belonged to Aborigines. "It's well known," said Birtles, "that the natives think more of their dogs than their children." Soon after this he was under attack when a hail of spears came straight at him.

"I pedalled for all my worth. One spear better judged than the rest found an opening between the spokes of my front wheel, but the angle at which I was going caused it to snap and the broken half caught me a severe rap on the right arm."

He survived and he told future bikers how to do it. A white cockatoo made a fine dish, roasted or stewed. Carry a rabbit trap, this would provide many a meal. Often he ate iguana, but one time he tried flavoring it with leaves and it ate into his vitals, so that it flavored everything he tasted for months. He swore he would rather die than eat iguana again. (22)

Birtles then stopped his bicycling and completed the first west-to-east crossing of the continent by car. He drove a Brush single cylinder 10 horsepower open tourer. He said: "Given good roads and a reliable car, there is no mode of travelling to approach motoring. But when the roads are bad - or when there are no roads at all - it is more comfortable to stay at home." Constantly he was asked which was the most useful, the car or the bike? He replied: "For crossing country the car requires petrol and oil depots...those supplies cannot be replenished in an emergency. The most reliable motor may break down under trying conditions...A bicycle is consistently fast for thousands of miles. It is reliable, it scorns most obstacles, is easily repaired. Summed up, with a car it is all worry, with a bicycle it is all work. Personally I prefer the work." (23)
