Edna at fifty

11 min read.

First published 2006

Charts the creation and evolution of Dame Edna Everage, from her debut at a 1955 Melbourne revue through her transformation into an international megastar, tracing the gladdie song, the sequined gowns, and her conquest of the Apollo Theatre and the world.

Humphries the satirist has created an amazing string of characters. There was Lance Boyle, the trade union leftie, who chewed cigars and found very lively things to do on 'missions'. There was Big Sonia, the folk singer, who wore Roman sandals, a long black wig and a micro skirt. There was Debbie Thwaite, the gushing nicely brought-up girl who was having a 'lovely time' in London and favoured lovely pure wool twin sets. Then there was Buster Thompson, the ex-public school boy who jogged, drove his Mercedes, knocked over lots of tinnies of Foster's and even enjoyed the obligatory coronary. And, of course, there was Neil Singleton, advertising executive, culture freak.

There are another three, let us say four, who have survived use-by dates far beyond those of 99 per cent of actors: Dame Edna Everage, Sandy Stone, and Sir Les Patterson. Then there is Madge Allsop, Dame Edna's incredible suffering bridesmaid. Madge, allegedly from New Zealand, is drab, mouselike, mournful, always silent and as emotional as a St Kilda Road statue.

In 1955 things were not good for Barry Humphries. He had failed at university. He had rejected what his parents felt was the right path, leaving the record industry and making a career in art, but he was more attracted by the theatre. He was appearing at night in repertory and joined the Union Theatre Repertory Company. The director was John Sumner, who offered Barry, age 21, the role of Duke Orsino in Twelfth Night.

'My first thought was, how am I going to tell my folks? This was an inconceivable way of earning a living. What was I? Who had ever met an actor? A fellow with dyed hair once came to school to read us poetry. He was considered a very ODD sort of person.'

He wanted to be a famous actress on stage, playing the role of Viola. And Ray Lawler, the resident writer-director, was writing the play Summer of the Seventeenth Doll. He used to do one-night stands in town after town, and inevitably there would be the official mayoral reception at the Town Hall complete with Mayor and Lady Mayoress, cakes, sandwiches and sponge fingers. Between towns there would be long boring bus rides. Barry had some ideas for amusing the cast. He did the civic receptions over and over again. His portrayals of Lady Mayoresses were as splendid and accurate as the real McCoy. The rest of the cast loved it, but his Duke of Orsino was less convincing than his mayoral ladies. He lacked concentration, forgot lines, and Sumner wondered if he would make an actor.

At the end of the season there was a revue, which Ray Lawler was directing. Barry was wondering what he could do, and Lawler said, 'Why not do a sketch for that character you were giving us in the bus? What's she called?'

Humphries plucked out the first name that came into his mind. 'Oh, I think she's called ... Edna.' Edna was the name of his old nanny back at 36 Christowel Street. 'She comes from Moonee Ponds,' he added.

'Who will I get to do it?' asked Barry. 'How about Zoe Caldwell?'

'No, you do it yourself,' said Ray Lawler.

He pondered carefully what to wear for this Victorian first solo performance. He chose a blue floral dress, a pink twin set and a pointed yellow hat. On his hairy legs he had men's short socks, and with bright red lipstick he gave himself a comical mouth. So was born Edna Everage of Moonee Ponds. The sketch was all about billeting guests for the 1956 Olympic Games in Melbourne.

For 18 months Edna was needed for visitors: the Olympic Games required 100 beds. Mr and Mrs Horace Hope lent the lounge. She was interviewed. From the beginning Edna revealed her home and her life. She described her bedroom:

'There's our bedroom, of course. Norm, that's Valmai's father, has just painted it a lovely shade of duck-egg blue, and the bird's eye maple two-piece suite looks lovely against it, and I've just bought a new chenille bedspread in a sort of —'

Then on to the lounge and dining room. 'If you open the double doors — little Kenny, that's my youngest, always calls them the reindeer doors because of the sand-blasted reindeers on the glass — if you open the double doors it's a lovely big room. We pushed back the Genoa velvet couch and rolled back the burgundy Axminster squares. Valmai's young people had the time of their lives.'

Many thought Edna Everage was so local, so suburban Melbourne, she could barely survive beyond Mordialloc, let alone go to Sydney where, as everyone knew, people were more rakish, abandoned and prone to gambling. However she did go to the Phillip Street Theatre in Sydney, where the Olympic Games sketch was amazingly popular. Edna began to blossom. No longer lacking in range, she was now a force with a mind of her own.

Barry had to think carefully about what she should wear. He was nervous, even frightened when he entered a well-behaved department store like the Myer Emporium in Melbourne's Bourke Street.

'First, I used to go into a store and say I need some clothes for my sister. She's sick in hospital so I have to buy for her.'

'What size, sir?'

'Well, oddly enough, she's exactly the same size as me.'

Later, he said, in a shop in Oxford Street, London, he tried the same routine and the young female behind the counter replied: 'Oh, don't worry, dearie, all the boys buy their clothes from us.'

Barry was friends with Lil Wightman. Lil was the doyen of fashion in Melbourne. In 1925 she opened 'Louvre' at the top end of Collins Street, a salon that epitomised late French fashion. It was Lil who turned her into a Pitch into the so-called — Her trademark was her spectacles. She had them especially handmade and they were extraordinary. They had great diamond-studded wings which opened up on either side. As soon as she saw them Edna Everage had to have a pair, indeed a dozen pairs. They suited her burgeoning personality.

Bert Newton, as a turntable boy, remembers her entering the studio and thundering 'Park it! Park it!' She drove a large American limousine and she could be seen coming down Lonsdale Street with her nose just above the steering wheel.

In the fifties Barry grew his hair long. This was long before the Beatles made it fashionable. As he put it, his long hair was enough to make gentle citizens cross to the other side of the road in horror. He was in the Journalists' Club in Sydney at the time he was performing in the revue at the Phillip Street Theatre. It was late at night and he spotted Kenneth Slessor playing billiards. Here was Australia's greatest poet — dare he go up and speak to him? He did. 'Mr Slessor, I have always been a great admirer of your work.'

Slessor looked him over. 'Why don't you get your fucking hair cut?'

Of course, the lank hair could not stay. Edna acquired gorgeous bouffant hairdos in the richest shade of mauve. She explained she had always been unique. Even in the playground at the Moonee Ponds Girls School other little girls had admired her mauve hair. Her standard of dress improved rapidly. She went from cotton, to seersucker to Orlon. In the 1970s she turned out in trendy denim, then in Thai silk. The colours varied from oyster to cyclamen, to salmon to peacock. She covered herself in sequins, diamanté and jewels. She had panels which opened to reveal kangaroos, emus and perhaps even the odd platypus. Her clothes were being made by the finest couturiers in London and Paris at the cost of thousands of pounds. She could look down on the 'paupers' in the audience and wish, 'in the kindest possible way', that they could dress as expensively as she.

She had from the beginning an ear for catching every cliché and every prevailing prejudice. It could be dangerous territory. Even in her first performances she was talking about the Catholics. Her voice would go up octave by octave until, as one critic pointed out, it had the hysteria of a railway whistle: 'They always get the best positions, don't they?' At first there was a titter. 'Was it politically correct to do this?' Then a feeling of relief, the laughter filled the theatre.

Edna reinforced that view with the line: 'Not that I have anything against Roman Catholics. On the contrary, one of my best friends is Polish, single, and lives in Italy.'

Every minority received its turn: lepers, 'little tinted people', and Jews — they were 'my friends the Red Sea Pedestrians'. Norm, her husband, received 'the treatment'. He was in hospital in a Prostate Emergency Support Unit, but she was so caring and compassionate. She would visit his room at least once a day and fill his drip with something yummy.

There were dozens of methods 'in the most caring and compassionate way' for humiliating her audience. The paupers in the gallery were one thing; the elderly bewildered in the dress circle were another, but she was merciless with latecomers. Her finest latecomer triumph was in Adelaide in 1976. Barry said, 'I noticed that in the entire theatre only two seats were empty. House seats they were, usually kept for very important people. I let the audience in on this secret and there was a nice sense of anticipation. Then for half an hour after the show started they arrived. I have my powerful torch built into my handbag. I turn it on by a little rosette.' The beam shone full on a gentleman dressed in the fineries of youth, tiptoeing in, and on his arm was —

Don Dunstan, Premier of South Australia, was the audience's target that night. The audience cheered. Then they quietened a little. Edna said, 'I am often asked, what is the latest thing in the theatre in Australia? Ladies and gentlemen, he has just walked in.'

He went from mere star to superstar, even gigastar. Of course, as Edna became too important for humble Moonee Ponds. She had to find a place a little more fitting. She moved to Highett and gave birth to her most enchanting song, the Highett Waltz. This was the fourth verse:

For I'm dying to try it in Highett With the aid of those nice housing schemes, Where the cream brick veneers stay hygienic for years In Highett the place of my dreams.

But then there was the irony here — Edna Everage megastar was born in Moonee Ponds, so obviously it had to become fashionable. As Edna said, 'Because of me everyone wants to live in Moonee Ponds ... real actresses, feminists and lady novelists.'

Edna was not an immediate success in London. This strange apparition from the colonies was an acquired taste. Humphries performed in Oliver! and in Excuse Me with a brief London season in 1963. By 1965 he had noticed passing interest but had not yet broken through. The Spectator critic described one performance as 'distinctly soporific'. The show closed after three weeks.

Then came the year Edna became superstar and virtually megastar. That was 1976. The show was Housewife Superstar! and Barry chose the Apollo Theatre — a large theatre in the West End. This was an example of the fearless Humphries and a trust in his own talent. Critics thought he would be lucky to last 15 or 20 performances. There was another big show opening the same night, so he insisted on opening at midnight. He had a handy punch line: he couldn't get the critics to come because the pubs hadn't closed. It was a great success.

He threw gladioli at the audience and had them waving their gladdies in phallic unison. Dressed brighter than any neon sign, Edna hauled a man on to the stage and forced him down with a red kiss on his bald head and shrieked, 'I'm your fairy godmother!' The show ran for five months and there was never an empty seat.

The origins of the gladdie phenomenon were with a vase of gladioli sitting on the accompanist's piano in London in 1965. Barry had noticed a woman shyly eyeing them enviously, so he gave one to her. To his astonishment she started passing the gladdies along to others in the front row. Suddenly he had the idea — why not get everyone to wave their gladdies in time to the tune of 'Home Sweet Home'?

From then on gladdies were the climax to every show. They had to be there, summer, winter, spring, no matter where, and often at great expense. The gladdie song went to the tune of 'Coconuts':

I've got a lovely bunch of gladdies, See them all waving in the stalls, Big ones, small ones, Give 'em a twist, a flick of the wrist, There's lots of different ways of arranging — Just remember all you mums and daddies, There's lots of flower power to be had — All you need's a lovely bunch of gladdies And you'll never be sad, as long as you wave a glad!

Humphries' triumphs continued. In 1987 he was at the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane in Back With a Vengeance. This was the first solo act to play and fill that theatre with its magnificent audience capacity since it opened way back in 1663. The show ran for nine months.

His popularity was ever expanding. He was gaining worldwide exposure through London Weekend Television with The Dame Edna Experience. Here was the Dame at her most hysterical and with ultimate power. It was a satire on all talk shows. She was the ultimate authority on all matters and her guests were rarely allowed a word. The guests included grand names such as Larry Hagman, Zsa Zsa Gabor, Douglas Fairbanks Jnr, Jeffrey Archer, Joan Collins, Tony Curtis and Bob Geldof. The set was a double-storey penthouse with a grand staircase suited for appropriate entrances by all manner of world royalty. Television viewers looked agog as their favourite celebrities were submitted to continuing indignities. The most spectacular was a trapdoor. If Dame Edna found her interviewee unsatisfactory, she pulled a lever and the unfortunate person disappeared, presumably shot down a tunnel and ejected back into the street.

This proved unequivocally that the Dame — gigastar — was more important than any of them.
