The most important entertainer

5 min read.

First published 2006

Introduces Barry Humphries as Australia's greatest all-round entertainer, setting out the range of his talent, his uncanny memory, and the ferocious mockery he faced at home before the rest of the world acknowledged his genius.

Barry Humphries is the most important entertainer-artist Australia has produced, he says in this volume, echoing the view held by those formidable Dames, Nellie Melba and Joan Sutherland.

Geoffrey Dutton, the writer and poet, put it another way. 'Genius is a category by itself. Though we have some poets who have written poems that will be read as long as people have poetry, I think we have only two writers (using the word in the broadest sense) of genius: Patrick White and Barry Humphries.'

Barry stands out because he has talent in all directions. He is a one-man show. He does everything. Reporters, biographers, TV people, hover near waiting for him to say something. He is so quotable. He is like an Oscar Wilde. Everything he says is worth putting down.

He treads a path which almost no one has taken before. He has studied suburbia as if under a microscope, identifying all sorts of mores which originally belonged to Melbourne, but are truly universal.

He writes and acts his own material. So many actors, so many comedians once off the stage are different people, as homely as the character next to you in the tram. Not Humphries.

It has been said often. It's not fair that he has so many talents. Any newspaper or magazine, be it the New York Times, the Spectator or the Sydney Morning Herald is desperate to get an article from him.

He is a brilliant writer, and even as a schoolboy he was brilliant at comic verse. He has written a novel, he is a song writer. He is a competent cartoonist and held several one-man exhibitions. His art gear goes wherever he goes.

Perhaps one key to his remarkable talent is his memory. It is a combination of encyclopaedia and thesaurus. He sprinkles his books with words like jaloux, propugnation, becquerel, sirupy, coppice, and for example, Les Patterson's trousers are priapally padded. Are you with me?

Old friend, film director Bruce Beresford, is amazed at his memory. He says Barry can identify any piece of music after hearing just two notes, that he has total recall of every book he has ever read, and never forgets a name or a face. He always asks to be remembered to wives, husbands, children, always recalling their names.

This memory enables him to be an expert on the Australian language. It is doubtful if anyone has had such an ear for the tone, the sounds, the phrases heard in our suburban pubs, supermarkets, coffee lounges and even boardrooms. He has gathered them all and we hear them in the theatre.

He has the mind of an Oxford don, an amazing esoteric knowledge of obscure English writers, and a devotion to Australian art, cluttering his cottage walls with the works of Charles Conder, Arthur Boyd, Arnold Shore and others.

There have been sessions that have been described as 'An Evening with Barry Humphries'. The theatre has been filled with celebrities and would-be celebrities, and for more than two hours they have asked questions and Humphries has simply chatted back with overpowering wit and skill, all ad lib, nothing primed.

He can dance. He can sing. Then there is the timing. All great comedians have this perfect sense of timing. One thinks of Roy Rene, Danny Kaye, Tony Hancock, they were the same. But Humphries has a versatility which is amazing. He is really the perfect music-hall entertainer, a type which is virtually extinct. He is the last of the vaudevillians.

There has always been a core of self-doubt hidden within him. He is, he says, 'ferbly nervous' the night before a show. When you are in the business of live entertainment you can't afford to hesitate. You must have the courage and effrontery and twenty per cent talent.

The early days were never easy for John Barry Humphries. He had a headmaster who said to him, 'Humphries, are you sure you're not just a flash in the pan?' He was bitter about critics, about Adelaide and the reception of his first gramophone record. 'It was always an open question how long Barry Humphries would last.' Knockery was a national characteristic, said Humphries.

He has reacted savagely to those who suspected his motives, to those who believed that anyone who left Australia's shores should be a mobile koala, a travelling public relations man. He was knocked many times in Parliament because he was not fitting the public image when abroad.

The most celebrated case concerned Harry M Miller, the show business entrepreneur. In 1969 Miller returned to Sydney from London and told reporters, 'That man Humphries is an idiot. We have enough trouble convincing the world Australia is sophisticated. Yet we have this idiot Humphries running around ruining the country. He is doing more harm to Australia each night on stage in London than a whole bunch of our dowdy politicians could do in a month.'

His show was being accompanied by a dear friend in improving it by issuing the audience with Australian flags, dressing the usherettes as koalas, and playing 'Waltzing Matilda' on the didgeridoo. He said further that he was making an intense study of Mr Miller's latest shows in the hope of learning further lessons in patriotic playwriting.

But how difficult it is to identify the real Barry Humphries. It is easy to think of him as the 'wild one'. Perhaps he does have something of the personality of Dame Edna or Sir Les Patterson. The truth is something different. There are many shades and many facets: anger at the incompetence of bureaucracy, he is a rebel against ignorance, and curiously, both a pacesetter and a conservative. In the 2005 Who's Who in Australia he lists his favourite recreation as 'baiting Republicans'.

He certainly tends to be conservative in politics and as for 'baiting Republicans', why not? Dame Edna is terribly close to the Queen. Edna even suggests that Her Majesty is jealous of her dress sense.
