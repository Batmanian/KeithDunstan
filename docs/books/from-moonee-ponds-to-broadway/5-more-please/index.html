More please

9 min read.

First published 2006

Recounts the decade-long drinking crisis that threatened to end Humphries's career in the 1960s, including his arrest on Burke Road and a savage mugging outside a Richmond factory, before tracing his recovery and his creation of the Barry McKenzie comic strip for Private Eye.

It was in the 1960s that the strain of being Barry Humphries began to show. In his autobiography My Life as Me, he has described his drinking regimen. In the mornings he managed to get to the pub 'precisely as it opened its doors' and he didn't have to name his poison to the barmaid, she already knew. He loved a Mayfair drinking well that was open from 11 in the morning until 11 in the evening. 'There were few bars in London that I liked, and few sensitive bartenders. If, when I ordered a drink the bartender had the impertinence to say "But, Sir, you haven't finished the one you're drinking," I would never go back again.'

By 1966 the alcohol problem was serious indeed, but he was remarkable: his memory did not fade and he did not miss a performance or a public appearance, but often needed half a glass of brandy just to keep going.

On 15 June 1970 police arrested him at 1.15 p.m. in Burke Road, Camberwell, on a charge of being drunk and disorderly. The police accused him of using insulting language.

'You must have had a dull day. Didn't you have any old ladies to arrest? Hope you have a better evening, you pathetic bastards.'

The drinking went on. The alcoholic believes that the best way to solve one's miseries is to have more alcohol. Three days later the worst happened. Mrs Ena Bonds, wife of the licensee of the Royston Hotel in River Street, found him collapsed in a factory doorway off Richmond. He had been mugged. Two men followed him up the street into a laneway, beat him up and left him. He lost his watch and a gold fountain pen. He went for treatment at St Vincent's Hospital.

Yet Humphries' resilience and dedication to duty was amazing. He had agreed that night to open an exhibition of Balinese paintings at the gallery. He looked terrible with bruises on his face. He said, 'Actually I am bruised everywhere except on the knuckles ... The police are looking for a pair of boots that fit two dents in my stomach.'

Furthermore, he had written one of his poems for the occasion. Many there thought it one of his best, it was so rich in memories and nostalgia:

There are schooldays I remember nonetheless So far off and yet so near When we had to write the same old essay in full Three times every year.

What did you do in the holidays? The English master said Then sat back to smoke his pipe And watch the little bastards sweat.

What did we do, where did we go in 44 minutes? What Shangri-La could be reached? On those dismal family sorties? The Pontiac and Oldsmobile, The faithful pre-war Dodge or Studebaker, Were lucky if they took us further south Than Dava Lodge.

There were ranges called Strzelecki and Aberfeldy And the bay called Half Moon. There were rolled oats for our brekky And ping pong in the afternoon. Autumn evenings at Olinda toasting crumpets on the logs And in summer buckles resting On a thousand Jantzen togs.

We could weave these into essays, But we couldn't mention things Like the man at Mornington before Who watched us on the swings.

And dark-eyed boys excused from Chapel Were forced to rally Their imaginations to describe The Warburton Chalet.

It was a long poem. Cowes, Lorne and Stawell all rated a mention, including 'the Lucky Little Bastard', son of the scion of a Bourke Street store, who went to Sydney.

Barry had the skill to turn a disaster into a triumph. He handed round copies of his saga which the newspapers were only too happy to print.

Those drunk and disorderly charges from the episode on 15 June came up in the Camberwell Court on 7 September. The Reverend Douglas Tasker, director of the Christian Television Association, told the court he was the minister who married Humphries to his wife Rosalind ten years before. He said: 'He has a problem of his public image and his own personal character — his own character is different from his public image. He is a shy retiring person and he is easily hurt. He originates ideas, writes, directs and produces them. This makes him sensitive to public criticism. It also puts him under excessive strain. I don't know how he does it.'

Humphries went into a private hospital for three months. He joined Alcoholics Anonymous and became as seriously anti-drinking as a religious person is about God. Not to this day has he touched alcohol, not even wine. It is interesting that two of his characters, Sir Les Patterson and Barry McKenzie, literally gush with drink. In this way he has exorcised some of his agonies. In More Please he says that composing and singing his songs 'Never Trust a Man Who Doesn't Drink', 'Chardonnay' and 'Pissing in the Wind' has been one of the most satisfying and liberating of all his stage experiences. He wrote, 'Les Patterson is the part of me that never stopped drinking.'

'As for Bazza McKenzie he never stops imbibing "chilled ones", alias cans of Foster's.' In 1960, when Barry was in London, Peter Cook, the English comedian, suggested that he might like to write something for the satirical magazine Private Eye. Cook believed that a comic strip about a naive Australian, an innocent abroad, let loose in London might be amusing. He introduced him to a New Zealand cartoonist, Nicholas Garland, and Barry McKenzie was born: a big-chinned character wearing baggy trousers, collar and tie, oddly enough, and a big-brimmed hat rather larger than the one worn by John Howard when he makes his outback forays.

Bazza ran in Private Eye for more than ten years.

Bazza was indeed an innocent. He was always drinking too much on an empty stomach, resulting in the 'chunder'. Chunder was hardly a word in common currency, certainly not in Moonee Ponds. According to Barry it was more common in refined Melbourne public schools. Bazza had no success with the girls — just when he was about to 'sow his oats' it never happened.

Geoffrey Dutton in the Australian University lectures of 1978 said: 'The deepest irony has been in the case of Barry McKenzie. Bazza began as a strip cartoon in London's Private Eye, satirising the Englishman's idea of the Earl's Court kangaroo Valley dinkum Aussie. In no time he was simultaneously reviled by Australians for the old vice of giving a bad impression of Australia overseas, and welcomed by Australians back home as "good old Bazza, one of our mob".'

Bazza's language in the strip was colourful beyond belief. There has been some controversy as to whether the sharp ear of Humphries had identified some of these phrases or whether he made them up. One suspects a little of both. Whatever the truth they slipped straight into the language both in Australia and Britain. John Lahr in his Humphries biography claimed that Partridge's Dictionary of Slang cites Humphries fifteen times.

For chunder there are many terms, such as laughing at the ground, technicolour yawn, a liquid laugh or playing the whale.

It seemed a good idea to produce a book of Bazza cartoons. On the cover of The Wonderful World of Barry McKenzie there was a picture of Bazza with the message: 'They say that Pommy bastard Captain Cook discovered Australia — Pig's arse! If it wasn't for me Oz would not be on the flamin' map.' Back home in Australia it went on the banned list. Obviously it was far too corrupting for young Australian minds. However there was a marvellous twist to this. On the one hand the government banned the book, then on the other hand the Australian Film Development Corporation thought it was a cultural duty to sponsor it with a grant of $250,000.

In 1968 Barry's film director friend, Bruce Beresford, suggested that they should make a film out of the Bazza strip. Barry McKenzie's face was inspired by Chesty Bond, the big-chinned symbol of the Chesty Bond singlet. They needed someone just like him and found the actor Barry Crocker. So Crocker had the lead role in The Adventures of Barry McKenzie (1972) and there were further parts for Humphries himself. He was Edna Everage, Meyer de Lamphrey the mad psychiatrist, and also a Jesus freak rock singer.

Humphries almost chundered with enthusiasm when he described the making of the film. He said, 'We are filming it in winter because this is ideal for making London as picturesquely horrible as possible. Practically all our extras will be as ugly as possible. We've got a large RENT sign outside St Paul's. Yes, and we've designed a meter designed so that every time an Australian gets into a taxi the meter clocks over five pounds until it registers twenty pounds.'

As for the chunder scenes: 'We have made a chunder machine. There is a pipe which will go inside the side of Barry Crocker's trousers then around the camera. The technicolour yawn will look magnificent on a big screen.'

This was the chunder recipe, designed after the original Humphries formula: large quantities of sweetcorn, tinned tomatoes and Heinz mixed vegetable salad.

The Bazza film took a caning from the critics and was loved by filmgoers. Its profit after costs was $570,000 and it ranked sixth on the list of twenty top-grossing Australian films between 1972 and 1977. The sequel Barry McKenzie Holds His Own was only slightly less successful. In this film Aunty Edna was mistaken for the Queen, kidnapped and flown off to Transylvania where Count Dracula lusted after her soft neck.

In the foreword to the comic strip book Bazza Comes into His Own (1979) Humphries wrote: 'I believe Barry McKenzie Holds His Own to be the only serious motion picture in recent times in which a Socialist Prime Minister (The Rt Hon Gough Whitlam) plays a starring climactic role. His appearance in the last reel is like a mighty chord concluding a work of symphonic music ...'

Actually this was a coup. Special guest artists were Gough and Margaret Whitlam. In the closing sequence of the movie Edna arrives back in Sydney and the Whitlams are there waiting for her. The Prime Minister says: 'I dub thee Dame Edna Everage. Arise Dame Edna.'

Anyone who thought she was Dame Edna just for the movie had to think again. She was Dame Edna forever. Barry pointed out she was a changed woman. 'She used to watch Bellbird on the telly, but she loves Power Without Glory — that's the Blue Hills of the 1970s. She used to be in large smocks, now she's into denim. She gets her denim in South Yarra and you wouldn't believe the number of people in London who want to buy Edna's clothes. I could open a Dame Edna shop in London.'

What next for Dame Edna? She should become a Baroness like Maggie Thatcher.
