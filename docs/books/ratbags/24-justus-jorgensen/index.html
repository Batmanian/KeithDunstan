Justus Jorgensen

20 min read.

First published 1980

IT IS NOT MANY YEARS since Justus Jorgensen died, but nothing much has changed at Montsalvat. His vast overpowering personality still seems to seep out of every mud brick and to lurk behind every bluestone block. Four thousand of his paintings are still there and at every turn one seems to be confronted by yet another self portrait. There he is with his white Van Dyck beard, no smiles, no gentleness, but the paintings always seem to catch the look of the visionary, the mystic.

Jorgensen, aged 82, died on the 15th May 1975 and there was even art involved in that too. While he was still at the funeral parlor art students made death masks of his face and hands. Jorgy's original dream was to be buried in his own chapel, but there was a fuss about that. You cannot inter your remains where you like in this world. However there is a cemetery next door and by special arrangement he managed to get a burial plot right at the back of the chapel.

So in this Eltham cemetery there are six mounds, nothing else, not even a marker to advise who is underneath. The six are Jorgensen, his wife Lily, Arthur Munday, lawyer; Eric Pedersen, carpenter; Mervyn Skipper, journalist and his wife Lena. Jorgensen's son Sebastian can tell you which grave is which, but few others can.

'But that's the point, don't you see?' he says. 'They don't need headstones. All Montsalvat is their headstone.'

As headstones go it is unbelievable. Some people call it Disneyland on the Yarra, some call it Jorgy's Camelot, but neither of them fit. Jorgensen looked upon it as a sculpture, and so it is, a marvellous thing, Victoria's one genuine architectural folly, and it has been building there on and off since Jorgensen first went there in 1935.

Hillcrest Avenue, Eltham was the address — and Jorgensen set himself up like a medieval grand seigneur. He wanted to create art in the ancient style away from the horrors of commercialism, untrammelled by petty suburban standards. Jorgy saw himself like the leader of an orchestra drawing out the talents of all those around him. Although, of course, if he was an orchestral leader then he was all-powerful. He wanted no challenge to his authority. He was a Thomas Beecham.

He had a hypnotic effect on people. His disciples — it is hard to find another term — were prepared to go to Montsalvat, give their lives for him, male and female, and labor unpaid. Many of them did more than that. They turned over their money to Jorgensen and paid thousands of dollars for the privilege of being there.

At one of his grand dinners Jorgensen said 'I'm in love with the Middle Ages. I like to think of anonymous builders, they were not afraid to die, of growing old. They knew that they would go on living forever in the things they had created — the great cathedrals, the lovely buildings.'

And of his disciples he added 'These are my people. I must take them with all their little stupidities, their little this and that, and make them into what I want them to be. They can do all sorts of stupid things, but I can't get on without them. So, if Lady Diana Manners offered me Haddon Hall and said I must desert them, I'd tell her to stuff it up her jacksie.'

Among the disciples were people like Helen Lempriere, Sue Vanderkelen, the lawyer Arthur Munday who came for a week-end and stayed 45 years. He dropped the law so quickly he didn't even go back to his office to pick up his things. Then above all there was Mervyn Skipper, Melbourne correspondent for the Bulletin and father of Sonia, Helen and Matcham.

I first became interested in Monsalvat in the early sixties. Melbourne newspapers rarely if ever mentioned the place and it certainly never made the art columns. It was a custom in those days to place an unofficial 'black ban' on anything that was too distasteful.

Stern newspaper proprietors felt it would be much better for the health of their respectable readers if the Jorgensen name were never mentioned. Who knew what went on at Montsalvat. There were stories that Jorgy ran his own private harem, that free love and free thought were rife. All the time they were painting each other in the nude and it was well known that it was the home of a nudist colony. There were people there with long hair and beards, they didn't get up early in the morning — oh, it was ugly.

Betty Roland has written about this. She has lived at Montsalvat, she has spent years going through the hundred volumes of typescript and letters left behind by Mervyn Skipper. In her book Jorgensen of Montsalvat she wrote: 'There were tales of obscene rites, of rape and drunken orgies, trafficking in drugs, indeed there seemed to be no limit to the vices that were attributed to them nor to the imagination of the right-thinking citizens who professed such revulsion of the practices indulged in there.

'This aroused interest to fever pitch. Strange faces peered through the fence, the more adventurous entering the grounds and stared through the windows, only to be disappointed at what they failed to see. This was not without its humorous side — on one occasion when a party of Boy Scouts was camped in the Eltham park and heard loud cries coming from the hill above they were convinced that murder was afoot and came charging through the undergrowth only to discover that a lady with a Wagnerian voice was standing on the terrace singing Brunhilde's battle cry, full throat. The delighted Jorgensen invited them to come inside and drink a glass of wine, an invitation that the confused Scout Master thought it wisest to decline.'

It was true, Jorgensen didn't see why he should live according to Moonee Ponds standards, but the harem, nudist colony business was no more than fruity gossip and the things that took place at Montsalvat wouldn't even lift an eyebrow in the nineteen seventies.

In 1963 rates were soaring and Montsalvat was heading for financial ruin. They had been keeping afloat by selling off blocks of land. The Duke and Duchess of Bedford had been visiting Australia and Helen Skipper had the idea why not throw the place open to the public in the manner of the English stately home.

So Montsalvat went commercial, two shillings admission and the Great Hall was open for weddings and parties. It was possible to go Tom Jones style with ox-on-the-spit served by medieval-costumed waiters, or if one preferred the full bit, there was dancing by candlelight with white tie and tails. It became a favorite too with the advertising agencies. The stately walls made distinguished backgrounds, and were particularly ideal for making mass-produced cars look more expensive.

Some of the old hands were shocked at all this, but Montsalvat had to survive. It was at this time that I went out to see the great man. When I arrived he was busy painting Michelangelo's Pieta and Beethoven's Fourth Symphony was crashing out from an amplifier. Jorgensen told me that he was born on the 12th of May, 1893, the son of a Norwegian sea captain.

There's no doubt that Jorgensen inherited his obsessive nature from his father. Old Simon Jacob Jorgensen had the same style, a determination to follow an idea even if the roof fell in. Loss of life at sea in the nineteenth century was hardly as common as loss of life on bitumen in the 20th but it was bad enough. Simon Jorgensen invented an unsinkable lifeboat.

He thought that he would only have to take it to the Marine Board in London and he would be praised as a benefactor of mankind. However he was treated as a nuisance and a crank and asked to go away. So Jorgensen gave up everything for his lifeboat, his career, his command. He built a prototype called The Storm King and just to prove his point, at the worst possible time of the year, he sailed it to Australia, embarking on 12 September 1889.

He had with him just one companion and it took him eleven months to reach Melbourne. The conditions were appalling and his worst experience was being totally submerged by a mountain of green water in the Great Australian Bight. That should have been the end but the extraordinary little craft, righted itself and rose to the surface like a cork.

Later he reported this to the authorities and they did not believe him. Yet he was lucky, a passing ship had seen the incident and reported it in the log book.

Jorgensen received a hero's welcome in Melbourne and he had no doubt now that when he returned to London the Storm King would become the standard model for life boats the world over. But no, the Marine's Board attitude was precisely as before. The Board members didn't believe his boat was unsinkable and took no action. He was ruined. So dispirited he returned to Melbourne — to shore work and eventually finished his days as Harbourmaster at Geraldton in Western Australia.

Jorgensen junior was even more tenacious. He was contemptuous of all those who sought mere fame and money. Certainly Justus earned little during his life. He married Lily Smith, an art student from Brisbane. It was his good fortune that Lily also was a medical student, became a doctor and supported him through their early married years.

His credo was this: 'The artist is the priest of the Muses. He is self-ordained and when he realizes the awesome nature of his choice, he sees his entire life unfold before him in terms of her service and vows that nothing will divert him from her.

'He knows that he must eat and drink and obey the demands of biology but that he must never allow these demands to interfere with his dedication to the Muse. At all costs he must direct his life so that she is paramount.'

Jorgensen first studied under his Uncle Charles Schreiber and from him he learned to be an architect. Then while studying at the National Gallery art school he became infatuated by Max Meldrum. He went to Meldrum and pleaded with him to become one of his students.

Meldrum was brilliant vain, vitriolic and he loved to mock famous reputations of other artists. There was a group of artists known as the Meldrum School and he was a great force in the art world of the nineteen twenties. Jorgensen became his favorite pupil and a teacher in the Meldrum school.

Jorgensen married Lily and went to Paris in 1924. Things were never quite the same again. Meldrum did not like his pupils to leave him.

By the nineteen thirties they had broken completely. Meldrum did not approve of Jorgensen's free and easy moral life and he accused him of stealing his pupils. Meldrum returned from Paris himself in 1931 and he expected Jorgensen to have all his students assembled for him as of old. But Jorgensen had his own students now and they had never learned from Max.

There are bitter letters between the two men. Jorgensen replies always refer to his master respectfully as 'Mr Meldrum'. In one of the letters he reminds Meldrum that in the presence of his friends the old man called him a scoundrel, a dolt, a megalomaniac and said that his studio was a whorehouse.

Betty Roland says that Jorgensen was very distressed over the split but never did he say anything derogatory about Meldrum and when I called at Montsalvat in 1963 he still said that his old master had been the greatest influence in his life.

But Schrieber must have been an influence almost as great. Jorgensen was a compulsive builder. He couldn't stop. Right back from the turn of the century Eltham was a home for artists and his wife Lily bought him land in Hillcrest Avenue in 1935. At first they lived there in a caravan, but within six years the Great Hall was up and the results were spectacular.

One should explain that the main building at Montsalvat is three storey and it looks like a 16th century French manor house, splendidly Gothic. Then is is surrounded by other buildings which are antique brick, mud brick, wattle and daub and they have a slightly off-centre weather-beaten look of immense antiquity as if you were wandering around Tudor England.

There are little bluestone Norman towers, there is a swimming pool which looks decidedly Roman, there are monk like cells beside the pool and the initials of those who used to occupy them are still there in mosaic tile — Helen Lempriere, John Busst, Matcham Skipper and others.

Jorgy took me on a grand tour. He pointed out the magnificent hammer beams, the gargoyles, fireplaces big enough to roast an elephant. One of his friends was Jim Whelan, better known as Whelan the Wrecker. Jim, according to Jorgy, was a rare creature, a wrecker with a soul. He adored the buildings and hated pulling them down. He used to say that his dream was to get his hands on to something big, glassy and shiny like the ICI. 'I could whip that down in no time at all.'

Jorgensen said he kept a close eye on all Jim's latest destructions. So he had a balcony that came from the Royal Insurance building in Collins Street, a window that came from the old Melbourne Jail. The great beams in the gallery came from a city bond store, and the flagstones on the floor of the Great Hall came from all over. Some came from a brothel in Little Collins Street.

There was one important question put to Jorgensen. Here was a man who saw himself as heir to the crown of Rembrandt. As well as an urge to build he had an insatiable urge to paint, yet his last exhibition of paintings was when he returned from Europe in 1929, none since. Why?

He said he wouldn't put up his paintings to be criticised. Critics should be great artists themselves before they attempted to criticise others. The critics here were ignorant fools. Furthermore he had nothing but contempt for the whole business of selling art for money. Why should he indulge in it? Did the great artists of the past do this? Certainly not. Australians didn't know anything about art, didn't like art' therefore he would not show his art to a world which did not like it.

Yet as a special concession he took me upstairs to his gallery above the Great Hall, and it was an extraordinary sight. There were nudes, there were still lifes, but what really astonished, self portrait, after self portrait, after self portrait, Jorgy, Jorgy, Jorgy.

Megalomania? A little perhaps, but there was another reason. He painted every day and he always needed a model. He was the cheapest around — and by far the most reliable.

We went to the Great Hall and looked at the massive baronial table. Jorgensen had his famous dinners here, often on Sunday night. They would be attended by academics, by professional people, by writers and artists. It was here that Jorgensen would have his discussions. 'Dialogues', they were called. He would introduce a vast moral theme and there disciples male and female would thrash it out all night. Jorgensen liked to think of himself as a great philosopher. He even said: 'I would rather be thought of as a philosopher who paints rather than as a painter who philosophises.'

The discussion one night was 'Helen's Babies'. What would happen if Mrs Grundy came to Eltham. This was a delicate subject. Jorgensen had three children. There was Max, the son of his wife, Lily, named in honor of Max Meldrum. There was Sebastian named after Bach and Sigmund, named after Sigmund Freud, both the children of Helen Skipper.

A fascinating point that came up in the discussion was the attitude of the doctors to Helen Skipper. When she had Sebastian they were relatively kindly, but when she returned two years later to have Sigmund, they were rude and unpleasant. According to the dialogue 'a girl who has one illegitimate baby is only a poor Fallen Woman, but a girl who has two looks as if she is trying to overturn the State.'

Through all this Jorgensen brilliantly managed to keep his personal household together. He had an ingenious argument. If he went ahead and acted the way his critics expected him to act, that is, if he didn't follow his natural impulses and take a lover, then he would resent the thing which compelled him to adopt this distasteful role. In other words: his wife. Then would it be considerate on his part to allow Lil to become the object of his dislike? Would she have chosen that? Certainly not.

Mervyn Skipper recorded all these discussions on paper and he would produce his report at the next dinner and so it would be discussed all over again. He could be brutal at times and seek out the weaknesses of his followers. So there were arguments, sometimes bitter, the colony came apart at the seams. There were those who became tired of having Jorgy as a feudal headmaster and they moved elsewhere. Only the most intensely loyal such as Mervyn Skipper, Helen, Arthur Munday and Lesley Sinclair remained. Jorgensen was able to say: 'Even Jesus had only twelve disciples.'

Yet when I returned to Montsalvat in 1978 it still had its coterie of artists, poets, musicians and writers. Matcham Skipper, sculptor, silversmith, was there in a studio farm house with his wife Myra and artist sons. Betty Roland, author, playwright, was in another house. Sonia Skipper had finished carving a marvellous corbel. That will go in the chapel.

Helen Skipper was at Belgrave caring for Justus Jorgensen's aged brother Dr Elef Jorgensen, but her two sons Sebastian and Sigmund were there.

The brothers make a fascinating contrast. Sebastian is lean, and not only a good guitarist but very nearly a great one. He's a journalist, an ex-editor of Oz, an all stops out idealist and inclined to be volatile. There was the famous occasion when Sigmund, after much manoevreing managed to get Prime Minister McMahon to attend a dinner at Montsalvat to celebrate the Eltham Centenary. Sigmund was fighting for a Government grant. Sebastian had returned from London only the night before and still a little shocked over commercial activities at Montsalvat. He made a grand entry in the middle of the dinner, struck a chord on the piano, then denounced the Government's policy in Vietnam, thus ruining any hope of a subsidy. Police grabbed him by the neck and hurried him off stage. Immediately afterwards Sebastian challenged the detective sergeant to a duel.

Sigmund is rotund, heavily bearded and describes himself as the quiet one, the pragmatist, non artist. Actually he is a very good carpenter and he is the co-owner of Clichy's in Carlton which would have to be one of the finest restaurants in Melbourne.

Sebastian took me on a grand tour. The big change from 1963 was the chapel, still far from finished. This too was classic Gothic. The bluestone came from a demolished Methodist Church in Carlton. Sig Jorgensen and Matcham Skipper have made some noble front doors, solid enough for a Benedictine Monastery, the timber came from a South Melbourne wharf.

Jorgensen was born a Catholic but became an atheist. Yet he never got religion out of his system. According to Matcham Skipper belief in God was still there, otherwise, why did he build Montsalvat. One time he even did a painting with himself on the Cross, being crucified like Christ. He asked the Roman Catholic Archbishop of Melbourne, Dr Mannix, if he would bless the chapel for him. His Grace replied: 'But you are not a religious man.'

Jorgensen said: 'But then how many religious men today build a chapel?'

The work continues on that chapel but progress is slow. Matcham Skipper begs the world not to be alarmed. After all it did take around 1000 years to complete Ely Cathedral.

Then there was another building new since 1963. It was on the other side of the swimming pool opposite the artists' cells. This was in mud, brick and stone and like all Jorgy's creations, massive, impermeable. It had no roof, no floor and its windows were open to the breezes. Sebastian thought that it should be left as it is, a Jorgensen sculpture. 'After all,' he said, 'if Rodin died in the middle of a sculpture you wouldn't try to finish it for him.'

Sigmund though has other ideas. He wants to turn it into the Montsalvat restaurant. Part of it would be a gallery for Jorgensen's paintings and the rest would be restaurant. 'We can finish it just the way he wanted,' said Sig, 'his plans are all here.'

Then we went on to another building which I didn't visit in 1963. It was the barn, an enormous, rambling old affair and you would swear it had been there since the days of Cromwell. On the front were two enormous cathedral-like doors that came from the burnt out shell of Melbourne University's Wilson Hall.

But the big surprise was inside. Here was a ship and like everything else of Jorgensen's, large. Sebastian said it was 56 feet long, an old ammunition carrier. My father had dreams one time of using it as a studio on the Yarra. Work was being done on it until recently, putting in cabins. Oh, you could sail round the world in it. And there's a magnificent diesel engine over there, just waiting to be put in. Only trouble is, take that boat out of here and the whole barn would collapse.

Phillip Adams described the boat as Jorgy's Ark and he said that he labored on it like some latter day Noah. He suggested too that Jorgy intended to live his final day within that ancient boat and maybe it was even his funeral barge.

That didn't take place, but Sebastian said that in the final years, when Jorgy was showing a preference for religious paintings, he did spend much of his time in the old barn. There was another room and Sebastian said that this was Jorgy's last studio. It had the predictable ox-roasting fireplace. 'You couldn't imagine him with a small fireplace could you?' he said.

'And look at this.' He took me over to the strangest bath and shower I have seen. 'It didn't matter if it wasn't completely functional. He just loved the look of things.'

It certainly had the looks. It was a bath composed entirely of bluestone blocks. It could have been built for washing down Sumo wrestlers and over the top there was a standard metal rose shower.

I went back to see Sigmund in his office by the Great Hall. There is yet another change from the old days. Back in the nineteen thirties there was only one lavatory. As Betty Roland put it, Jorgensen had a fine disregard for such trifles. Now there were splendid toilet blocks put in by order of the Health Department. But these were no ordinary lavatory blocks — they were solid bluestone, modern-medieval type, Gothic archways and all.

The first question to put to Sig was how did he keep it going. 'It's awful,' he said. 'I have a terrible job. I need to raise $400 a day just to keep it going. We have banquets once a week and we do our own catering. We have concerts in the Great Hall once a month, Chamber Music, George Dreyfus, people like that. We have a poets' festival next month... 700 people came last year.

'But it's still a battle. Everything I make out of Clichy's goes in here. If only I could convince someone in Canberra that it is important to the nation that it be saved.

'The official view tends to be that it is a private piece of madness we have created ourselves. If we are running it as a stately home they think we should have it open all the time. But we don't have the staff. I rely on a few dedicated people who work for s.f.a. But I guess we'll manage somehow.'

If Eltham means anything as an art colony then Montsalvat will have to remain as its centre its shrine. Maybe the best tribute to Jorgensen was paid by Phillip Adams that day the death masks were made:

'It might seem that Australia was the last country on Earth where you'd expect to find a Jorgensen. But I've a contrary theory that goes something like this: because of our country's crassness, because of our lack of history of European richness, because of the boredom produced by our remoteness and for many years, our oppressive censorship, social tensions were created that produced a number of extraordinary figures.

'Just as diamonds are born from common coal, the pressure of Australia's boredom created such improbable figures as Patrick White, Barry Humphries and yes, Justus Jorgensen... Their rather patrician heads are, if you like, the other side of our kangaroo'd coins.

'It's too easy to dismiss Jorgie's attempt at Camelot as an absurdity, a folly and a failure. As someone who prefers a magnificent obsession to dullness, who would rather visit a folly than a factory any day, and who believes than man advances as much by his noble failures as he does by his smug successes, I'd like to pay tribute to Jorgensen. And I hope that the memory of this magnificent ratbag lives on in Montsalvat, not so much as an inspiration to others but as a mystery, as a nagging doubt as a constant questioning of glib assumptions.'

But what of the mystery why he called the place Montsalvat? He told me: 'Wagner, of course — it used to be a joke. I would say "I am the son of Parsifal and I come from Montsalvat, home of the Knights of the Grail."'

Yes, Jorgy was Lohengrin, the Silver Knight and everything was there but the swan.
