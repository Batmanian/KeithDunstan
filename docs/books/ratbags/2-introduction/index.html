Introduction

20 min read.

First published 1980

Keith Dunstan explores Australian conformity and defines the eccentric 'ratbag', profiling characters such as Alan Russell, Russell Cole, Phillip Adams, Patrick White, Rosaleen Norton and Andrea.

The truth is I have always had a soft spot for Ratbags. A ratbag is anyone who dares to be different; a ratbag is the creature who creates a pinnacle, perhaps only tiny above the great drear of conformity. Most of us see a million eyes focussed upon us, waiting for us to make one move out of the norm. So we sit there figuratively sucking our thumbs and conform.

And the whole system has been designed to impress upon us the importance of conformity. There was the school uniform, the mass education system with every child chanting the same mathematics tables, learning about the same English kings and sitting by the thousand in the same public buildings, puzzling over the same matriculation papers, answering the same questions about The Mayor of Casteridge or School for Scandal or Newton's Third Law of Motion.

Conformity in the nineteen twenties was a stiff collar, black boots and blue serge suit, and it is always fascinating to look at old pictures of our big sporting arenas. There you see the utter conformity of the so-called dashing Australian, not a head without a great felt hat, hardly a neck without a tie and, ah yes, all those blue serge suits.

Conformity in the nineteen seventies was different, but then it had its own special style, the youth were almost desperate in their desire to conform. Denim, the faded denim, was the most conforming fabric since the days of the blue serge. Every parent knows it was a rare teenager indeed who was prepared to break the bondage of the tattered denim, the long lank hair, the gym boots and a perfect in-depth knowledge of rock'n'roll.

The businessman is no different. The propagation of ideas is a dangerous thing. Ideas are something that come from overseas, and anything from that mythical country 'overseas' is better. The businessman hunts for his ideas on a 'fact-finding-tour'. Our prime ministers, our premiers, even our humble backbenchers go on fact-finding tours almost every year. The FFT is as Australian as the meat pie, and besides, it is a tax deduction.

The very Continent conforms — a Hans Heysen could paint almost precisely the same gum tree on the Cape York Peninsula in the far north or on the Yorke Peninsula in the far south. The language conforms, the Australian has the same all-embracing nasal twang. I have heard that there are air hostesses who can listen to their passengers and nominate immediately the Queenslanders, the West Australians, the Victorians, Tasmanians or whoever, but one suspects it is the words they use rather than the accent. Inevitably the Victorians would be talking about football.

The conformity goes right through to the astonishing similarity of our country towns; the same Flag Motel on the perimeter, the same town hall with its clock and would-be Italianate architecture, but the whole thing was stolen from mid-Victorian Manchester. There is the same mass-produced World War I memorial, the white uniformed soldier with his rifle turned upside down. There are the Four Square stores, the Colonel Sanders chicken houses, the McDonalds, the swimming pool, the drive-in theatres and the signs which announce that Lions, Apex, Jaycees and the Rotary Club meets on Tuesdays at the Commercial Hotel.

But the Ratbag somehow, and sometimes at awful cost, manages to escape all this conformity. It is a lovely word, succinct, clear to the point and as Australian as dinkum, chunder or come the raw prawn. Is he an eccentric? Well...yes. One time in the Bulletin I described Percy Grainger as an eccentric and there was a reply from Ellis Glover of Waterton, NSW: 'Obviously Grainger was a man of means. Wealth is the benchmark for the appelation "an eccentric". In this country if an eccentric has no money he is "a ratbag".'

Maybe that is true but Ratbag sounds better. The origins of the word are obscure. The obvious assumption is that it has something to do with the catching of rats, and any man who indulges in such an occupation would have to be considered odd. That great authority on the Australian Language Sidney J. Baker said: 'We have numerous utility expressions for people such as king dick for a boss or leader; pannikin or panno, for a foreman; sparrow, squib, nugget and streak, for men of varying sizes; fang carpenter, gum digger and gum puncher for a dentist; greasy for a butcher; rabbit snatcher, a mid-wife; bottle-oh and bottle shepherd for a bottle collector; bottle-oh's rouseabout, a nobody... none of them has quite the stature of the ratbag, for in some ways he is as much a distinguishable figure in Australia as the wowser. Unlike Britain, Australia has no great enthusiasm for eccentrics. Hence the use of ratbag for eccentric carries a certain disapproval and ratbaggery some suggestion that such conduct is un-Australian, or at any rate not quite acceptable in this environment.'

So there is ratbagging, ratbaggery and there are ratbaggers. According to Baker the term has been in use since the nineteen-thirties. And as Baker says, it is true. We do not like ratbags. We don't want anyone to rise above the norm. We have little love for the superior in excellence in any form.

That is why we detest authority, someone is being put in charge, someone is being made superior and different. That is why Ockerdom and the whole cult of the Ocker is so engaging. The Ocker with his beer-gut, his genial jokes, his devotion to ball games and taking it easy, calls for minimal mental stimulation, and he fits easily into a design — he is the beloved Australian archetype of the late twentieth century.

So who are the ratbags? A great many are considered ratbags merely by occupation. You can take it for granted that virtually all politicians are ratbags. Naturally some have a ratbag virtuosity not equalled by others. One thinks of Sir Henry Bolte, when at the height of his eloquence he was capable of such lines as 'The SEC strikers are a bunch of brigands. They can march up and down until they are bloody well footsore; it's nothing to do with me.' There is Jim Cairns, Joh Bjelke-Petersen, Don Dunstan, Jim Killen, John Gorton, Tom Uren, Clyde Cameron...where does one stop?

In the eyes of the public all artists and writers are ratbags. There is the artist Ivan Durrant who in 1975 slaughtered a cow and dumped it outside Melbourne's National Gallery and fooled the public by crafting a hand of synthetic material then stuffing the wrist end with meat. Pro Hart on the other hand launched a book in 1977 by bombing canvas with paint from a low flying plane and did an ice sculpture at the Adelaide Festival. His sculpture contained 1000 blocks of ice, was 11 metres high and had flowers on top. It had the advantage over some other sculptures, it was guaranteed to disappear in 10 days.

Patrick White was always under suspicion of being a ratbag. Like all great writers he suffered merciless treatment from the critics. One critic described his Tree of Man as illiterate verbal sludge. He had to win the Nobel Prize before he was accepted in his own country. He had his own back when he received a plaque for being Australian of the Year. He said he would like to cut it up three ways and give pieces to Barry Humphries, Professor Manning Clark and to Jack Mundey, the Communist activist turned ecologist.

Yes, intellectuals are a danger. In 1974 the Australian Labor Party received huge backing from artists, writers, academics. They took full page advertisements. For example on May 9 there was an ad in the Australian which said '404 Australian academics want Labor to have a chance because...' In the eyes of the Ocker the academic has to be a ratbag and there was little enthusiasm for such advertisements in the elections of 1977. One gets the picture by following the more modern Australian plays. Alexander Buzo had quite a success with Norm and Ahmed. It reaches its climax when an RSL club Ocker beats up a Pakistani student for talking clever.

But Ratbags don't have to be politicians, writers or intellectuals, anyone who moves even slightly out of the public straightjacket is a ratbag.

A Ratbag can be a lonely creature of the city, a mystery oddball such as those which fascinate Phillip Adams. There is one who travels Melbourne's Yarra Bank daily between 8.30 a.m. and 10. He wears a baggy brown suit; he is always two days without a shave, his hair is unkempt; he has a slightly haunted appearance yet a look of immense determination. Always he has a bundle of newspapers under his arm and always he is in a great hurry.

What does he do with these newspapers. Does he read them. Is it always the same bundle? At first I thought they were for salvage and he was carrying them from the city to some depot. That was until I discovered that if I travelled the Yarra Bicycle Path an hour earlier, then the man was carrying his bundle the other way. The newspapers were just going back and forth. Why? It took two years of daily encounters just to get a flicker of recognition. Then I thought it would not be difficult to stop and ask him why he was carrying these newspapers, but then if I found that out the magic of the thing would disappear.

Phillip Adams would see him on a bridge over the Southeastern Freeway, newspapers tucked underneath his arm. He would be there just staring and his position was exactly above the 'I' in the Dickies Towels advertisement on the side of the bridge. As Adams drove underneath he always waved to the little man. 'I never got a wave back,' he said, 'but it was always a great comfort to see him there. Then he disappeared for a whole year, but suddenly one morning he was back. I was overjoyed. The only difference was the Dickies towel sign was gone but he was standing there where the 'I' used to be.'

Another marvellous Ratbag was Joshua, the old Jewish violinist. He had a big hook nose, long white hair and he could have been a Biblical prophet just like his namesake. It was fascinating to see him move; he went at such a pace. He didn't exactly run, it was more of a lope, a cross between a walk and a run.

He was always taking notes. You would see him standing in doorways pencilling furiously, and then he would whizz down Collins Street, twist between the cars and he would be away. Legend has it that he was a shellshock victim from World War I. Occasionally he would stop to chat on some crazy subject, but you couldn't hold him for 10 seconds before he was off again. He added charm to the city — he had an extraordinary fey quality.

Sydney has Joe the cartpusher. Spotters see him trundling his pushcart around the city parks, Hyde Park, Liverpool Street, Macquarie Street. He wears a sort of a yellow parka, shapeless grey trousers, a polo neck jumper and a white construction helmet. According to Nation Review one time a mocking builder's laborer tossed it to him and he has been wearing it ever since.

It is the cart that gives him class and quality as a ratbag. He made it himself from bits and pieces, and it runs on motor bike wheels. It has a handle out front and he pushes it like a supermarket trolley.

For years there has been speculation — what does Joe carry in his cart? There was the suggestion even that he carries the ashes of his dead son, but the truth is more mundane. He carries clothes and spare parts for his pushcart.

Perhaps my favorite ratbag is Clive Bush. He lives in Coplelen Street, South Yarra and he is one of the last men in the country with a true sense of indignation. He believes that if only he writes sufficient letters to the Age, the Sun and the Herald then the Prime Minister, errant union leaders and others will understand the perfidy of their ways and the world will be a better place.

He goes further than that. He bombards the nation's leaders with telegrams. At his peak during the nineteen sixties he was benefitting the Postmaster General's Department by more than £500 a year. In order to get the night letter rate he would lodge his telegrams at midnight and off they would go to the Prime Minister, the Leader of the Opposition, the State Premiers, and anyone he thought would listen. Yet sometimes he does have his triumphs. He was a better letter writer than St Paul when the time came to save Melbourne's Regent Theatre.

Then there are those with offbeat interests. Russell Cole is a retired bank manager and a compulsive stair climber. Back in 1959 when the ICI building was the tallest in Melbourne, he climbed all the stairs to the top. It was his 69th birthday.

From then on he celebrated every birthday by climbing the stairs of a lofty building. He went up the National Mutual, the Royal Insurance, the CRA. In 1972 on his 82nd birthday he climbed the AMP tower, an ABC team filmed him in action and viewers that evening saw him on This Day Tonight.

He had wanted to run up the stairs of the BHP building on the opposite corner, but the BHP administrators were far from enthusiastic. What would they do if Mr Cole had a heart attack? So the answer was 'No'.

TDT did not know after that climbing the AMP building in the morning Russell Cole did a sly climb of the BHP tower in the afternoon. His style was beautiful to watch, slow, even, but relentless without a pause. He went from the bottom stair to the 42nd level in 14 minutes with no dramatic increase in the pulse rate.

Indeed he claimed that stair climbing was better than wasting money on doctors. His favorite line was: 'Stair climbing is the best possible test for the heart. If you get to the top and it's still working then you know it must be in good shape.' In 1978 at the age of 87 he was still climbing stairs.

The true eccentric is so absorbed with his own interests he is not concerned whether other people find him odd. You could almost describe Alan Russell as unique. I first came across him in 1958. He was the most successful dentist I had ever seen. His practice was in Windsor, Brisbane. He had three chairs, two dental assistants, three nurses and two dental mechanics. He had a passion for electrical gadgets. As the patient walked towards the surgery, as if by magic a nurse would suddenly appear at the door, ready to show him in.

The secret was an electric eye out in the garden. When the patient broke the beam it gave the signal inside that he was on the way. Alan Russell kept those chairs going morning, afternoon and night. He made a fortune.

My next contact with him was in 1965. He had sent around excerpts of some of the more volatile sections of Grace Metallious' book No Adam in Eden. I wasn't specially privileged. He also sent the same excerpts to 348 other newspaper people around the Continent.

This was the week that the Government freed from bondage Lady Chatterley's Lover, Borstal Boy, Lolita and Confessions of a Spent Youth. Alan Russell sent telegrams to every member of Parliament. The telegrams were 228 words each and they began:

YOUR GOVERNMENT'S ASTOUNDING RELEASE OF FOUR SEX-SOAKED BOOKS CONSTITUTES MAJOR CONTRIBUTION TO POTENTIAL CAUSES OF DELINQUENCY BOTH JUVENILE AND ADULT STOP CUSTOMS MINISTER WAS APPARENTLY GUIDED BY FOUR UNIVERSITY INTELLECTUALS INCLUDING THREE PROFESSORS WHOSE CAPACITY TO MAKE RIGHT DECISIONS ON MORAL ISSUES HAS NEVER BEEN ESTABLISHED.

His crusade to purify the nation began back in 1962. He took his sons to see I Am a Camera at a Brisbane Theatre and was terribly shocked. His sons found it so unpleasant they walked out.

The next day he went back with a tape recorder. He recorded large excerpts and made a complaint to the Police. Next he went to work on Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf. He sat in the stall with a Pentax camera fitted with a 200 mm telephoto lens, took pictures of the love scenes and counted the blasphemies. His score came to 95 blasphemies.

He admitted that people in the theatre thought he was some kind of a perv... 'But I kept telling myself: "They're the cheeky ones for putting on this sort of play. If they've got the hide to do this, then I've got the hide to photograph it".'

He fought against the Mavis Bramston Show, he campaigned to have the African Dancers cover their breasts and he raged against the 'immorality' of TV shows like 'The Box'. Yet particularly he made a name for himself by sending telegrams to parliamentarians. He was more diligent even than Clive Bush. His magnum opus came in 1972 — 600 words and it went out to the Governor-General and 200 Federal members of parliament. The cost was $505. It was so long it looked like a roll of lavatory paper. His protest was against the Customs Minister, Mr Chipp and the release of the sex education Little Red School book. The telegram began:

CHIPPS PERFORMANCE IN CENSORSHIP IS BLACKEST CHAPTER IN AUSTRALIA'S HISTORY PERIOD HE HAS DONE MORE HARM TO AUSTRALIAN SOCIETY AND WILL WRECK MORE LIVES STILL DEVELOPING THAN OTHER DOZEN MEN PERIOD BY PERMITTING OUR PRE-TEENAGE CHILDREN TO BE SITTING SHOTS FOR AVARICIOUS UNPRINCIPLED PUBLISHERS OF SEX FILTH HE HAS ACTED UNFORGIVEABLY AND HAS SUNK TO LOWEST DEPTHS IN PUBLIC LIFE...

Alan Russell said that like Clive Bush he always telephoned his telegrams late to get the night rate, and the operators knew him very well indeed. On this occasion he rang through at 1 a.m. but the operator put him off, and said he should call back. Being crafty, Russell was put off to the next shift. It was a very long job. He started dictating at 2.30 a.m. and finished at 4.30 a.m.

Alan Russell left his big Brisbane practice in 1967 and moved to Maroochydore where he now lives in a classic weatherboard Queensland house on stilts and there he carries on an awe-inspiring range of activities, including dentistry. The house being on stilts is an advantage for down below he has set up his own modern printery which he uses to further his campaigns against the permissive society.

Oddly enough it is much harder to find the brilliant female eccentric. Perhaps the female mind is less imaginative, less creative, and more safely down to earth. More likely since the birth of the nation the Australian woman has been such a captive she has never had a chance. The male very early created an insidious apartheid where the female was expected, except for show purposes to stay out of sight and stick to child bearing. She has found it almost impossible to get into politics, or to take any serious executive role in the community. Ratbags for full eccentric flowering need publicity and applause — without they wither.

Yet some women have dared to be different. At the turn of the century Adelaide had Thistle Anderson, married name Mrs Herbert Fisher. She was a musical comedy actress, a poet, writer, playwright and all round extrovert with passions for riding, shooting, hunting, fishing and even motoring. In 1905 she caused a sensation when she produced a pamphlet titled Arcadian Adelaide. She pointed out that Adelaide put on a noble show of being intensely respectable, a city where it was a sin even to play cards for money, but this was sham. There were more prostitutes to the population than anywhere else in Australia and the girls started work at thirteen. She called the men dull, the women plain and dowdy, the theatre practically non-existent, the architecture 'a horror' and the furniture vulgar.

'The daily excitements of Adelaide,' she wrote, 'are the coming of letters, and the going of the Melbourne express — the fascination of the latter will be readily understood... Be a little more humble, ye people of Adelaide, try to remember that your hills are not the greenest, or your morals the cleanest, or your shops the brightest in the whole world — and if you cannot bring yourselves to remember these things, then bear in mind that your wines are the worst ever made, that some of you are passing plain to look upon, and that you have acquired a world-wide fame for your cruelty to animals — especially horses.'

There were some furious replies, plus a counterattacking pamphlet pointing out that Adelaide had no equal in perfection, and its churches were better attended than any other city in Australia. It said: 'Let Thistle stand on the platform of an incoming train and watch the ecstatic look of joy and relief on the faces of those who have been on a visit to Melbourne.'

Thistle retreated to England and she caused no more stirs in Adelaide.

There was Dulcie Deamer of Sydney. Dulcie Deamer another writer-poet was called the Darling of the Cross and the Queen of Bohemia. In the nineteen-twenties she used to attend parties once a month at Theo's Club in Campbell Street. There were wild dress-ups. One time she went in a leopard skin as a character out of a stone age story. Another time she was the Empress Messalina and she received a ceremonial Roman bath by handmaidens. Another time she was Lady Godiva on a horse. There were chariot races with butter boxes and gladiatorial combats, Christians and wild beasts used to chase each other round the room. Dulcie Deamer's costumes revealed a certain amount of comely flesh but she always had the essential garments on underneath. Even so Sydney was terribly shocked and the Bohemians were considered daring people. They would not cause even a story on page 70 in the nineteen seventies.

Rosaleen Norton, the so-called witch of King's Cross is another. In the nineteen-forties and fifties her painting had a Norman Lindsay gusto and passion with a similar tendency to shock. For 20 years she was almost constantly in and out the courts. In 1952 she teamed with the young poet Gavan Greenlees to produce the book The Art of Rosaleen Norton. There was a limited edition of 1000 copies at eight guineas each. According to The Sun this was a book of such 'stark abandon' an all-male staff had to do the binding. Originally the plan had been to bind in tanned bat skin, but Miss Norton herself objected to the killing of the bats. The most remarkable picture showed a black panther in the arms of a naked woman.

The Sun asked the vice-president of the Progressive Housewives Association, Mrs D. Woodward for her comment on the book. She said it was the most shocking thing she had ever seen. 'The drawings could ruin any home they went into,' she added. A few months later at the Central Court in Sydney Mr Solling S.M. announced that the book was 'obscene and offensive to public chastity and delicacy.'

She was always a spectacular female with batwing eyebrows and large fiery eyes. On the door of her room in King's Cross she had a sign WELCOME TO THE HAUNTS OF GHOSTS, GOBLINS, WEREWOLVES, VAMPIRES, WITCHES, WIZARDS AND POLTERGEISTS. She denied that she indulged in Black Masses, she was more interested in ceremonies to the God Pan. She would have a skull painted with blood of a freshly killed rooster and she surrounded herself with such items as a mummified raven's claw, tame rats, salamanders, goats' head masks and incense. She claimed she was capable of casting spells and putting hexes on people.

One who could cast a spell on a much larger scale is Andrea, who for 40 years was one of Sydney's greatest characters. She was born Dorothy Hetty Fosberry Gordon and even the way she found her name is spectacular. She spent 10 years in Hollywood making silent films, working for Cecil B. de Mille. She spent £25 to get a list of possible names. 'It had everything on it,' she said, 'from cirrhosis to vagina. Then I spotted Andrea and I thought, that's it.' She claims she was twice disengaged, twice married, twice aborted, and twice divorced. Her first marriage which was very brief was to Mr Murray McEwan and her second was to George Onesiphorus Jenner. The Onesiphorus came out of the Bible, but at first she suspected it might be some disease. She divorced him after she found that he had a gift for forging share certificates.

Andrea was a newspaper columnist for three decades. She went away as a war correspondent in 1941 and she was captured by the Japanese in Hong Kong. She spent nearly four years in prison camp. The full blossoming as a character came as a radio talk back lady in the sixties and seventies, that magnificent, deep, name-dropping voice which always began: 'Hello Mums and Dads.' Andrea was the supreme authority, the lady who knew everybody, the lady who had the answer on what to think of Bobby Kennedy through to how to cure warts. When she retired she was never adequately replaced.

Yet unquestionably there is something of the Ratbag in all of us, if only it is allowed a chance to come out, to set the spirit free. To rephrase an old line: 'Everyone here is a ratbag, except me and thee and even thee is something of a ratbag.'
