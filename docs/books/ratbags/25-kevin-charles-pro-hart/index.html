Kevin Charles - Pro Hart

23 min read.

First published 1980

Profile of Pro Hart, the former Broken Hill miner turned Australia's top-selling naive painter, whose galvanised-iron gallery of bull ants, Picassos and junk-metal sculpture reflects a restless enthusiast for motorcycles, weightlifting, religion and giant ice sculptures alike.

BROKEN HILL is a mining city in the arid far west of New South Wales, 1125 kilometres from Sydney, 515 kilometres from Adelaide and just over 900 from Melbourne. It is an island, a civic island in the desert as isolated as a desert atoll. The umbilical cords to civilisation are long unbending strips of bitumen across a landscape that consists of red ochre sand, salt bush and stunted Mallee.

The Mallee grows in weird twisting shapes, always struggling for survival and its wispy leaves give no shade from the blinding sun. The population of Broken Hill is 30,000 but the sense of isolation and clanishness is intense. It is hardly the place one would expect to find Australia's top-selling artist.

Top-selling? Well, his sister Margaret says so. Pictures by Pro Hart rarely, if ever, go unsold. In 1978, all at the one time, he had exhibitions in Hong Kong, Grand Rapids Michigan and Sydney. He is a favorite with Prince Charles, Prince Philip bought three of his paintings at an exhibition in 1973 and Australia's union chief, Bob Hawke, is a fan. He bought a painting for $1100. Pro Hart is represented in most Australian galleries and he has had shows in New York, Los Angeles, London, Singapore and Tokyo.

Pro Hart has been called the heavyweight champion of the naive painters. He has been spurned consistently and viciously by the critics but the racketeers continue to pay him the ultimate compliment, the Pro Hart forgers are at work from here to Hong Kong.

There is not the faintest sense of arrogance or self satisfaction about Pro Hart. At first sight he seems an extraordinarily simple, uncomplicated character. One writer suggested that he looks like the president of the local Rotary club on his day off. Yes he does, but he could also be the plumber who has come to mend the guttering or even the insurance salesman.

Pro Hart is short, he wears big horn-rimmed glasses and he is distinctly tubby about the middle. He wears thongs, a T-shirt or a wind cheeter, plus pale cotton pants. As he paints he wipes his hands or his brushes on his stomach and occasionally on the side of his pants. So always there is a brilliant abstract Pro Hart across his stomach with lesser Pro Hart originals on his thighs.

He is renowned for his enthusiasms, or should one say, his passions. They cover motor cycles, religion, weight lifting, playing the organ, pistols, cameras, sculpture, oxy-acetylene torches, inventions and gadgetry of all kinds, anti-Communism, U.F.O.s and simple plain talking.

It would be a great mistake to under estimate the importance of Broken Hill in the creation of Pro Hart. It is the bricks, the mortar and the roof. When you ask him why he lives there, he says as if you had asked a particularly foolish question: 'It's a great place. It's the centre of everything isn't it? Look I've got great bitumen roads. I can tear off to Melbourne, Sydney, Adelaide, to Brewarrina, back o'Bourke, Wentworth, there are roads in all directions.

'Sometimes when I want to relax I throw my leg over the bike and I just ride off, go say a 100 miles, straight road, no traffic, probably not a human being. Yair, I go pretty quick.

'The heat, no, that doesn't worry me. I like it. In the summer time your paint dries quicker. You mightn't understand, but it's half the problem in being an artist. While you're waiting for the paint to dry the inspiration's gone.'

Broken Hill is a curious mixture. There are great brooding grey slag heaps on which no grass grows. Civic buildings like the Town Hall, Post Office and the Trades Hall are extraordinarily pompous as if they had been shipped out in error when really they belonged in Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds or Stoke. There are streets and streets of little cottages composed entirely of galvanised iron — galvanised iron roofs and galvanised iron walls, the one cheap material not easily chewed by white ants. And the effect is interesting, from on high the tin roofs make a great galvanised lake, an eternity of rigid ripples.

All this along with the salt bush, the Mallee scrub, the pubs with their lean-to verandahs, the grasshoppers, the bull ants, the red soil, it is Pro Hart country exactly as he painted it. The enthusiast who seeks him out drives up Chloride Street until the bitumen runs out, turns left at the water tank on the top of the hill, drives past the splendid mansion once occupied by the millionaire grazier, Sir Sidney Kidman, and there it is, the Pro Hart Gallery.

Nothing quite prepares one for this. There is a white Spanish-style garden wall. Pro Hart is skilled with an oxy-acetylene torch and this wall is adorned with all kinds of junk metal sculpture. One almost looks like a waggon wheel, except upon closer examination, one realises the hub is a manhole cover and the spokes radiate to an ingenious rim composed entirely of shoe lasts.

Walk inside and the garden is a dashing extrovert clutter, every item a symbol of the enthusiastic Pro life. Today there are four motor cycles — sometimes there are more — and one is a potent 500 c.c. Yamaha. There is a very elegant, black, slightly middle-aged Bentley, and one suspects Pro is very proud of that. It has done only 48,000 miles. Half hidden by a tarpaulin lies a 1927 Chevrolet roadster. Pro likes vintage cars and he will restore it when he gets around to it.

Over in the corner there is a striking looking thing, a silver metal cigar with a Spitfire canopy. He is involved here in sponsoring an attempt on the world motor cycle speed record and he has decorated it with the renowned Pro Hart symbol, bull ants. It is difficult to bring to mind any other artist who is an enthusiast for motor cycle racing.

Oh, there are sculptures everywhere. One is 4 or 5 metres high and composed of steel boxes. It is called Metal Crystals. But he has a sense of humour this man, on the left is an irresistible thing composed of antique fire alarm boxes and he has welded a garden tap on the side.

Rising out of the earth there are very odd misshapen bits of metal. There's a story here — Pro Hart is always experimenting with new art forms. On this occasion he took some sheet metal from car bodies, placed them in sand out on a mine site and blew them up with gelignite. He was thrilled with the abstract shapes that came out of the blast.

On the right is the Pro Hart dwelling. Here he lives with his wife Raelee, his three sons and two daughters. It is white and classic Broken Hill, acres of galvanised iron, which in the desert dryness is almost impermeable. Straight in front is the Pro Hart Gallery, built of concrete blocks on three levels and emblazoned on the white wall, almost like an ad for detergent, in enormous black letters are the two words... PRO HART.

It costs a dollar to go in and it is a very busy scene. You can buy copies of The Art of Pro Hart by Eugene Lumbers, copies of the poems of Lawson or Paterson, illustrated by Pro Hart, or you can buy Pro Hart drawings or prints. In the midst of all this there are three organs; there's a large pipe organ down the back, but in the front just behind the counter is an electronic organ with a console imposing enough for the grand old days of the Regent Theatre or the Majestic. Actually it was used at the opening of the Sydney Opera House and it cost $35,000.

But it is the pictures that take the eye. This is a gallery that has less spare space than a flea market — pictures, pictures, pictures. He has more than 600 of them, but what a collection — Herman, Tucker, Perceval, Fairweather, Dickerson, Pugh, Looby, Heysen, Blackman, Nolan, Conder, Roberts, McInnes, Whiteley, Streeton, the whole tribe of Boyds, plus Chagall, Dali and Picasso.

How does one insure, how does one care for such a collection? Some years back he did try to insure them all for a million dollars, but the rates were too expensive. He has a complicated series of burglar alarms and a panel of lights in his bedroom which gives him the security story in every corner of his gallery.

Interspersed, side by side with the odd Picasso, Tom Roberts or Arthur Boyd, there is a retrospective exhibition of Pro Hart going back to the nineteen fifties — they are all there, grasshoppers, desert flowers, haunted Dobell-like miners deep in the underground, blue-grey Mallee scenes, those naive paintings of his such as the miner's wedding, full of boozy figures, an old pub and, of course, dog, mean hungry dogs. You take it for granted that a Pro Hart dog is always lifting its leg. It is practically a signature like the bull ant.

Where isn't there the art of Pro Hart? On the door to the public lavatories there are two glass panels. Pro has done little pictures on these also, including some bull ants. But there is a large painting by the door and every visitor ponders over this. It looks like a modern Tower of Babel, a very ricketty Tower of Babel. It is still under construction and everywhere in this picture there are Cardinals, Bishops, Priests, grandiloquent church processions, the clergy looking very fine indeed, but at the same time ridiculous. The Pro Hart message is obvious, the traditional formal church is trying to build expensively into the sky, but what for?

'You don't need all that nonsense,' according to Pro Hart. 'You can find Jesus in a rubbish tip if you are prepared to look for him.'

Another picture shows Christ crucified out amongst the red sand and the sun-blasted Mallee. The message is just the same.

Near the Opera House organ console is a smaller picture, called 'Larloona Homestead'. You couldn't get anything more typical, a roof of red galvanised iron, a low verandah all around, a few outhouses, a windmill, a water tank, bare ungrassed ground, some Mallee trees and kitehawks hovering up above. Larloona is 24 kilometres from Menindee and 112 from Broken Hill. This is Burke and Wills country. The explorers started from Menindee when they struck out for the Gulf of Carpentaria in 1860.

Pro Hart was born at Larloona on 30 May 1928 and he was christened Kevin Charles Hart. Raelee and sister Margaret both call him 'Kev' but to everyone else he is Pro and Pro Hart is the signature on his paintings.

It is short for 'Professor', the old-fashioned term for anyone who was slightly off-beat, ingenious or eccentric. He told John Lapsley of the Australian: 'It goes back into the dark ages. When I was about 14, I think, it just started. You know, one of those things, a bit like calling Murpheys "Spud". It was through inventing things. I used to work on explosives. I'd do anything that I thought I could sell — I was interested in making money but someone else always put it on the market first.'

It is not easy to persuade Pro Hart to talk about his youth. For one thing he says he has a zero memory. 'Shocking it is,' he said. 'I can't remember a name for two minutes. What's yours again?' For another he said: 'Look I'm not interested in the past or tradition. I'm a NOW man. What's going on this minute is of importance to me.'

He does admit that his love of drawing, cartooning as he calls it, started very early. Like many an outback bush boy his early schooling was by correspondence and his lessons came from Blackfriars School in Sydney. Eugene Lumbers writes that unasked he used to illustrate his correspondence lessons. Busily he drew everything in sight, the drunk sitting under a tree, a duckshooter stalking the edge of a swamp, the outback house ants and dragonflies with their diaphonous wings. The teachers were impressed. They asked Pro's mother to keep her son drawing and asked if they could keep the pictures for their records.

In 1941 Pro Hart's parents moved to Broken Hill so that they could tackle the problem of how to educate their children. Pro for three years went to the Marist Brothers College. Desperately he missed the kindly Blackfriars Correspondence School. He found the discipline of the Brothers tough, unbending and life was tedious. He even tried unsuccessfully to leave school under the legal age. He was so unhappy that for years he deserted both art and religion.

His first job at the age of fifteen was driving a baker's cart. He then did other such things as laboring at a cordial factory and working on the grape vines at Mildura.

At 19 the inevitable happened, like most young available men in Broken Hill, he went down the mines. 'I worked in the mines fifteen or sixteen years. It was bloody terrible mate, horrible. I hated every day of it. I was scared all the time I was there.'

He told Eugene Lumbers: 'I've worked with blokes who've been killed later. I've had lumps of ore fall off the roof and I've been hit with a piece of steel. I've run over gelignite with the train and it hasn't gone off. Miners used to amaze me. They have their smoke and go underground and walk around. They don't seem to worry they could get hit with a dirty big slab of rock or something. I'm damn sure I used to worry. My blooming oath I did. I used to show it. I'm sure the blokes I was working with didn't worry like I did.

All the years he was working underground Pro used to paint. He said: 'I used to live opposite the hospital and sometimes my paintings would be hung over there. I never thought about making money out of it. My paintings would go for five bob.

'I remember back around 1955 I had never heard of Jackson Pollock, but I did a picture Pollock style, dropping paint from a height. The boys hung it in their crib, you know, the humpies underground where they have tea. I suppose some bugger has got it now.'

'Oh yes, they'd rubbish you. They don't like a bloke to be different. They thought I was a bit of a twit, a crackpot. Of course, it was the thing at the mines when your shift was over to go to the pub and drink until you're stupid. I didn't like doing that so I didn't fit into the groove.

'I was crazy about art. Instead of going into the pub I spent every penny I had on art. The first painting I bought was a George Winnen. The second was by William Dobell, "The Lavender Seller". It cost me £150, a fortune then, but I was single at the time.

'Next I bought three old masters, then I plunged. I bought a John Perceval for £350. You should have heard the howl. "Fancy paying all that for a bloody old tugboat." What a nit, they thought. The same old tugboat is now worth $30,000 or $40,000. People are easily led, they can't see in front of their noses.'

Life changed very dramatically for Pro Hart in 1962. Kym Bonython, the Adelaide art director, jazz lover, racing car driver, was putting on a show of sixty paintings in a Broken Hill Lubritorium. 'I don't remember much about it now,' said Pro. 'All I can remember is that Kym had one of his underwater photography submarines on show. Isn't that funny. I can't remember anybody who was there but I can remember practically all the paintings.

None of Pro Hart's paintings were in that exhibition, but wandering around hotels and cafes Kym Bonython spotted Pro Hart after Pro Hart. He became fascinated and he knew that he had discovered a naive painter of considerable talent. Soon after he put on a one man Pro Hart exhibition in Adelaide and sold every picture.

This was the great beginning. There were exhibitions all around Australia. The days underground were over and he was a full time artist. The paintings which were sold in pubs for five shillings rose in value to over $3000.

When I found Pro he was busy on a large canvas which was to be the pub at Menindee. This was his new style, he said. He was laying on acrylic paint in big dobs. The colors were heavy and dark and the road outside the Menindee pub was like a great blue river.

'I didn't have any art training,' he said. 'An art school will only ruin someone with talent. All they can do is teach you how to mix paints. To me painting is a happening. It's a NOW thing that comes into my head. If I lie in bed at night and worry about it and work it out, then it's not good, hopeless. The natural inspiration has to flow.

'Aw, this has happened to me so often. I have planned and worried about paintings. Then I've ruined them. I've thrown away over two hundred.'

He had a color picture of the Menindee pub on his easel. 'Yes, that's what I do. I take a color picture first. Well, why not make use of modern techniques? One time I used to make working drawings first, but you lose inspiration in the process. I do better using my camera. No, no, I don't do a copy of the photo. I couldn't even copy one of my own pictures. My paintings are a bit of everything that comes into my mind.'

Pro Hart is very fond of a good happening. Only two days before he had taken his 'Balloon Happening' for exhibition at the opening of a new Broken Hill gallery, the Frieburg. The Balloon Happening was Jackson Pollock in the grand style.

'It was simple enough,' he said, 'I put three sheets of fibre board on the ground, six feet by four feet, then up I went in the balloon. We positioned ourselves between sixty and a hundred feet then I had all the paint tins ready in the proper order. And I had the consistency of the paint just right for dropping.

'There were red, blues, browns and I had a special tin of yellow at the finish to give a lovely sprinkle right across.'

We went across to the Frieburg Gallery to inspect the finished work. It was vast and much more imposing than one expected. The yellow flecks of paint look like lights on an earth-seascape. Pro looked at it with beaming admiration, like Rolf Harris admiring British Paints. 'Acrylic paint — that picture will last longer than the Pyramids.'

He had another Happening at the Adelaide Art Festival of 1978. He built an ice sculpture twelve metres high, symbolising a vase and on top he placed huge flowers which he fashioned out of fibre board. It contained 1008 blocks of ice, 40,000 gallons of water and it took twenty-three truckloads to cart the ice from Glenelg to North Adelaide where workmen put the blocks into place with the help of lift conveyors.

Adelaide's Melbourne Street Traders' commissioned the work and it cost $8000. Pro commented: 'It was bloody cold working with that ice. I had my gloves on but my hands were numb after ten minutes.'

It took more than ten days to melt and some cynics commented that it had tremendous advantage which should be possessed by many other sculptures around Adelaide, it was self-destructing. It had an even more exciting advantage, frozen into the ice were five dozen bottles of champagne, hidden treasure for those who watched it melt.

The theory was that it would melt into fantastic shapes, but Pro was a little disappointed. 'We used chicken wire between the ice blocks to hold it all together and that spoilt it a bit. I'm going to do another one in Broken Hill and I'll get the melting moving the way I want it. I'll attack the ice blocks with a chain saw.

He has another painting in mind which he will do on a majestic scale. He will put paint down on one of the outback saltpans. Maybe he will use a crop duster and as they fly across the white dry lake jets from the aircraft will release paint on the biggest canvas ever. 'I'll need a lot of paint so I'm looking for a sponsor among the paint companies.'

He is also looking for a sponsor for his bull ant. He wants to build a bullant 15 metres high, something which will tower over Broken Hill. He has had the idea for five years and he is still working on it.

'I have built two models,' he said, 'one small, one about a metre or so high. I have had structure problems. You see God gave the bull ant very fine legs and when you build the thing 15 metres high in steel those legs aren't strong enough to take the weight. I think I've overcome it. I'll put the tail on the ground and I may put a support under the centre. I want it to be safe enough for kids to climb on. I intend to build it all right — I might even pay for it myself.'

He has studios all around the back of his gallery. They are an amazing clutter of bits and pieces — tubes of paint, brushes, cameras, tripods, a Honda lighting set, oxy-acetylene equipment, a radio controlled model aeroplane, spare engine parts, and as we walk he insists that he is a very poor talker with nothing to say, but he talks all the time jumping from one subject to another. 'See that screen there, the one with the hole in it. I love to get behind that and take pictures of little kids pushing their noses against the window, trying to see what I'm doing.'

He interrupted that idea with; 'look at that! He grabbed an imposing Nikon camera, ran outside, pointed the camera straight at the ground and started taking pictures.

As he came back he looked triumphant: 'Marvellous. A circle of blowies. Look at them, see how their green backs shine in the sun. That'll make a good shot. I'll send it to the camera club.'

He says that he works regular hours, then takes two clear days off at the week-end. This is something utterly denied by Raelee, who says he works all the time, week-ends as well. Pro waves that amiably aside and says he knocks off at five o'clock to do his weight lifting. 'I go into my gym there and I lift weights for about an hour. I have a routine, working to heavier weights. Sometimes if I'm busy I break it into two sessions morning and evening.'

His gym with all his weights is in between the clutter of his studios. One begins to wonder if there is anything this man can or doesn't do. The Professor still likes to invent. He invented a fast-firing machine gun with a disposable magazine. The idea was that this was a gun which could be used, say, by little old ladies in times of invasion. But he wasn't over anxious to talk about it, the Army took it away.

He invented a machine for turning copper ceramics into bronze sculptures. He heats the copper with an oxy-acetylene torch, then under great pressure puts the molten copper through a spray.

He has an idea for a new much more efficient carburettor. 'The present carburettor has too many jets and screws. My idea is to pre-mix the fuel, to have it pre-vaporised so that the engine always gets what it wants. I did have troubles with explosions, back-firing, but I think I have solved that now.'

He admits that he rarely has time to follow through his inventions. The thought occurs that maybe all these things are a form of escape from the intense pressure of the art world.

Every so often he received merciless treatment from art critics. A criticism, far from rare, appeared under the name of the Australian art critic:

'He illustrates country folklore with a series of repetitious color schemes, syrupy and pretty, which are thinly washed over an "ocker" drawn scene.... Hart's technical handling of the paint and compositional keyboard is unnervingly uniform and excruciatingly predictable. His subject matter, on the other hand, is directly aimed at the city slicker's nostalgic view of the bush and his country brothers.'

Does that sort of thing worry him?

'Yes, it used to worry me, but I find it doesn't make any difference what they say. I've just had an exhibition in Hong Kong and they gave me very good reviews there. Except one got my name wrong and called me Pop Hart.

'These critics think you should do the same paintings all the time in the same style as when you started. Can't bear anything different. I did a series on the Battle of Trafalagar. I got interested in that, went to England, inspected the Victory and did a lot of research.

'The point that moved me was this. None of the paintings that ever had been done of Trafalgar depicted the pain and the suffering of the sailors. That's what I wanted to bring out. Critics didn't like the shift of pace. "Pro Hart is all at sea", said one. "He should stick to bull ants." I thought it was my best series and I've sold all but one of 27 of them.

'I can tell you the name of one critic who went to the gallery at Sydney. He only went as far as the front door and put his foot on it. This was to prove that he had been to my exhibition, but he hadn't seen the paintings. He still went away and knocked them in his criticism.

'It gets through to the public too. They come up here and say "Hello Pro, still churning them out?" Churn, that's the word they use. I don't churn. Sometimes I take three weeks over a picture and I rarely have more than twenty in my exhibitions.

'Yes, it looks as if I paint a lot of pictures, but I take them around and say, "See that picture it was painted in seventy-two. That was done in sixty-six, that one in seventy-four." Same thing — a lot of my pictures come up at auctions, but they've been done over a long period.

'I don't charge big money for my pictures either, none of them have ever been sold for more than $3000 and that's the way I want it. I believe God gave me a gift and I should share it.'

He talks a great deal about God, about Jesus. His faith constantly comes into the conversation... 'People say I'm a man of many parts. I'm not really. I believe God gives every person a gift — it just has to come out. My gift is painting. My other gift perhaps is weight-lifting. I don't have any others. Motor cycle racing? No, I always come last. The organ? No, that's just playing on Saturday nights for my own amusement.'

Pro said he had a surfeit of religion when he was a boy and lost his faith. Then started a search to find some meaning in life. He read philosophy, studied Buddhism. Then one day down the mines he had a revelation, a belief that God was still there.

'Even then,' he said, 'I didn't understand that Jesus was here and relevant now. I never read the Bible, but somebody told me to look at Matthew Chapter six. You know the line, Take not thought unto you what you shall eat, what you shall drink and so on. I was stunned by this. It was what I had been looking for.

'A few days later I was playing tennis with my wife. The ball went out of court and I noticed a piece of paper rolling across the ground. Do you know what it was? written on it was the same Matthew Chapter six.

'So I discovered Jesus. From that moment my entire life changed. Kym Bonython came to Broken Hill, all my pictures began selling.'

Pro and his family belong to a group called the Christian Revival Crusade, a hearty outgoing church group, which believes utterly in the word of the Bible. Pro himself believes the signs are there, taken from the Bible, that the Second Coming is very near, that within a year or two Christ will return to earth. He doesn't spurn the various Church denominations. He says: 'They're all getting together. I was in Rome recently, they're throwing away their vestments, getting closer to Christ. I'll go into any Church to worship.'

Religion, he says, keeps him humble, stops him getting big-headed and one must give it credit for the disarming, remarkable, self confidence the man has. He knows the inspiration will be there for the painting he will start tomorrow.

As I left him he was getting ready to go pistol shooting. 'We do it on a range out in the bush,' he said. 'I haven't shot with the club for months. We have a fifty metre range. You are supposed to do forty shots in about two hours. I do mine in ten minutes and I'm gone.'

That's Pro Hart, he hates to waste a second, even when he's pistol shooting.
