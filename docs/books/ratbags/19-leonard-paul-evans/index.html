Leonard Paul Evans

23 min read.

First published 1980

Profile of Len Evans, the ebullient Welsh-born wine judge, restaurateur and promoter who built the Australian Wine Bureau, the Rothbury Estate and a string of eccentric wine societies while transforming the nation's drinking habits.

IN 1976 Len Evans listed his private dreams in Cleo Magazine. He made it clear that he would like to 'be a lot taller, a lot lighter, trim, taut and terrifically terpsichorean. I'd like to have an I.Q. of 200, be an authority on the sex habits of the Bolivian ant and an expert player of the 10-string guitar. I would like to be the head of the Welsh Society of Free Thinkers, a very small group indeed. My life would be a combination of the English country squire's week-end, the Algonquin Round Table luncheons and Slocum's first solo voyage. I'd love to open Parliament for Australia and close the Mouse Trap and I would give anything to be physically, mentally spiritually and emotionally equipped to star in Number 96. But then, if I did change myself in all these ways I am terrible afraid that I would lose my wonderful humility'.

Apart from these humble ambitions he is chief wine judge at the Sydney Show. His wine palate would have to rank among the first six in the country and he would certainly query the other five. He is a wine writer, a restaurateur, the chairman of one of Australia's biggest vineyards, a TV entertainer, a born talker and story teller. If you can raise $1000 for charity then he will talk to your club. He does forty such talks a year.

He is a collector of 18th and 17th century oak, he is a reasonable authority on old pewter and silver and he keeps draught horses. He has an ambition to run a draught horse stud. He is terribly impressed with draught horses. He is small, chunky, curly-haired and his weight flows between fifteen stone and twelve according to the ferocity of the threats of his doctors. He is likely to burst into song on festive occasions and there is much about him both visually and through downright ebullience that reminds one of Dylan Thomas. He has had his critics and opponents, but there is a formidable band who believe that he more than anyone else has changed the drinking habits of a nation. Back around 1966 on a trip home to Sydney he announced that he would make a million in ten years. Journalist Denis O'Brien queried him about this extraordinarily ambitious remark.

'I was misquoted' said Len. 'I actually said that I would make two million in ten years.'

Even if he didn't quite make that amount, he has borrowed much more, created much more, and lived 65 seconds of nearly every minute. One time I said to him: 'Why are you so lazy? You work at the office from 8.30 am, put on dinners and such until midnight daily but you do literally nothing between 6.00 am and 8.00 am."

'You haven't heard?' he said. 'I am now doing potted wine talks on radio to catch the going to work traffic'.

He does things in style. He has a burgundy or should one say a dry red Bentley Cosmopolitan plus a Silver Cloud Rolls-Royce.

The Rolls has a curious history. He was doing promotion and consulting work for Tooth & Co. They were having problems in negotiating payment when the chairman said: "Well, would this solve the situation? Whereupon he brought out the company's Silver Cloud Rolls-Royce."

'So now I can use the Bentley as my day car and the Rolls as my night car.'

He uses the Bentley when driving visiting wine dignitaries to the Hunter. It is invariably a very long day so he starts early. The custom is to stop half way and have breakfast. He chooses precisely the right spot, a noble view across the mountains and serves champagne and caviar. The champagne, naturally, is French, because he believes this is the field in which the French remain supreme.

What's more it is another part of the Evans style. He likes to serve champagne as an aperitif and he sees it as the incomparable conversation promoter. "I think champagne, French, of course, if one may borrow or bludge it, is the drink for friends popping in and out and I can understand what wealth is all about when I yearn to have buckets of it, appropriately chilled, for my friends'.

An example of his style was the famous $200 a plate dinner. In February 1977 he invited eleven of the nation's top palates, plus the Prime Minister, Malcolm Fraser, to dinner. The fee was $200 a head, all to go to charity.

This was the menu:

FOOD: Turtle Soup; Sand Crag; Tahmoor Qualis; Beef Medallions with Truffles; Rasbery Souffle; Nuts and Muscatels.

WINE: Very Dry Oloroso, 1795; Le Montrachet (Domaine de la Romanee Conti), 1972; Clos de la Roche (Barolet), 1921; Chateau Haut Brion, 1929; Vouvray Liquoreux (Marc Bedif), 1921; Chateau d'Yquem, 1921; Quinta do Noval, 1931; Seppeltsfield, 1878.

OLD WINE TASTING: Rudesheimer Apostelwein, 1727; Chateau Villemaurine, 1898; Chateau Ausone, 1894; Chateau Lafite, 1893; Chateau Peyrabon, 1877; Chateau Lafite, 1874; Sunbury Hermitage, 1872; Chateau d'Yquem, 1888; Chateau Gruaud Larose, 1825.

He brought the half bottle of 1727 Rudesheimer at Christies in 1975 for $450 and the 1825 Gruaud Larose cost him $510 in 1976.

The dinner caused an eruption of biting letters in the newspapers. For example Mrs D. Hughes of Elwood wrote in the Herald, Melbourne 'Not only was this event discriminatory, but downright suspect. I'm tired of ruddy snobs, and the pretentious behaviour that accompanies this kind of event. If Mr Evans is sincere in giving to charity why not write a cheque for the equivalent of what this "do" would cost thus permitting him to keep his precious wines'.

Females across the country were unanimous in their fury because no women were invited, but Evans showed remarkable courage. When asked to comment he said: "There's no female palate good enough to invite. There are no female wine judges." He did add that he delighted in female company, but this was different.

There were suggestions also regarding the sanity of a man who would pay $450 for a bottle of wine and miserable half bottle at that. Evans commented in the Bulletin:

'Pay $450 for a bottle of wine! The man must be mad. Probably, but it's my money, and I don't criticise the gamblers and the questers for the Admiral's Cup and the racehorse owners and the status seekers, all of whom, to me, spend their money other than I would. But it's their business. I just wanted to taste the wine.'

The dinner took place at his restaurant in Bulletin Place, Sydney. The guest of honor was Michael Broadbent, Head of the Wine Department at Christies and just possibly the top wine critic in the world. They had little glasses specially blown for the occasion to taste the Rudesheimer and Evans commented.

'The color of the 1727 was a deep, yet light, yellow gold brown, rather that of a light shaded piece of amber. The nose was extremely rich and penetrating, like a fine old Madeira yet more intense and without spirit overtones. The palate was deep flavored and subtle, with a denseness of flavor still light and neither cloying nor in the least coarse... The perfume persisted in the glass long after we had finished. In fact, I nosed my glass five hours later and the wine was still there. And here it was, 250 years old. Think of the empires wasted, the music written, the words exchanged, the love made, the endless machinations of man, while that wine waited in the cellar at Bremen.'

Mr Broadbent thought the wine 'amazingly drinkable'. More like a sherry than a hock. A reporter from the Australian asked Broadbent if he thought Evans was mad. 'Both mad and a man with enormous imagination', he replied.

However you could question the madness. Had he wished to advertise his wine business the same amount of newspaper space, the same amount of time on radio and television he received that week would have been worth at least a million dollars.

When did Len Evans first become interested in wine? He was born in 1930. His parents were Welsh and his father was a mechanic in the Royal Air Force. He described himself as a 'very callow youth' when one day his father brought up some celebratory bottles from the cellar. He stole a bottle of Chateau d'Yquem and hid it under his bed. This he uncorked and he proceeded to drink in small sips progressively night by night. Nobody had told him that wine exposed to the air tends to go off like milk. So he was surprised when this sweet luscious wine became progressively more sour and even developed fungus on the surface.

His next wine experience was at school. He won a scholarship to Framlingham, Suffolk's top school and rose to the elevated position of prefect with his own study. He created history by entertaining in anything but schoolboy style. He provided dry sherry, the local potent cider and chicken sandwiches. When one asked whether this was with the approval of the hierarchy he replied: 'Christ No'.

As a bright young man he left school and he had the choice before him of any number of careers. He could have gone on to Cambridge, but first he had to cure himself of a Walter Mitty dream, he wanted to be the world's greatest golfer. He got a job as an assistant professional at Shandon Golf Club, at £3 a week. This was in 1951-52 and the first thing he learned was that assistant pros don't get to play much golf. He worked from 8.00 am to 7.00 pm and he spent most of his time in the pro shop working on clubs.

He did finish fifth in one open tournament but he laments that the opportunities for a young man to make a living out of golf then were nothing like they are today. The money didn't exist and even if a player did well in a tournament the prize money might only be £10.

But where was the wine? There was an occasional glass when as physical training officer he did his national service with the R.A.F. in Germany. Then at the age of twenty-three he migrated to New Zealand to work as a Forestry Officer. Here he drank mostly dry sherries and madeira. During the week-ends he went to the beach, slept in caves, dived for abalone and washed it down with flagons of rough New Zealand dry red. The taste was gathering force.

In 1955 he established his beach-head in Australia worked for several months in a car factory, an event he remembers with a screwed up face as if he had swallowed a glass of Cold Duck.

He was also a dingo fencer, a welder, and he even tried something which has sounded good in feature articles ever since — he was a sucker basher. It didn't call for great skill — it was knocking the shoots off ring barked trees.

Next he went to Mt. Isa where he graduated from laborer to steel hand to foreman. Now his interest in wine was real. Whenever he went to parties instead of beer he took along his wine. 'They didn't actually think I was a poofter, the wine was just a charming aberration and eventually people began to buy it for me. One day I went into Boyds Hotel and asked for a case of Arrawatta Riesling. The man in the bottle shop said: 'Ker-rist — you'll buy a whole case of wine — Jeee-sus"'

He met his future wife Trish in Mount Isa. He said: 'She was recovering from a broken serious romance and I was recovering from the thought of any serious romance. I courted her with cold chicken and half bottles of champagne'.

In the midst of all this he had another Mitty dream, Evans the writer. He had some success with short stories, so in 1958 he went south to Sydney to seek fame and glory. He wrote for Pocket Book, Man, for Bobby Limb and Dawn Lake for the ABC. He even used the byline Singapore Evans, which he thought had a romantic ring. Quickly he discovered the first law of motion about writing — there is very little money in it.

In his own biographical details he writes: "In the CASUAL WORK AVAILABLE ads one day appeared: "Casual man wanted, glass washer, Ship Inn, Circular Quay". Being very casual I applied for the job, and got it. And it was the last job that I have every applied for. I'll never cease to marvel at what an amazing country Australia is.

'A succession of quick promotions, sudden deaths, mysterious disappearances, I felt a bit like Richard III, and all of a sudden I was assistant beverage manager at the Chevron Hilton Hotel, not yet opened, not yet even built, but we had a cellar to put the wine in. In the meantime my chicken chewing sweetheart and I got married. I had become an authority on controls systems in hotels, and we kept our wine under the bed.'

At Chevron he says he literally grew up with the wine industry. He went there to learn but it was a case of the blind leading the blind. There were no courses, no books, 'But I did have to buy enormous quantities of wine and this gave me a chance to do a lot of tasting. People were fantastic to me. As soon as they saw I was interested they gave tremendous encouragement. They invited me to their wine clubs, to Margaux dinners, people like Thyne Reid, the great old wine buff looked after me."

He was there four-and-a-half years until he was assistant general manager to Frank Christie, now his very good friend, but wine was the thing. The palate — some might say the magnificently arrogant palate — was flowering. He became an assistant wine judge, the first step towards becoming Chief Judge in 1978.

The blunt character who launched his palate on Chateau d'Yquem could never stand the voluble pompous characters on wine. He loved to deflate. On one occasion he dipped his fingers into the wine:

'It's obvious it's a Hunter'.

'Why' some asked.

'You can tell by the feel of the wine, quite different from a Barossa. Notice what a rubbery texture it has.'

Very smartly he had everyone around the table, dipping their fingers into the glasses looking for the feel.

Then he devised the ideal speech for the Australian at the dinner party, who knew nothing. When asked to comment on the wines he could come out with the right brilliant words.

First there are these laws of probability. More than two-thirds of all wine produced in Australia comes from South Australia. The predominant grape used in table red production is shiraz. Table wine consumption has increased to such an extent that there are few old wines around today. Yet most wine makers like to give their wines a few years bottle age on top of the year in the cask.

So Len offered this advice.

'Pick up your red and sniff thoughtfully, sip some on to the tongue and roll it round your mouth, stare soulfully into the middle distance and then finally pronounce...

'A very good Australian red, showing the strides made by local wine makers in recent years, with a good nose and flavor, light and balanced with a good clean finish. Typically South Australian I should, think, and the predominant grape used in the making of it was the Shiraz variety. Will still improve though good to drink now. I would say it's about three to four years old!'

'Chances are you'd be dead right'.

In 1965 he left Chevron and formed the Australian Wine Bureau to promote wine on a national scale. He had a fine team with men like John Stanford, Fred McKeever, Arthur Moore, Frank Margan, Frank Doherty and Jack Ludbrook but the ebullient Evans was everywhere. He was writing Cellarmaster in the Bulletin, at least three other wine columns. He was on radio, on TV.

Maybe it was partly Australia growing up but during the Evans era at the Bureau wine consumption in Australia almost doubled. At one stage he made this juicy plea:

'In simple terms, you're not doing enough! After all, a measly 1.22 gallons a year. And in a country that produces truly consistent wines of high quality at most reasonable prices. Of this just over .4 of a gallon is table wine. There are six bottles to a gallon. So you are drinking on an average, two bottles of table wine a year each! Two measly bottles.

'Yet being conservative, aware of the means of means, assessing off-days, dry-days, sick days, furiously busy days, I still reckon I average a half bottle a day. That's 182 bottles a year, fifteen-odd dozen, or thirty-one gallons! You other blokes have a hell of a lot of catching up to do'.

Not only did consumption go up new vineyards were planted all across the nation. His old friend and fellow wine judge, Dr Max Lake, put seventeen-and-a-half acres under vine at Pokolbin on the Hunter. His initial outlay, which seemed awe-inspiring at the time, was $40,000 and he called it 'Lake's Folly'.

The first crushing was in 1966. Len Evans, Max Lake and friends did it in the pure, classic style. They foot-trod the grapes in an open-ended vat. One guest was so anxious to help he took off his trousers, shoes and socks so that his pink feet could join all the others. The scene was a little curious because he was still wearing his coat, shirt and tie. The wine like many that were to follow was of great quality even though it had been squeezed by some of the finest and flattest feet in Sydney.

Sometimes Evans promotion methods were too robust for the mature old hands. After all, wine making is like the law, its practitioners believe profoundly in precedent.

After two-and-a-half years as the number one promoter he became a consultant, but his voice on the wine scene was hardly less dim.

One did suggest that sometimes there was a spare hour before breakfast when Evans could be caught silent or even idle. In 1970 he was involved in two massive projects, sufficient to put a red trembles through the bank balance. He bought an old warehouse in Bulletin Place, Sydney, not far from the Ship Inn where he used to wash glasses, and he created Len Evans Wines.

The warehouse was built, probably in the late eighteen-twenties and he found it most satisfying to be able to tell all Victorian visitors that here was a building that was in action even before Melbourne was invented.

On the ground floor he opened his wine business, on the first floor he ran his Tasting Room quickly became the In place to dine in Sydney, that's if you had three hours. It was all run with a ruthless Evans eclat. If you were not there on time he would boot you out. 'Good cooking demands good timing', said he.

The menu always fixed, often something old-fashioned like English plum pudding, or Irish stew or Sydney Harbour fish stew with mussels and oysters, or game pie, followed by cheese.

First he would serve champagne, then at 1.00 pm he would beat a gavel and demand that everyone sit down — church pews and long wooden benches. The ecclesiastical feeling wasn't entirely by accident.

Then he would proceed: 'I trust you have cancelled all your appointments for this afternoon. With the main course today we have three young reds I want you to look at. A Tahbilk '67, Shiraz — rather light in character, a '66 Oven's Valley Wynne's, soft, smooth, very drinkable; and a '67 Nottage Hill, good character, would repay cellaring'...

One half of Evans was at Len Evans wine, the other half was busy creating The Rothbury Estate. Until 1960 the famous Hunter River produced maybe half of one percent of Australia's wine, and most of the wineries were ramshackle depending on their charm on genuine, indigenous galvanised iron. Indeed most of the Hunter reds received their annual cooking under iron every summer to temperatures up to 120 degrees fahrenheit.

In 1968 a group of ten investors created the Rothbury Estate with Len Evans as chairman, Murray Tyrrell as vineyard manager and Gerry Sissingh as wine maker. They spent $4 million and with associated vineyards had nearly 1,000 acres under vine. Pre 1960 the entire Hunter had only 900 acres.

But the edifice they put up; that was the thing, a pure example of Evans gusto. The locals called it the Pokolbin Opera House but it looked as big as Melbourne's Exhibition building. The Evans plan was to sell all the wine direct from the Estate and to make Rothbury a cult, complete with society.

One can't begin to describe how noble the great hall of Rothbury looked. Some people go to church just to observe the ceiling. This ceiling was magnificent, dark beams against white, so ecclesiastical the visitor tended to cross himself upon entering.

Then down the nave were three vast baronial tables. On either side and overhead were 1,000 gallon oak casks and no doubt it gave dinners a feeling of comfort and security to know they were surrounded by three million gallons of wine.

As in many a good church or cathedral there was a choir loft up above for madrigal singers. Then outside there was a large church bell to be rung on deeply significant occasions such as the beginning or end of vintage.

In March 1973 Len Evans launched the Rothbury ribbon dinners. The theory was that members of the Rothbury Society would undergo wine examinations on their wine tastings. First stage was the white ribbon the next was the green and the third was the red. The ultimate ribbon for those who could practically identify the very paddock from where a wine came was the purple. Evans never imagined that no more than twenty people would ever rise to purple.

The first ribbon night had all the atmosphere of a degree conferring ceremony. As the skilled tasters stepped forward the famous Rudy Komon, art dealer-wine expert, shook their hands and placed the ribbon which was adorned with a pewter tasting cup, around their necks.

But all this does not explain the entire range of Evans activities. For example, there is cricket. Back around 1969-70 a group of gourmets and literary gentlemen decided that life on the Paddington Hill at the Sydney Cricket Ground needed more comfort, more style, so they formed the Paddington Hill Ancient And Royal Tasting Society; or better known as P.H.A.R.T.S.

There were office bearers. Captain and Wine Master was Len Evans; vice-captain and salami master was the columnist Ron Saw, Walter Sullivan was ale master, Val Sowada, the only French speaking Swiss cricket lover was cheese master; David Reed, the publisher was fish master and so on.

All this caused some astonishment on the Hill. Ron Saw recalls this dialogue between a Hillite and the Society.

'What the xxxxxx' he asked, 'are youse gettin' into yez?'

'It is', said Evans, 'a sly little traminer which you might find droll. Can I tempt you?'

'Kernoath', said the Hillite. 'Tel yez the truth, we bin watchin' yez for a while and me mates reckoned yers were a buncher queers. But I dunno — I reckon you jokers must know how to do it'.

Another esoteric Evans activity has been the Options Club. Every Monday in Bulletin Place the club has met for a little game. Each member has a masked bottle and certain questions are asked such as what grape variety? What district? Is it older than ten years or younger? Is it a '64 or '65 etc. For every question, members have to put 20 cents on the table and if they fail the money goes into the kitty.

Just lately they have made it very tough with questions such as: 'This is not a Bordeaux or a Burgundy. It is a Medoc. Which of the tour major communies? Or perhaps: 'This is a Cote d'Or. Does it come from south of Corton, Clos de Vougeot, or Chambolle Musigny?'

All the losing 20 cents pieces go into Opera House Lottery tickets and Len Evans believes there is no question that members of the Options Club have built one of the sails of the Sydney Opera House. At the end of ten weeks there is a dinner and the member who has done most miserably, has to pay for it. There is a case of champagne for the winner.

The Options club progressed so beautifully that in 1975 Len Evans challeged Victoria to a Test Match. He claimed that he had a wine-drinking team which, for sheer skill and knowledge, could out-taste any other wine drinkers in the world.

Peter C. Joyce, president of the Wine and Food Society in Victoria, accepted the challenge. 'It is beyond dispute', said he 'that the palates of the Wine ahnd Food Society of Victoria are superior in every aspect of wine tasting to those of any other group, state or conglomeration of people".

Both teams went into very hard training. Evans decreed that his men should eschew all damaging physical exercise, but they should concentrate, say, on a Montrachet, a Mearsault and an Alsatian Traminer in a hard training session before breakfast, with at least four Bordeaux before lunch, and then pant through five or six Burgundies.

There were no orders about dress but the New South Wales team arrived all wearing quite alarming Edwardian blazers. They had the NSW waratah on the pocket and underneath the letters NSWOCGTVVV75. This stood for New South Wales Options Club Grand Tour Victorian Visit Vintage 75. Len Evans was captain with team members Neville Baker, Tony Albert, John Beeston, James Halliday with Dr Max Lake as honorary team doctor.

The Sydney team, according to local bookmakers, were 5-1 favorites. They were in magnificent physical condition, all 14-stone or more.

The Victorian team was young, obviously nervous and all members were in formal black tie. The captain was Peter Joyce with Joe Sullivan, David Thomas, Peter Walker and John Cunnington. The Test Match took place at Two Faces Restaurant with Herman Schneider as field umpire and master of cermonies.

The Sydney team cleansed their palates before the opening ball with several bottles of Pol Roger. This could have been a mistake. In the classic options style Schneider asked five questions on each wine. He might ask whether it was a European wine or some other? Was it an appellation controllee wine or not? Did it come from the Loire area, Burgundy or some other? Could it be a '66, '67 or '69. Each team member who scored a question right won a point.

Victoria won 129 points to 125. 'My team is magnanimous in defeat', said captain Evans, 'We were beaten on the night by a better team. But the Bulletin did report that his eyes hardened like Cabernet grapes when he pointed out that his team had to contend with four Victorian wines and not one from the Hunter. Then there was an obscure Victorian wine, the Wantirna South 72 red, Melbourne's only suburban vineyard made by solicitor Reg Egan. 'You wait until you come to Sydney', said Evans. 'We'll spring a Dubbo Cabernet, a Back O'Burke Shiraz, a Colloroy Chardonnay'.

It didn't take more than six weeks to recover from this defeat. There were other matters to reflect upon. Len Evans has written six books including the vast classic The Australian and New Zealand Complete Book of Wine. He and his wife Trish have had three children, Sally, Jody and Toby. Toby was born on the fourth of July, 1966 and Len wrote: 'Well, I should have a son more often. Toby... was wet-headed with some of the best wines I've seen. I had to make a quick trip to the Hunter the following day. Doug Elliott offered a 1945 red, a 1936 white, and a 1928 Madeira from the area, a rare Hunter dessert wine. They were all marvellous and Toby was toasted in glowing terms. That night Keith Tulloch produced Dry Reds of the 1958, '57, '55, '54, '52, '47 and '31 vintages. The '54 was the famous wine that won both a burgundy and a claret prize in the same show'.

Of course, young Toby received none of this, yet by 1977 he was making progress. Soon after his eleventh birthday he was confirmed and celebrated his first Communion. When he returned from Church the conversation went like this:

'Dad, you go up to the altar and you eat the flesh and drink the blood of Christ'.

'Uh-huh, that's right'.

'But it's not really flesh — they give you bits of wafer and instead of blood they give you wine'.

'Mmmm, how did you find that?' asked the senior wine judge.

'Aw, the wine wasn't bad, but do you know, I couldn't pick it'.

Over a glass of Krug during his 1977 staff Christmas party Evans announced the formation of Len Evans Vineyards Pty. Ltd. He and his partner barrister Peter Fox bought 20 acres under vine in Coonawarra South Australia plus a small vineyard, Chateau Rahaul in Graves, France. They had planned to buy Suduiraut, the great Sauterne Chateau but their bid fell short by four or five million dollars.

However, Rahoul cost just over half a million and he announced a plan to acquire three or four French vineyards including one in Burgundy, and maybe others in Clare, South Australia and on the Margaret River in New South Wales.

The Evans philosophy of not wasting a minute continues. It even includes what he calls the Evans Law of capability. Once upon a time he believed that just occasionally he should drink an inferior, even a flagon wine, just so that he could appreciate the great wines when they arrived. Now he has discarded that.

In 1976 with an air of infinite sadness he made this pronouncement: 'We are all moving on in years. Do you realise that I may only drink another 8,000 bottles, so they all have to be good ones. So I am determined to drink as many great wines as I can. Under the law of capability there may be only 2,500 succulent steaks left to me.

And looking even sadder his voice sinking: 'I might make love only another 5,000 times'.

It would be foolish to speculate on his other activities but his bottle situation would appear to be well in hand. He has at least 40,000 from which to choose.
