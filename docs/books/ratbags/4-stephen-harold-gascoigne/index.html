Stephen Harold Gascoigne

11 min read.

First published 1980

The Barracker of the nineteen-seventies tended to be a bore. He lacked wit, style and, above all, knowledge. Often his main form of self expression was to run naked across the wicket. The ultimate in tedium has been to listen to the character in shorts and thongs, can in right hand, droning interminably AVA GO.

There have been occasions. There was the very first Test Match in Perth. Colin Cowdrey was batting in a particularly somnolent style, when suddenly he called for a new bat. 'What's wrong with the old one, Cowdrey?' inquired a critic, 'white ants!'

Then there was a New Year's Day at the Melbourne Cricket Ground which produced a lone, stirring cry: 'The only thing you Poms can do better than us is play God Save the Queen.'

Trevor Bailey also was capable of evoking strong comment. There was the occasion in Brisbane in 1958 when he didn't score for 48 minutes and a barracker commented. 'Look, he's got a bonzer bat worth about seven quid. Ain't it a shame he don't use it.'

Yet the best cries, the best wit, always seemed to come from the Paddington Hill at the Sydney Cricket Ground. Back in the old days 'Paddo' was not the trendy area for the $100,000 a year artists and advertising account executives, it was working class and so too was the Hill at the SCG.

The cricket enthusiast sat on the hard ground, grass if he was lucky, roared in fury at every interruption to the game such as appeals against the light and the diet was always bottles of beer, meat pies and tomato sauce. One brewery admirably recognised its responsibilities when it printed on its can pictures of the Sydney Cricket Ground.

There have been some famous cries from the Hill, such as the day when the restless mob, tired of watching E.S. Wyatt when they wanted the super star Wally Hammond, gave the shout:

'Come on Wyatt. Get out will you? We've seen all your strokes except one, and it'll be a good job when you get that — sunstroke.'

Or there was the cry which was never precisely identified. The slow bowler had just delivered a disastrously expensive over when the Hill offered some helpful advice:

'You're all right Bill, you've got him in two minds. He doesn't know whether to hit you for four or six.

Yet the Hill really owes its reputation to Stephen Harold Gascoigne, better known as Yabba. If Donald Bradman was the ultimate in batsmen then Yabba deserves some credit for being the ultimate in watchers. Nobody has ever explained conclusively how he won the name Yabba. He was never called anything else; the impressive, aristocratic line up Stephen Harold Gascoigne was something he preferred to keep quite. You never want to appear too uppity around Redfern and Glebe.

He was born in Redfern in 1871. His mother was a Sydney girl and his father was a storekeeper who had migrated from Oxford in England. In his twenties Yabba was a soldier in South Africa fighting in the Boer War. The combination of Redfern and the Army was almost the perfect education for a barracker.

But he was to give it further splendid post graduate treatment. When he returned to Australia he bought a two-wheel cart, a horse, and became a rabbit hawker around the streets, of Balmain, Rozelle, Annandale, Leichhardt and Glebe.

There were no such devices as C.B. radio, or loud hailers, so Yabba was forced to rely on his gravelly voice which had all the penetrating power of the fog horn of the Queen Mary. He would cry 'Rabbo... Wild Rabbo'. As he did this the result was quite magical, both housewives and cats appeared all along the street.

His contemporaries claimed that his rabbit skinning skills were something to behold. Even the awesome Jack Gregory in full bowling flight was barely as swift. The housewife would get the rabbit, the marauding cats would get the giblets.

Some say the name Yabba derived from the incomprehensible howl of 'Rabbo' which could sound like Rabba, Yabba, or anything you like to name. Others say it obviously came from Yabber, a good old Australian term meaning to run on like a drain. The expert on language, Sidney J. Baker says Yabber was once a term used to describe speed in the shearing shed. So maybe the Yabba was for the gun rabbit skinner.

Yabba was an identity wherever he went. On the Hill he held court as a celebrity, someone who was instantly recognisable. He was 15 stone, and nearly six feet tall. He had his hair so close cropped he might have been an old time wrestler and he wore a cloth cap. Everything else was very plain, dark trousers, white shirt, sweat rag round his neck, but sometimes he wore a white jacket so that he could almost have been mistaken for an umpire. His position was by the scoreboard and his equipment was a basket which contained his lunch and two bottles of beer.

There are a number of Yabba classics that have gone around the world until now they belong to the cricket language. The merciless cry for the dropped catch 'gitter bag' was all his. Another which always sounded pure musical hall originated with Yabba: 'Gawd, Give Me Strength and a Walkin' stick.'

He originated the 'ferrets', the term for the last two batsmen. The story goes that it came up for the first time when the last man in the batting order was walking to the wicket.

'You'll do me mate. You're the ferret that goes in after the rabbits.'

Also he was very proud of his discovery Johnny Won't Hit Today, a play on the initials of England's captain, J.W.H.T. Douglas. Douglas was a patient, dogged and shockingly dull batsman. He used to sit for hours on the hill behind the sight screen using binoculars, trying to work out the googlies of Arthur Mailey.

He was so bad against Mailey he became a joke. It was always 'Johnny Won't Hit Today' and as J.W.H.T. went out to the wicket Yabba shouted to Warwick Armstrong 'Put Arthur on and don't waste time. Poor old Johnny is wanted on the telephone.'

Yabba had a number of advantages, a strong voice, a good knowledge of cricket and a showman's sense of timing. One of his best efforts was with Maurice Tate, who like most fast bowlers suffered terribly from sore feet. Now Tate's feet were of Brondignagian proportions. They were size 13 and during a heavy bowling session he signalled to the twelfth man to bring out another pair of boots. It was an interminable operation and Tate sat on the pitch for what seemed five minutes squeezing his feet into the new pair.

'Thank goodness he's not a bloody centipede,' cried Yabba.

The English captain Percy Chapman had feet not quite up to the majestic proportions of Tate, they were a mere size eleven, but as soon as he took block Yabba remarked: 'The Feet's In.'

Of course, the management at the Sydney Cricket Ground should have put Yabba on the payroll. He was particularly useful during those dull-eye lid drooping moments when the crowd yearned for something to happen.

During the 1924-25 series Hobbs and Sutcliffe seemed to set like cement. This was the greatest opening pair of all time. There were other matches when they did better but during the Sydney Test they were moving steadily towards an opening total of 157. Collins tried almost everybody except the wicket-keeper to get them out.

Finally in an act of desperation he brought out on the last of his bowlers. The Sydney Cricket Ground was pin-drop quiet until the bellow of Yabba:

'It's no use skipper, you'll have to get the fire brigade to get 'em out.'

He had his favorites and he had his enemies. He didn't care much for Douglas Jardine. He didn't go for the public school type and along with the crowd he mocked at Jardine's stiff-legged running in the outfield. He suspected also that Jardine had no sense of humor and that made the man doubly vulnerable. So when Jardine ran past the boundary fence he put on a mock upper crust accent: 'Mind your stays, old man!'

On the other hand he was fond of the Australian fast bowler Tibby Cotter. He would shout encouragement 'Give him one on the big toe, Tibby.' Tibby didn't need any urging. A young man came in, his first big match and the demolition was almost too quick. A yorker flattened his middle stump. Then as the young man made his long, long walk back Yabba passed the comment:

'Don't worry son, with that ball he woulda bowled even me.'

He was also most impressed with Charlie Macartney. Yabba always called him the Governor-General, not because he was vice-regal but rather because of the superior even Lordly way in which he handled the bowling.

When he came in to bat he used to announce to everybody on the Hill: 'Stand up on yer legs you coves. 'Ere comes the Governor-General.'

And by heavens they did.

The name Governor-General became so popular the story goes that a Rolls-Royce was passing Waverley Oval. The gentleman inside was wearing a top hat and morning suit. There was such a large crowd he said to his chauffeur: 'What's on there today, James?'

The chauffeur was very brave. 'They tell me the Governor-General is playing there today, Sir.'

Thereupon the Governor-General, Lord Stonehaven, suggested that they pull up. 'Let's go and see my counterpart,' said he.

Yabba did his homework. He always knew the occupations of everybody out there in the middle. For example he knew that Umpire George Borwick was a meter reader for the Australian Gas Light Co. The First Test match in that bodyline series of 1932-33 was at the Sydney Cricket Ground and the Englishmen put together a frightening total including 194 from Sutcliffe, 112 from Hammond and 102 from the Nawab of Pataudi.

The situation could hardly be described as serious, but Pataudi was desperate to get that century and he was motionless in the eighties for what seemed an eternity. It was Yabba who got him moving again. He called out: 'Put a penny in his meter, George, he's stopped registering.'

Then there was the Australian wicket-keeper, Hanson Carter, who was an undertaker, a natural for Yabba. The time would have been the First Test Match at Sydney in 1911. In England's second innings Jack Hobbs was bowled Tibby Cotter, caught Carter for 22.

Yabba gave the comment: 'You can take the body away now, Hanson.'

There was a noble feeling of mutual respect between Jack Hobbs and Yabba. Hobbs last visit to Australia was in 1929. The team on the Hill knew he was retiring and they loved him so much they passed the hat around to buy him a present, a boomerang.

Hobbs walked around the ground waving the boomerang. He stopped at the Hill and asked Yabba to come down to the fence. They swopped a few words, shook hands, and the enthusiastic mob cheered both heroes.

He understood what was going on in the middle. If the runs were coming too easily on an easy wicket, he would say 'I could bat out there with a walking stick.' Or if he didn't like the bowling: 'Your length is lousy but you bowl a good width.'

He used to refer to himself as 'The One and Only' and the One and Only spent countless hours on the Hill never getting into any trouble. In 1940 he was in an Annandale bar when a fellow drinker knocked over his beer. He provided the bar with some of the choicer phrases which he learned during the Boer War. This time a police constable was in the audience and he was arrested for having used bad language.

The Court fined him £1 or 48 hours in jail. Yabba decided that he would prefer to go to jail, but this was his area. He was so well known that his friends had paid the fine within an hour.

Yabba commented later: 'It was the longest hour of me life. Also the quietest. When the juice went round that the One and Only was in the clink, some of the boys had a zack in the tit-for-tat to get me out.'

The One and Only died in January 1942. Delegates to a meeting of the NSW Cricket Association stood in silence to his memory. In 1961 there was a move to put a brass plate on the Hill to his memory, but nothing came of it. He will be remembered in other ways.

One story in particular will never be forgotten. There was a great to-do one day over the moving of the sightscreen. Umpire George Borwick held his arm straight up aloft to indicate exactly where he wanted it. Yabba put on a little boy's voice:

'It's no use umpire. You'll have to wait till playtime like the rest of us.'
