Alfred Edward Lynch

13 min read.

First published 1980

Alfred Edward Lynch was not lacking even slightly in self confidence. He thought himself supreme both as a thinker and a being totally in command of his own physical resources. His literary output was prodigious ranging from poetry to mathematics to philosophy, but he considered that his finest work was Psychology and Ethics.

His publisher asked him the delicate question, how did he expect it to pay. Lynch replied that never once did he consider the book in such simple terms. The writing of it gave him a peculiar pleasure because it was necessary to the world. Indeed he announced that Psychology and Ethics was 'destined to be a lamp to the feet of the world when the British Empire itself is forgotten'.

He was born in 1861 at Smythesdale just out of Ballarat and his early references for rebellion were excellent. He was the fourth son of John Lynch, a civil engineer and mining surveyor, a native of Tiermaclane, County Clare, Ireland. He had fought at the Eureka Stockade on the side of the rebels. His mother was Isabella McGregor and she came from Perth, Scotland.

As a child he was not over healthy, but he had a passion for succeeding in everything. He was impressed by the physical skills of his old grandfather, who even at the age of 60 could still jump through his hands. Young Arthur Lynch learned to do this and practised it every day.

He wanted to be a Spartan, an athlete in the tradition of the ancient Greeks, and by hard work he developed considerable skills as a runner. On one occasion he defeated several famous runners. 'I had phenomenal eyesight', he wrote, and he was actually able to see the starter's index finger moving on the trigger. Lynch was away even before the explosion.

He went to school in Ballarat and then on to Melbourne University where he took a degree in civil engineering and became a Master of Arts. He taught mathematics and worked as an engineer with the Victorian Railways, but not for long. He was too restless and when he was 27 he went off for a year to study physics and psychology at the University of Berlin.

Next he tried journalism and after some success as a freelance Lord Northcliffe gave him a job on the Evening News. He wrote a column called 'The Seamy Side', in which he extracted some of the more exotic tales from the police courts. One time in 1895 he said to a fellow journalist 'Look after the Seamy Side like a good fellow. I want to go out rather particularly.'

It was an hour and a half before he returned and he apologised profusely for being so late. 'But you must excuse me', he said, 'for I have just been and got married.' And so he had. He married Annie Powell, the daughter of a clergyman.

He found life unbearable if he were not where things were happening. He went off as war correspondent for the Evening News in the Ashanti campaign and for three years he was Paris correspondent for the Daily Mail. But the definitive act, the one that was to make him notorious was his adventure in the Boer War. At the outbreak of the Boer War he went to South Africa as a correspondent for a Paris newspaper Le Journal.

On January 15, 1900 from his hotel in Pretoria he wrote a letter to Colonel Villebois Mareuil announcing his sympathies with the South African cause and three days later he signed a declaration of his willingness to take up arms and made a declaration of allegiance to the South African Republic.

His Irish background made any sort of rebellion most attractive, but he genuinely admired the South Africans for their toughness, their manliness and their skill with horses. When he was in Pretoria he met the South African commander, General Botha, and he was immensely impressed. 'He was a great figure at the time. A man in the prime of life, tall, broad and deep-chested with all that air of health and a great reserve of strength which I found to be characteristic of my new friends. When he offered his hand that hand seemed to be heavy and strong as the paw of a lion'.

His real motives for joining the Boers were subtle. He believed that life should be lived to the utmost degree and that one should make the most of every attribute. He believed too that he possessed one of the great minds of the age and that the thinking man could be equal to or superior to the men of action.

'I was resolved to show,' he wrote, 'that I, a thinker, could ride with the boldest horsemen in the world and pitted with the "men of action", would set them a pace which would test their nerve and call forth their every ounce of energy.'

Lynch trained and fought with the Second Irish Brigade, which wasn't precisely Irish. It contained representatives of every nation in Europe. Lynch claimed that there was only one nationality missing and that was Turkey. Just to complete the polygot picture he set out to find a Turk, but here for once, he failed.

Later in Court there was much discussion about the validity of his rank of Colonel, but Lynch had no personal queries about it. At his first meeting with General Kruger there was a discussion as to who would be the Brigade leader.

'Have you ever commanded men in battle? asked Kruger.

'No'

'Do you think you can command men at the front?' he continued.

And Lynch replied, 'Of that I have no doubt. I will lead this troop all right.'

After that point was settled Kruger wanted to know what rank he would require.

His immediate reply was 'Colonel'. This irritated Kruger, because here was a man who had never once possessed a command and now he wanted to be a colonel.

But he pressed the point: 'It is necessary for success to give me that title. Please make out the commission as Colonel.' Thereupon Kruger signed the papers and Lynch became the first instant colonel.

In a few short months he saw a great deal of action and he kept control over his men with his own ruthless brand of discipline. In his biography he told the story of a Dutchman who among many other offences was guilty of desertion.

'This same fellow had the impudence to return to the Brigade, not to fight, but to defy me... and to break up the force... I knew that I was being tried in the balance and that if this fellow got the laugh on me that morning I might as well disband the troop.'

'He was about three stone heavier than myself, but at all risks I did the one thing on which he had never counted. When he came up I first took his rifle, then after questioning him and provoking an insolent answer, I told him to put up his fists. He looked astonished, but without giving him too much time for speculation, I shot out a blow to the point that stretched him on his back; and then picking up a short stick, I flogged him with my own hands until even the men standing around begged for mercy. I then took out my watch and my revolver and gave him thirty seconds to be out of range, warning him that I would shoot him if I ever saw him again. He lay still for two or three seconds, got up slowly, and then ran like a hare'.

Frequently he held his troop together threatening to shoot his own men. The rumor even got around that already he had shot fifteen. One Irishman tried to run to freedom. Lynch put him under arrest, drew a circle around him and told his troopers that if he made one move outside that circle without being ordered to do so, they were to shoot him on the spot.

He claims that discipline in his troop was as good as any in Africa and he was one of the few officers who insisted on the salute.

He was disillusioned when the Boers would not accept his ideas. One plan was for Kruger to give him a specially selected force of 2000 men, then to make a brilliant dash to Cape Town and in a lightning coup take over the Government, set up a United States of South Africa. On a great cry of principle they would appeal to the world and in particular the United States of America.

He thought this was their one chance in a hundred of survival. Finally as the Boer forces were forced to flee, Lynch escaped to Lorenzo Marques, caught a ship and fled back to Paris via the United States.

Back in 1892 he had stood unsuccessfully for parliament as a candidate for Galway City. His rebellious activities had not gone unnoticed back in Galway so he tried again at a by-election in November, 1901. He had to do this in absentia from Paris, but he won.

Lynch was a fatalist. He had a mystic belief that a force above guided his activities, that he would survive no matter what perilous situation he entered. So he made no attempt to hide his entry to Britain. He announced precisely when he would cross the Channel and as he stepped off the boat at Dover he uttered to himself some lines from Keats:

He ne'er is crowned with immortality
Who fears to follow where airy voices lead.

Two officers from Scotland Yard were waiting for him and immediately they placed him under arrest. Lynch always claimed that this was an outrage. He said that Botha and told him that he would not have signed the Peace Treaty but for assurances both from Lord Roberts and Kitchener that there would be no treason prosecutions. He claimed that if he had been tried anywhere he should have been tried in Australia. He said it was not Alfred Lynch who should have been tried but the British Government for its 'villainous' attacks on the liberties of a republic.

He had to wait in prison for eight months before he came to trial before a jury at the Old Bailey. There were three judges on the bench which included the Lord Chief Justice of England and the Senior Puisne Judge. It was an extraordinary case because there had been no trial for treason in England for more than sixty years, and the last man to be tried had been a lunatic. Furthermore the law under which he was tried was brought down 600 years before in the reign of Edward III and theoretically he could have been hanged, drawn and quartered for the offence.

Mr Shee, counsel for the defence, made no attempt to hide the fact that Lynch had fought for the Boers. He argued that Lynch was an Australian, that Australia was not formally in a state of war with South Africa and he did indeed become a naturalised subject of South Africa. He argued too that he was a war correspondent and here was the one opportunity to write about the war from the South African side.

The prosecution replied, why Australia? — nowhere on earth could a man get a fairer trial than in England. As for naturalisation any person who wished to turn against their country could adopt the ruse of naturalisation. Then the innocent journalist if he wished to write should not take rank and carry incriminating papers.

It was now January 1903, three years after the treasonable events had taken place and Mr Shee made the appeal that for this reason it was absurd for the trial to take place at all.

The learned judges did not agree. The jury took only 26 minutes to find Lynch guilty. The judges put on their black caps and Mr Justice Wills, the Senior Puisne Judge, pronounced judgement.

It was an extraordinary address. Even the staid Times reported that there was 'evident emotion'.

Here are some of his lines from a very long address: 'What was your action in the darkest hour of your country's fortunes, when she was engaged in the deadly struggle from which she had just emerged? You joined the ranks of your country's foes. Born in Australia a land which has nobly shown its devotion to its parent country, you have indeed taken a different course... you have fought against your country not with it. You have sought, as far as you could, to dethrone Great Britain from her place among the nations, you make her name a byword and a reproach, a synonym for weakness and irresolution. Nor can I forget that you have shed the blood, or done the best to shed the blood of your countrymen... How many wives have been made widows, how many children orphans, by what you have and those who acted under your command have done, heaven only knows. You thought it safe at that dark hour of the Empire's fate when Ladysmith, when Kimberley, when Mafeking, were in the very jaws of deadly perils — you thought it safe, no doubt, to lift the parricidal hand against your country.

'And against what a sovereign and what a country did you lift your hand. A sovereign, the best loved, and most deeply honoured of all the long line of English kings and queens, and whose lamented death was called back to my remembrance only yesterday as a fresh sorrow to many an English household.

After continuing on the line that how could any man raise his hand against a country which was the very fountain of liberty, freedom and decency, he gave formal judgement...

'The sentence of this Court — and it is pronounced in regard to each count of the indictment — is that you be taken hence to the place from which you came and from thence to a place of execution there to be hanged by the neck until you are dead.'

Lynch was not over disturbed by this dramatic affair. He wrote that Mr Justice Wills' 'little frame was trembling with excitement' and he could hardly refrain from laughing at the line when he threw up his hands and spoke of the injury to the Queen. Nor, he said, did the death sentence worry him.

'I was worked up to a point at which on that night I could have gone joyously to the scaffold, feeling that thereby I had set the seal on that resolution which had carried me through the whole campaign, and that I had thereby wiped away the record of all that had ever been weak in my composition.'

In Brixton Prison he read all the library books, Milton, Shakespeare, works on physiology and astronomy and he set out the lines for the Principles of Psychology which he hoped would provide a gate that the thought of the world would pass.

But he was in jail only a year. First the Balfour Government commuted his sentence to life imprisonment, next it released him on licence and in 1907 it gave him a free pardon. Nobody was more surprised than Lynch himself.

His extraordinary career continued. Already a master of arts, an engineer, a scientist, a novelist, a philosopher, he studied medicine in London and qualified at St. Mary's Hospital, London.

In 1909 he stood as Nationalist candidate for West Clare and took his seat in the House of Commons.

It was impossible to predict the actions or thought of this man. When the Great War commenced in 1914, instead of opposing the British he took the opposite line — it was the duty of the Irish to join with the British and French to oppose this invasion. Constantly in the House he called for a more vigorous prosecution of the war against Germany. As a British Army colonel, the man who had been convicted as a traitor, he went on a recruiting campaign to Ireland. Some of his old Irish friends threatened to shoot him and at several meetings in Dublin he was in danger of being killed.

In 1918 he left Parliament and settled down practising medicine and writing books. His arrogant courage never left him. Two years before he died in 1934 he produced a learned volume, packed with mathematical symbols, in which he set out to disprove Einstein's Theory of Relativity.
