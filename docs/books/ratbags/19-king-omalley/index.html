King O'Malley

26 min read.

First published 1980

Profile of King O'Malley, the flamboyant American-born Federal MP whose fruit-cake oratory drove the founding of Canberra and the Commonwealth Bank, and who lived to 99 still defending his claim as the bank's true founder.

THE MOST MARVELLOUS thing about King O'Malley was his language. It was like old-fashioned Christmas cake, so loaded with rich ingredients there was no room left for flour. He would open a political meeting like this:

'Brothers, sisters, fellow Christians. I don't see any profiteers, racketeers, boodleiers, or gilt-spurred Brahma-pootra roosters in this meeting'.

In the O'Malley Christmas cake language good people were Brothers or Fellow Christians and the villains were roosters, pronounced with a sonorous rolling of the Rs- R-r-roosterrrrs.

Or how about this for another example, his opinion of the Argus newspaper: 'Some anthropological professor would say that the Argus was a sort of petrified corpse of Conservatism but I don't. I simply say it is an embalmed relic or embodiment of Conservative, antidiluvian, fossilized mummydom, masquerading in the pious garb of up-to-date hypocrisy. Democracy is to that Tory paper, as a bull ant is to a fat man at a picnic, anathema'.

The descriptions of him that have come down through history have a nice variety. He has been likened to a Wild West romantic hero, spruiker from Barnum's circus, an old time Southern Senator, a Mississippi gambler with all the eloquence of a snake oil salesman or a revivalist preacher.

There is no question he did look awfully good. He wore a frock coat either of dark blue or brown, a low cut waistcoat to show off his pleated shirt and large tie or cravate. Then in the centre of his tie display there would be an opal the size of a walnut encircled by diamonds. Melbourne Punch claimed that if you tapped his hip pocket, likely as not, you would find a pistol there. He liked the Western touch. Just to complete the beautiful picture he was five feet eleven inches tall, he had a face enveloping beard and a lion's mane of hair. Yet it was the hats that one remembered, the real wide-brimmed 10-gallon affairs. When asked why he wore them he said the wide brim kept his eyes from being offended by the sight of the tall and atrocious structures erected in our modern cities.

King O'Malley was born on 3 July 1854, but as to the precise spot that was always a matter of great speculation. He used to say that he was born in Canada just across the border from the United States. Had he been born five miles south he could have become president of the United States.

But here's the rub. Had he been born five miles south he could not have become a member of a British legislature. In later years he did admit to his biographer, Dorothy Catts that he was really an American and it is interesting that the 1950 edition of Who's Who in Australia gives his birth place as USA.

As a young man his uncle gave him a job in a bank in New York and this is where he gained his passion for banking and insurance. His rise was so rapid soon he was credit manager and began telling his uncle how to run the bank and use modern methods. After several encounters his uncle called him into his office and said there was not room for two of them at the bank and he had no intention of leaving.

So the young King went off on a grand tour of America selling insurance and real estate. At first he was not successful, but then he learned that the world belongs to the stunt man, to the fellow who dares to be different. In Wichita, Kansas, he hired a sign writer and put up a large banner in calico: THE WHOLE EARTH FOR SALE BY KING O'MALLEY — COME INSIDE.

The buyers did come too, it was a great success. In Seattle he splendidly sub-divided and sold a whole estate by doing something similar: THE EARTH FOR SALE BY KING O'MALLEY WHO DOESN'T WANT IT.

Yet his real ambition was to start his own bank and for this he needed money, a great deal of it. He noticed that down in Texas the Government was making financial grants to religious bodies. New religious groups were all the rage throughout the United States and the Government of Texas had found that religious bodies could be used to people the land and establish new communities.

King O'Malley saw no reason why he could not do this. He went to the Pan Handle district and started his own church which had the beautiful name of the Waterlily Rockbound Church — the Red Skin Temple of the Cayuse Nation.

The title was a little complicated because he wanted to attract Indians as well as whites. He based his religion on Christian brotherly love and he adopted the classic approach — sincere thoughts with lots of bright singing, plus the warning that the twin demons, drink and tobacco would be the ruin of us all. At twenty-three he appointed himself Bishop of the new church.

O'Malley was sometimes a little romantic and he could add some fine decorations to a memory, but there is no doubt O'Malley was just the man to start such a church. He claimed that the Waterlily Church needed help, a positive sign, so he called together his disciples and decided to perform a few miracles. One of the disciples, incredibly enough, had the surname of Angel. O'Malley used to send Angel off into the Cadellera Mountains and at the appropriate moment, when Bishop O'Malley was telling his congregation that greed and sin would be their downfall, a great flame burst on the mountain top, a sign from the Almighty.

The church picked up numbers rapidly until O'Malley thought the time was ready to apply for a grant. He needed something extra, a further boost, a great revelation and this would come in the form of sacred tablets on which would be etched, in some ancient language, the rules of the Waterlily Church.

Mr Angel found these tablets, like Moses up in the mountain, and there was great rejoicing but regrettably Mr Angel ceased to be as angelic as had been hoped. O'Malley paid him off to disappear and keep quiet but it did not work. Disaster was revealed when a newspaper arrived with the headlines:

KING O'MALLEY'S ANGEL FOUND DRUNK IN DENVER.

So the Waterlily Rockbound Church very smartly shrivelled and died. Yet it wasn't entirely fruitless. There was a lovely young Waterlily enthusiast Rosy Wilmot, so keen she even played the harmonium during the services. She believed in King and remained a believer despite the Angel exposure. They married and the O'Malleys went off to a bank career in Georgia.

After only three years when she was twenty-three and he was twenty-eight Rosy died of tuberculosis. Rosy was pregnant when she died so disappeared O'Malley's only chance for a child and heir.

He continued to prosper selling insurance for the New York Equitable Life Assurance Society and he had his first taste of politics campaigning for Grover Cleveland in the presidential campaign of 1885. But like Rosy his health began to fail and he was coughing blood. He went to Europe on a health cure, to Germany, Switzerland, Russia, studying banking methods and he tried to regain his health in the warmth of the South of France, but it did not work.

He was in San Francisco in 1888, thin, weak and ill, aged thirty-four, when a doctor gave him six months to live. Then he heard that the place to go was Queensland, to the Tropic of Capricorn, the healthiest place on earth. There was a ship sailing immediately for Rockhampton, so O'Malley decided to catch it.

Even when he was dying he liked to do things with a flourish. He boarded the ship with his own lead-lined coffin. There was much trouble in trying to persuade the captain the merit of this idea. He thought it a very ill omen. However O'Malley was able to point out the tremendous advantages, should he have to be buried at sea. There he had his own ready container.

The indestructible O'Malley did not die. The ship berthed at Port Alma which is sixty-four kilometres downstream on the Fitzroy River. The crew deposited him on the wharf, bags and coffin. What could he do with that bizarre object. Even if he needed it that afternoon he could not carry it, so he left it on the wharf.

Local fishermen suggested that he go to Emu Park and according to Dorothy Catts he stayed there for two years, living in a humpy with local aborigines. They fed him on quail, kangaroo meat and fish, so there out of touch entirely with his old life, slowly he recovered.

When the Aboriginals went walkabout it was time to leave. First he visited Rockhampton, contacted representatives of his old firm, the New York Equitable Life Assurance and then moved south on foot, first to Brisbane, then on down to Sydney.

It did not take him long to re-establish his shock-em-into-action approach. Mrs Catts tells how he called on Anthony Hordern, the number one businessman of Sydney. Mr Hordern told him coldly that he did not have the time to discuss life assurance. 'My time is too valuable to waste".

'I figured on that', said O'Malley. 'Will you allow me to buy five minutes of your time at a pound a minute? I'm sure it's worth all of that'.

O'Malley put his watch down on the table plus five sovereigns. He spent the time telling stories and he did not even mention life assurance. 'Time's up', he said, 'can you spare another five pounds worth?'

Hordern was charmed. He pushed back the money and said: 'Tell me another story then let us hear about this insurance policy'. Of course he bought several thousand pounds worth and when O'Malley left he said: 'If you make me laugh like this it'll keep me alive for a long time and you won't have to pay out on this policy until I've paid for it in premiums".

He worked his way around Australia and in 1893 he settled in Adelaide, eager to get into politics. There had just been an election so he had to wait until 1896 when he stood as an independent for Encounter Bay. His American accent, his 10-gallon hat, his opal tie pin made him unforgettable. As he pointed out many times there is no point in looking like everybody else, you need to be noticed.

He won the seat narrowly, then set about making himself unforgettable in Parliament. He tried fruitlessly to establish a State Bank, but he had more success in getting lavatories in railway carriages, getting legal recognition for illegitimate children and in outlawing barmaids.

His barmaid speech during the second reading of the Licensed Victuallers Bill is one of the best examples of his fruit cake language: 'All Government bills for the regulating and limiting of the drunk traffic are so much waste paper, as long as the barmaid system continues. We might as well try to tame and manage a huge rattlesnake, an Indian cobra, or an Australian rattlesnake without extracting the fangs and destroying the poison pouches as attempt to regulate and limit the drunk traffic while the barmaid system remains in full force, bespattering with mud the golden mantle of the loving moon-eyed goddess of democracy — South Australia. Unfortunately, according to the science of political economy, the beautiful barmaids can only be regarded as the polished fangs of the stagger juice rattlesnake or the attractive medium between the pouches containing the poison and the blear-eyed, swelled-headed, red-nosed victims. By sanction of the law they are angels of mercy luring men to their own destruction'.

Naturally he was not without opposition in this but then he had an answer to his detractors and slanderers. '... They are all the carrion vultures in human shape, the blacklegs, bluelegs, and the redlegs of vulgarity, destruction, vice and violation of law, while I am the embodiment of an immortal principle, and when clad in the armour of truth and justice, one man is more than a match for thousands wandering in error and darkness. The social rats, and the crawling human skunks, and the inhuman polecats have combined with the thieves, pimps and prostitutes of South Australia to finally crush me'.

O'Malley's superb eloquence did not triumph, but it did have a delayed reaction. Come the Licensing Bill of 1908 the Government brought down a bill that there was to be a register of barmaids and only those who had been barmaids for three months could apply. So barmaids like withering flowers slowly disappeared and they did not return until 1967.

The barmaids themselves hardly loved O'Malley for his kindly protection. A group of girls invited him to a hotel for a glass of lemonade. As soon as he arrived they staged an assault and all squirted him with soda siphons. O'Malley commented: 'I felt like a nymph in the fountains of Rome'.

His assault on the social rats, the crawling human skunks and the inhuman polecats did not help him either. He lost the 1899 election to a former Mayor of Adelaide, Charles Tucker, by fourteen votes.

For a time he contemplated returning to the United States, but this was the turn of the century and the birth of the Commonwealth — he saw too many opportunities, and in Tasmania he saw a beautiful chance to get into the first Commonwealth Parliament. There was a State election only three weeks off, so why not try for that? Win or lose he could still switch to a Federal seat immediately after Federation.

He failed in his State bid but he did so well the Tasmanian Government, as he put it, became "excited" and tried every possible angle to keep him out. First they tried to introduce a bill which would allow candidates only if they had been resident in Tasmania for two years. That didn't work, but they had other methods for getting rid of the 'Yankee Bounder', the 'Colorado Kid', 'the Irish Yank', as they called him.

O'Malley said that they divided Tasmania into five electorates and Sir Edward Braddon, the former premier, moved that the bill not be read until six months hence. So Tasmania for its first Federal election was all one electorate in the Senate style. Braddon believed that in an all-State vote the 'Yankee Bounder' wouldn't have a chance.

Of course, while all this was going on O'Malley received so much publicity that he polled second in the State, only a few votes behind Braddon himself.

He made the headlines on the very first day of the sitting of Federal Parliament in Melbourne. He moved a private members bill for pensions for the aged. His idea was a national contributory superannuation scheme which would mean £6 a week to aged married couples. It was an inspired idea, fifty years ahead of its time, but Prime Minister Barton did not enthuse.

His flair for publicity rarely deserted him. Federal members in 1901 started off with a salary of £400 a year, O'Malley complained about this. He said that by the time he had paid living expenses, assisted impecunious constituents, and subscribed to public objects to which a Member of Parliament was expected to subscribe, his savings had reached vanishing point. So to assist him in his poverty he asked the Speaker for permission to erect a tent in the grounds of Parliament House. The Speaker refused, but the newspapers did not refuse to print the story.

The things which moved him to passion were pensions, tobacco, drink, the national capital, an Australia House in London, a trans-continental railway to Perth and the establishment of a Commonwealth Bank.

Much of the time he liked to insult, provoke, provide pure drama. When the Governor-General's speech alluded to a bill on Federal tariffs he called for an attendant to bring him some lighted candles under the guise that he couldn't read his notes. Then he said: 'Yes, Mr Speaker — these lights, these guttering, flickering lights stand for free trade, which now goes out tonight'. And so with a flourish he blew out the candles.

Smoking was the cause of every kind of evil and he voted for an increase in tobacco excise with these words: 'I am thoroughly satisfied that smoking is detrimental to the intelligence of Australians who I notice now are beginning to look sickly, pale and intellectually destitute'.

He would even taunt the Prime Minister about time off for the Melbourne Cup. 'In view of the fact that next Tuesday will be a great National holiday — a day on which all Christians will go to the races and semi-Christians to picnics — will the Prime Minister adjourn the House until next Wednesday at 3 pm.

Prime Minister, Mr Cook was not amused.

The Press conducted a love-hate relationship. Melbourne Punch said of him: 'All over Australia he has become known as the very model of the mountebank politician. Everything about him, his appearance, his speech, his methods, his manners, his dress is odd and fantastic. Nor is this accidental. All his mannerisms, all his oddities are deliberate, studied. None of his poses, no matter how casual it may seem, is unrehearsed. King O'Malley is a political actor, a low comedian on the Parliamentary stage who tries for a laugh always, and tries for it by all kinds of extravagances in action, gesture, voice and dress.

His big flambuoyant personality was a little too much for some of his colleagues. It was J.C. Watson who invited him to join the Labor Party, but when Watson became Prime Minister in 1904 he did not want him in Cabinet. The second time Labor won power was in 1908. Now the solemn, humorless, Andrew Fisher was leader and he thought O'Malley nothing but a buffoon.

However the buffoon was quick enough to outsmart his Prime Minister. He went to Labor Caucus and moved that the Prime Minister should only have the power to allocate the portfolios, it was the Caucus which should select the Cabinet. Well, naturally every backbencher believed he would have a better chance by this method. For a start he could vote for himself. So the O'Malley proposal went through and as he guessed, Caucus elected him to Cabinet. His big hope, his dream, was to become Treasurer. Instead the much annoyed Fisher made him Minister for Home Affairs.

He went back to Tasmania delighted to be a Minister of the Crown. He told a crowd of his electors: "I am supposed to be wiser and look wiser than any man on earth could ever hope to be'.

Someone interjected: 'You'll be right, King'.

'Of course, I will', he replied. 'Haven't I frequently told you that in me you had a £3000 a year man representing you for a few hundreds".

Even if he did not earn £3000 a year he took his job immensely seriously. He introduced a system of a time book which he signed himself at the Ministry diligently at nine o'clock every morning. This infuriated the public servants, many of them retired colonels who had been drifting in at 10 o'clock. One afternoon he arrived at the Ministry at 2 pm and the only member of the staff present was the office boy. O'Malley smartly gave him the honorary rank of Colonel and called him Colonel from them on. The head of the department who was a genuine colonel did not turn up until 2.30 pm. The promotion of the office boy did not go down sweetly with the military gentleman.

The foundation of Canberra was very much his child. In the first Parliament he moved a recommendation that the Government should buy 585 square miles of land from the State of New South Wales for the establishment of Capital Territory.

Oddley enough having done this he mocked the idea of the capital in New South Wales. He felt that they were very comfortable using the Parliament House in Melbourne and he claimed "We old Roosters will never see the new site".

In 1908 the running favorite spot for the capital was Dalgety in the Snowy Mountains. He asked the Prime Minister, Mr Deakin: 'In view of the fact that the Honorable Gentlemen has announced that next week we shall deal with the question of the capital site I wish to know whether he has any photographs or pictures of Dalgety, showing the immortal Snowy River, the heights of Kosciusko and the peaks covered with perpetual snow, so that we can see these features when forming an opinion of the beauties of the situation'.

The Prime Minister replied: 'I don't know whether we can equal the honorable member's vivid descriptions but what we have we shall place at his disposal'.

When the site switched to Canberra in November of the following year he said: 'There is a proposition to establish the capital in a district which is so dry that a crow desiring to put in a week-end vacation would have to carry its water bags".

He then suggested to the House the attractions of Tasmania for the capital. It was well known, he said, that through all history all the brilliant brains and diligent workers came from frigid climates.

When he became Minister for Home Affairs no longer did he pass satirical comments about Canberra. Now it was his responsibility and he was all dedication, passionately working to make the capital a success.

There was astonishing interest in the name but O'Malley was unobliging at Question Time. Not only did he refuse to give information, nor did he give the members the privilege of voting on it. All he would concede was: 'The name I prefer is Shakespeare'. Why he liked Shakespeare is not known, but perhaps it would have suited a capital known for its comedy, tragedy and dramatic productions.

Sir George Reid wanted Pacifica, Andrew Fisher liked Myola, Austin Chapman, Austral; Hugh McMahon, Radiance. Meanwhile the Star in Sydney ran a competition and among the frightful names suggested were Coo-ee Commons, Dulce Domum, Kookaburra, Triumph City, Gone Broke, Yass-Can, Australamooloo, Wattle City, Federalia and Sydmelperbisho a beautiful amalgam of all the States' capitals' names.

On 12 March 1913, King O'Malley handed a gold card case to Lady Denman, wife of the Governor-General. She read out the name from the card inside it: not Coo-ee, Kookaburra or Dulce Domum, it was just dull predictable Canberra, the name that had been there all the time.

O'Malley's greatest claim to a place in history comes from his part in the foundation of the Commonwealth Bank, and like most O'Malley stories it is full of controversy. No one can take away from him this point — it was he who called for it more than anyone else. Every speech he made in the early nineteen hundreds mentioned the need for this bank and it was almost an obsession.

His enthusiasm for the bank made J.C. Watson invite him to join the Labor Party and it was the O'Malley speech at the Brisbane Labor conference of 1908 that made it officially part of the Labor Party platform. The following year when he outlined his plan for the bank in the House of Representatives his speech went for a blockbusting five hours.

The trouble was that he did not get on with the Prime Minister, Andrew Fisher, who was an unsmiling Scot and an ex-miner, the opposite in temperament to O'Malley. Nor did he get on with the Attorney-General W.M. Hughes — they were bitterly jealous of each other. There is a famous story that they disapproved of O'Malley to such a degree they held secret Cabinet meetings without him. Then a senior public servant gave the tip off. He asked O'Malley why he wasn't going to the Cabinet meetings.

'I've never missed one', said O'Malley.

'You may think you haven't but the real meetings are held in the P.M.'s room on Mondays. The ones you go to on Tuesdays are just to confirm the meetings the day before'.

The next Monday O'Malley found a strategic spot in Melbourne's Treasury Gardens, so that he could watch the comings and goings into the Federal building over the road. When all the other ministers had moved in he decided the time was right for his dramatic entry. The story goes that there were some very red faces inside. 'Come in King', said Fisher, 'we're having a cup of tea. Joint us'...

O'Malley was angry. 'It's something more than a cup of tea. It's a Cabinet meeting. You have excluded me from them'. That was the last secret Cabinet meeting.

Yet he had secret meetings of his own. When he felt his plans for the Commonwealth Bank were getting nowhere he formed a group called the 'Torpedo Brigade' and they met at his house, 52 Bridport Street, Albert Park. There they worked over the plans for pushing the new bank from every possible direction. Naturally the private bankers looked upon the plans for a Commonwealth Bank as the most crimson form of Communism. They called for a special conference with Fisher, and O'Malley asked if he could be present. The answer was 'No'. Fisher believed that the Government would be adequately represented by himself and Attorney-General Hughes. After the meeting was over the bankers returned home to Sydney and Brisbane by train and they discussed the successes of their mission in the parlor car.

They did not notice a quiet — even unusually quiet — gentleman sitting there with his face hidden behind a newspaper. It was King O'Malley and he stepped off the train at Seymour very well armed with information for the Torpedo Brigade.

But as for actually founding the bank that is another story. Robin Gollan of the Department of History, Institute of Advanced Studies at the Australian National University, wrote a history of the Commonwealth Bank and a paper titled: King O'Malley: Founder or Fraud. He came very clearly to the conclusion that O'Malley was not the founder.

There is a fascinating story of master intrigue how the ultimate move for the bank was pushed through. The Torpedo Brigade needed only one more vote to conquer the Fisher-Hughes team... Dorothy Katts said in her biography that O'Malley was all frustration. 'It's no use, my Christian Brethren, we're one rooster short, and we can't find one... So near yet so far.'

Then he received a letter from a colleague who was away on leave of absence. He took his signature and forced a proxy vote with his typewriter. 'Worse tricks have been used against me,' said he. 'We must have the bank.' By the time the member returned the bank was on its way and as for the forgery he presumed he must have been on the drink when he signed the proxy, because he couldn't remember it.

Robin Gollan says there were at least five conflicting stories as to how O'Malley pushed Fisher towards the Commonwealth Bank. He says there was a slow movement towards a bank long before O'Malley came on the scene. On 5 October, 1911, the day when O'Malley was supposed to have sprung his coup, Caucus already had before it the text of the Commonwealth Bank Bill. Then according to Kim Beazley, Fisher had committed himself to the establishment of the bank. There was no motion from the floor of the House forced on him and there was no division. The bank was a Cabinet recommendation.

The bank opened its doors for savings bank business on 15 July 1912. Prime Minister Fisher opened the first account, W.M. Hughes the second and King O'Malley had to be content with pass book number three. But it was hardly the great central bank that O'Malley had visualised. It was really just a government bank, repeating the work of the private trading banks. 'O'Malley pasteurised', as one critic put it. O'Malley himself said: 'No brother, they've made her a pawnshop. Instead of backing enterprising businessmen they have adopted a policy of lending money to anyone who can put up substantial collateral security. The private banks used to do that.'

He loved to be associated with grand enterprise. As Minister for Home Affairs he was there to help turn the sod for the start of work on the Transcontinental Railway through to Perth. The occasion was at Port Augusta and the date was 14 September 1912. His speeches had the passion of the old time pulpit and this again was one of his classics: 'Australia is a land where Britain's children have enjoyed unexampled privileges and prosperity, and we may liken it to a great temple of liberty, the keystone of whose strongest arch is universal justice, and the Bill of Rights and our matchless Constitution are its solid foundation. Stone by stone has this temple, grander than the Ephesian dome, been raised through long years of toil and patriotic statesmen, beginning with the organisation of the States and ending with the creation of our magnificent Commonwealth.'

He had two spells as a Minister, in the Fisher Cabinet from 1910 to 1913 and in the Hughes ministry from October 1915 to November 1916. He fell out with Hughes in the great split over conscription then in 1917 to his profound astonishment he lost his seat. However his defeat was not entirely due to conscription. His lively tongue was clever at upsetting Tasmanians. He would refer to 'Tasmanian fossiliferous despotism'. Of Sir Edward Braddon he said here was a man who 'to all intents and purposes died politically many years ago but through the arts of the embalmer had been preserved as a political curio for the delectation of the present generation.'

When Tasmania was divided into electorates for the 1903 election he became the member for Darwin. This was a mixed electorate with many farmers but the real strength came from miners around Zeehan, Queenstown and Mount Bischoff. The miners gave him his Labor support and he didn't care much for the cocky farmers. At an election meeting his audience heckled him so much he shouted: 'There'll soon be a Labor Government in Australia, I'll be in the first Labor Cabinet then I'll get square with you. I'll ring-bark you cockies. I'll put an excise on spuds.'

That was a line which started a brawl and O'Malley had to escape out the backdoor. It was also a line that his opponents never allowed him to forget. In 1917 his opponent was a former Labor man, C.R. Howroyd. O'Malley said: 'A plough horse like Charlie Howroyd will never beat me. He'll kill himself trying.'

The Darwin electorate was not what it used to be. Many of the miners had drifted away into other occupations so the cocky farmers ultimately had their triumph, they ring-barked the King. As for O'Malley's prophecy, it was a little too true, Howroyd did kill himself trying. He collapsed on the night of the election and died the next day.

O'Malley stood again two years later against W.H. 'Laird' Smith, but he failed and it was not even a narrow failure, so he never tried again. He lived in quiet retirement with his wife Amy at the unpretentious little cottage in Bridport Street. It was here that O'Malley lived right from the days of the Torpedo Brigade. Amy's mother was O'Malley's housekeeper and he married Amy not out of any wild fling of romance, but rather it did not look altogether proper for him to live with an unmarried lady under the same roof. The wedding took place on 10 May 1910. He was 55, she was 38, and the ceremony was conducted by the Reverend James B. Ronald, an ex-member of Parliament and a Presbyterian Minister. O'Malley as a Bishop of the old Waterlily Church was not concerned whether it was Catholic, Protestant or anything else.

Life could not have been that easy for Amy — he was the master of the home. He kept a picture of his first wife Rosy on view and he would not allow Amy to smoke in the house. 'I don't want a smoking chimney for a wife,' he said.

Yet he was rich. He had been a good businessman and in the old days he sold his insurance not only to individuals he did it in bulk to business firms, lodges, clubs so that by his retirement he owned more than thirty-six cottages around South Melbourne plus considerable property in the city.

The big event was rent collection on Monday mornings and he would set out on foot with an old friend — it was a great chatty round. Later in life he gave away some of the cottages to his more faithful tenants. So he became a grand, white-haired patriarch. He and Billy Hughes were the last surviving members of the original Parliament. He did curious things like building his own coffin and his other pastime was to defend his bold claim that he was the founder of the Commonwealth Bank.

Billy Hughes hotly denied the O'Malley boast about the bank. He said: 'O'Malley has told this story so often that he has become, as did George IV with his apocryphal story of the part he played at the Battle of Waterloo, to believe that things really did happen as he says.'

O'Malley wasn't beaten there. He replied that Hughes knew less about economics and finance than any blackfellow knew about Euclid, and that the only bank he had ever had anything to do with was the Yarra bank.

He lived to such a vast age people could be excused for thinking he was dead. When Prime Minister J.B. Chifley announced his nationalisation of the banks there were two consecutive items on the national news. Sir Arthur Fadden, then Mr Fadden, said: 'If King O'Malley could hear of this proposal he would turn in his grave.' But O'Malley was still around, an interviewer had been out to his house that day, and he wasn't revolving at all, he thought the nationalisation scheme was wonderful.

He didn't quite make the century. He died at 58 Bridport Street on 20 December 1953, aged 99. Amy who survived him by less than two years commented: 'My old friend is gone. He was a grand old fellow.'
