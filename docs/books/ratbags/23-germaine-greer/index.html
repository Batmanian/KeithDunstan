Germaine Greer

22 min read.

First published 11 Nov 1980

IN 1972 Dr Germaine Greer was the best known Australian in the world. One could go better than that. She was better known than Errol Flynn and only Dame Nellie Melba back in the nineteen twenties could outshine her fame. She had mixed with all the great from Truman Capote to Norman Mailer. She had been interviewed by the BBC, The Times, Life, Time, Newsweek, Washington Post and Playboy went half way round the world to a farm house in Cortona, Italy, just to worship at her feet.

She wrote The Female Eunuch, which was a phenomenal best seller. It made the top best-seller lists in USA and Britain and wherever it appeared copies were unobtainable after the first two weeks. In Australia the paperback edition of 90,000 copies sold in that time and many critics thought it the best book on male-female relationships ever written.

However, 'Germs' as they called her was a little too shocking, a little too overpowering for her own country. Had she done something much easier, like won a gold medal in the Olympic Games, won the Miss Universe competition or raised $300,000 for the Stranded Dogs appeal she would have won an OBE or at the very least an MBE. Not Dr Greer. The best known Australian wasn't even worthy of an entry in Who's Who in Australia.

The reviews of The Female Eunuch were extremely mixed. The most famous was by Thelma Forshaw in the Age. She described the book as 'the orchestrated over-the-back-fence grizzle.' She said: 'Let's take a look at that blood-curdling scream The Female Eunuch, which is based on the curious fancy (theory is too noble a word) that, in the beginning, we were all men, and then some fiend castrated half of us and gave us a ghastly internal bookie's bag called a womb. We were thereafter afflicted with a monthly inconvenience called menstruation and could no longer fornicate at will without that bookie's bag filling itself with a messy nuisance called a baby. This was the rottenest trick ever played on woman and he ought not to be allowed to get away with it.' The review ended with the punchline 'Save us from shaggy Germ O Man.'

On the other hand Sylvia Lawson in the Australian said: 'Both editions of The Female Eunuch have been greeted in Australia with some fantastically myopic, complacent and resentful printed comment. You'd never guess from most of that this essay on women's (and not so incidentally men's and children's) liberation is neither dogmatic nor complacent, neither strident nor paranoic. That it is in fact ranging, exploratory, and questioning; not only galvanised and galvanising, but also compassionate and gentle.'

The letters, too, had a special savagery. Mrs B.S. of Canterbury wrote 'What enormous fun Miss Germaine Greer is having at our expense, laughing all the way to the bank as sales of her book soar.'

Mrs M.J.B. of Hawthorn: 'If over-education produces people like her, thank heaven for little brains.'

G.B. of Kingsdale, NSW: Sir. — In recognition of the work of Germaine Greer in arousing the hitherto moribund emotions and intellects of woman I nominate her for "Man of the Year."'

Germaine Greer has been greeted in Australia with a mixture of shock, horror, revulsion and worshipping admiration, but clearly her country is not ready for her and never is she disposed to linger on Ocker shores longer than is necessary. Yet if she can be attracted for interviews, brought near to a microphone, and particularly, placed on a television screen, she seems to have the power of a nuclear reactor. She stands over six feet tall and stands well — proud and defiant. It is fascinating to go through a pile of interviews. Some have seen in her the remarkable face of Greta Garbo, some Vanessa Redgrave, Anna Magnani, Tallulah Bankhead, Jane Burden, and one writer even said that if you put a shroud around her head you could see the Madonna. But all have noted her sexuality and found her an extremely attractive woman. She is not acceptable in Australia because she sets out to shock and provoke both by her lifestyle and her writings. The Female Eunuch is an extraordinarily sensitive book, packed with erudition, literary allusions, vast research, and not only does it deal with the agony of the misused female, but the haunted, sexually-misunderstood, frustrated male is there also.

But the vigorous Germaine Greer likes to assault wowserdom head on, to tear up Victorian neuroses by describing things exactly as they are. Repeatedly she made venerable Melburnian walls shake, tape recorders rattle in boxes by saying 'fuck' instead of the absurd euphomism, sexual intercourse, or horror of horrors, the Anglo Saxon 'cunt' for the wretched Latinism, vagina. Sometimes she has been gentler to her audiences by saying instead 'a woman's hole.' Her theme always has been to prove that sex, genitalia and such, is only dirty because prurient minds have made it so.

In The Female Eunuch she drove the message home hard. She said: 'We are conceived somewhere between pissing and shitting, and so long as these excretory functions are regarded as intrinsically disgusting, the other one, ejaculation, will also be so regarded.'

She said further: 'They still say "Fuck You" as a venomous insult' they still find cunt the most degrading word outside the dictionary. Cunt-lapping, mother-fucking are words to provoke a sense of outrage. Being forced to play the role of a woman in sexual intercourse is the deepest imaginable humiliation, which is only worsened if the victim finds to his horror he enjoys it.'

She might have added that honest and direct words like cunt and fuck were in the Eighteenth Century English dictionaries. It was the dishonest Victorians who changed the scene.

Germaine Greer was born near the Elwood beach opposite the Silver Lagoon milk bar on 29 January 1939, the oldest child in a conservative middle-class family. Her father was a newspaper advertising executive, but 1939 was a significant year — he went to the war almost as soon as she was born and he was away for six years. He went through the siege of Malta, suffered famine, and when he came up the railway platform on homecoming day he had aged so much they did not recognise him. The marriage broke down through lack of communication yet the parents stayed together. Germaine Greer said: 'You can't leave a returned ex-serviceman, even though he is a total stranger to you. At the same time I don't imagine my father had the guts to leave us.'

The unhappiness of the family comes out in The Female Eunuch. She reports that very early her father decided that life at home was pretty unbearable and he lived more and more of it at his club, only coming home to sleep. This gave her mother more opportunity to tyrannize the children and disenfranchise the father. So she does not advocate the old Church idea the parents should always stick together for the sake of the children no matter what — separation is better rather than the awful atmosphere of bitterness and tension.

Yet the unhappy home life did little harm to her education. There were so many scholarships that virtually from twelve years on she supported herself. She started with a junior scholarship to the Star of the Sea Convent in Gardenvale, Melbourne. She still has allies among the nuns who taught her. Claudia Wright interviewed Sister Raymond, who was with her for four years at the Convent. She said: 'I don't know that anyone would have called Germaine brilliant — a lot of girls can stand out at times. She was exceptionally good and consistently good in the years I had her... I found her a delightful girl in class and she was big in form and mind and had a generous heart. She was very uninhibited and would come out with some very blunt remarks — but that was in discussion and that didn't matter in an art class because she was terribly creative and the more a person is like that the better it is.'

University education was not free to all matriculants in 1957 but Germaine went on to Melbourne University armed with a Commonwealth Scholarship. Beatrice Faust has written affectionately of this period. 'She gave me a china rolling-pin for a kitchen tea present — so that I'd think twice about breaking it over his head, she said. She was good humored enough for that. She bitched with superb fluency. I always felt that she was more taken with the marvellous flow of words and ideas than with the person she used as an inspiration for a vicious monologue: the wit justified the wickedness; perhaps that was because I never gave her grounds to bitch me... Germs, menstruating with monotonous regularity, suffering abortions, pneumonia, not eating, not sleeping, living in a loft off Grattan Street with green slime on the walls in winter; scrounging meals from friends in College where they put the food out three times a day; the only one of us to attempt anything like a salon.'

She graduated from Melbourne University with first class honors, B.A., majoring in English and French literature then she went on to her M.A. in English with first class honors at Sydney University. She was a school teacher for three years and in 1964 she won a Commonwealth Scholarship to attend Newnham College, Cambridge, where she studied to become a Doctor of Philosophy. Her thesis was on the early comedies of Shakespeare.

In 1964 she became a lecturer at Warwick University, teaching English literature, four days a week. But she was like no Ph.D Warwick had ever seen. She did everything. She appeared in a TV series. She went touring with rock musicians. She contributed to a stunningly varied range of magazines from the august Listener and Spectator on one hand through to Oz, Rolling Stone on the other. She was a contributing editor to the shocking sado-pornographic magazine Suck and she was a judge at the Amsterdam Wet Dream Festival. Frequently she had to defend herself over these activities but Germaine Greer is very good at that.

On a BBC talk back programme an interviewer asked her to what extent she considered Suck and a Wet Dream Festival a contribution to human dignity. She said: 'I thought we needed a sex paper which took a revolutionary attitude and didn't simply purvey the stereotyped images of pornography or cheesecake. This proves awfully difficult to do in newsprint — to build up the image of the human body as something erotic when it is presented as it is, not polished and lit from all angles and suspended from here and there and dusted and powdered. Suck still has the dubious distinction of being the only more or less female oriented paper of its kind. It does try to open the straitjacket that seems to afflict erotic literature, which has a pronounced sado-masochistic bias — indeed in this country it has an outrageously sado-masochistic bias.' But she expressed terrible disappointment in the Wet Dream Festival. 'It didn't make us more loving and more tolerant with each other. We got rather fractious and immensely bored with pornography.'

In 1968 she married Paul du Feu, a very athletic, hairy-chested, builders' laborer, five feet ten inches, Welsh born of an Irish mother and English father. Clearly he was a charmer, a good talker, an entertainer. Although he was tiling roofs in London, he was a University graduate, English degree, Kings College. His marriage to Germaine was a three week disaster although it did enable him to pose nude for the centrefold of Cosmopolitan magazine and to write a book about his experiences titled Let's Hear It For the Long-Legged Girls. In this book he said he saw her for the first time walking down the middle of Portobello Road among the market stalls on a Saturday morning. 'She was like a Dodge City gunfighter headed for a shoot out, a strong intelligent face under a halo of brown hair.' He said that over breakfast next morning in a coffee shop he fell profoundly in love with her. The spring sunshine was highlighting 'the extraordinary beauty of her brows and cheekbones and nose as her head tilted and pivoted. At one moment she was a pre-Raphaelite beauty, then a flick of her head transformed her in a wisecracking heroine of a 1930 Hollywood movie.'

Those romantic thoughts were brief. He had been married before and had two sons. He liked the idea of the new arrangement because she would be at Warwick University all the week and they would meet only at week-ends. Their rows started within two hours of the marriage at the Registry Office — soon he was sleeping alone in the bedroom with Germaine elsewhere on the living-room floor. Du Feu said it was a case of two people each wanting to dominate the other. He sued for divorce in July 1973 so that he could marry American negro authoress Miaya Angelou.

The Female Eunuch was first born in England in October 1970 and immediately received all the best accolades. The literary tyro Kenneth Tynan said: 'I think is is much the best women's lib book yet written. She writes not like an underdog refusing to grow up.' The following April it had another triumph in New York. Newsweek called it women's libs most realistic and least anti male manifesto and one could almost feel a Newsweekian ejaculation of relief when it said Miss Greer openly admitted bedding down with men and quoted her with the line: 'I don't think the penis is entirely irrelevant, largely because I like men.' The New York Times Book Review gave its blessing — 'Brilliantly written, quirky and sensible, full of bile and insight.'

She was on the cover of Life, she was on the Dick Cavitt TV show two nights running, she joined the great with an entry in Current Biography, a British TV camera crew followed her all around the United States — so after that what could be left? The ultimate accolade, of course, is Playboy. This is the magazine which has pages and pages of flesh yet yearns to balance its nipples and image with an intellectual flow. Every month there is solid material before the gatefold ranging from an article by Robert Graves to an in-depth interview with J.K. Galbraith. In the issue of January 1972 the subject was Dr Germaine Greer and she was interviewed over thirteen pages by the assistant managing editor Nat Lehrman. He reported that he flew all the way to Rome from the U.S., then drove 140 miles north to find her at a rented farmhouse in Cortona. He felt that he deserved commendation for effort but he received the low temperature greeting: 'I'd planned to sneak out tonight to see a play in Montepulciano. But you would show up on time. Just like a bloody American.'

He did however describe her as intelligent, refreshing, and frequently outrageous, chiefly because she loved to give the answer least expected. She certainly gave the poor man some splendid reader reaction about his own magazine which included such lines as: 'It's not just the centrefold I disapprove of. It's all the other images of women in Playboy... all those bleary faces and those haggard men and those pumped up women in their see-through dresses, with everyone's nipples poking out and those fixed, glittering maniacal smiles on the girls' faces. And I don't like the Vargas cartoon. Or the Femlin in the Party Jokes page. Or the jokes themselves — not to mention the cartoon. They all give the illusion that fifty-year-old men are entitled to fuck fifteen-year-old girls — especially if they are given diamond bracelets — while fifty-year-old women are too repulsive to be seen with — you display your girls as if they were a commodity. Sex ought not to be that.'

The January issue of Playboy caused a small stir in Australia. There were some angry letters particularly over a line which laid down conditions under which she would pose nude for Playboy. Playfully she suggested that she would like to be seen scrambling on a motor cycle or fucking any one of a hundred men from her list. It was a joke but a number of people did not see the humor. Mr Guy Petersilka, a Canberra restaurant owner, wrote a letter to all Federal members of Parliament and enclosed with each one a photo-copy of the offending page. He left them in the Parliament House mail-room for distribution. The Speaker ordered that they be returned but undeterred Mr Petersilka then put them all through the post. One Labor member, Dr Dick Klugman, sent back a telegram asking if anyone had forced Mr Petersilka to buy Playboy.

Germaine Greer arrived back in Australia just before Christmas 1971 and there was a surge of publicity almost as splendid as the coverage she received in London and New York. She applied for full membership in Sydney's Journalists' Club. This was a calculated move because female journalists were only associate members, so just as she expected, the committee rejected her application. Then as part of the tactical ping pong she refused an invitation from the club to be their guest speaker.

Yet she did agree to talk to the National Press Club in Canberra. She told the journalists: 'One reason why I could never live in Australia is that I cannot do without a decent newspaper and there just isn't one. Australian newspapers constantly talk about the way things ought to be and they very seldom address themselves to the way things really are.'

She put her case more spectacularly in an article for the Sunday Review. Originally the article was to be one of a series for the Sunday Australian, but when the time for publication came 'unaccountably' the paper was suffering from a lack of space.

It was not difficult to see why the editor of the Sunday Australian suddenly acquired cold feet. She began the article by saying that not once in a press interview in Australia had she been reported with any fidelity. There was the television interviewer who asked her how she had got on with men since her homecoming.

'I put an end to his interminable question by saying mildly "It sounds as if you are asking if I've been getting any." "Er yes", he continued, quick as a flash "Have-you-er-been getting any?" Well what could I say? "I've got too many mates here — they'd never see me go short."'

A neat exchange, she thought, but it never made the silver screen. Nor did her views on prostitution, birth control, illegitimacy or the media. She said the prudishness of Australian media was balanced only by its extreme impertinence.

'I had the pleasure of seeing one woman journalist discomforted after a particularly gross series of personal questions. She told me that my unfortunate bridegroom was about to appear as a nude gatefold in Cosmopolitan magazine. "Is he posing with or without?", I wondered aloud. "Clothes?" she asked crossly, about to rebuke me for not understanding what I had been already told.

'"No dear," I explained, "an erection." She shot through like a dose of salts and I'm happy to say I got no more of those questions about whether I kept up a dutiful correspondence with my mother.'

She confronted the Methodist preacher, the Reverend Alan Walker on television and she became guest editor of the magazine Pol. The special issue contained, among other things, articles on lesbianism, pack rape, abortion plus a revealing but at time moving history of her love life by Germaine herself. She was brave enough to tell of the first seduction and of an abortion... 'impeccably performed in the proper conditions for £35, by a man who should be a hero but, instead, is regarded as a criminal.'

She told too of the days in London when she was a groupie, fascinated by the rock scene. She explained that originally 'groupie' was not a sneering word. It referred to the girls sought out by the travelling musicians doing the long and inutterably dreary circuit through the motels of middle America.

'At this point,' she said, 'I wrote an article in Oz magazine in which I, as Germaine, interviewed a famous groupie called Dr G, the day-tripper, who was also me. In it I talked about the rock musicians I have known and loved, without mentioning their names, and the life led by people who are caught up in the rock culture. Rock and roll was much sexier than jazz from the outset, and very frank about its sexuality. This really was a liberating influence for us all.' So for years she was a groupie, a matter much made use of by the News of the World, Time and Rolling Stone.

Pol which rarely if ever receives any comment from other journals, received plenty for this issue. Said Tanya Birrell in the Age: 'It's just too contradictory for Greer to be editing a magazine that includes ads for kangaroo fur coats and vaginal deodorants. So, in order to make sense of this chaos, I've decided that she was simply used because of her publicity value and just happened to step in trustingly, to what has turned out to be a rip-off.'

Pol didn't stop there. In the very next issue it had the gatefold picture of Germaine Greer's husband, Paul du Feu, lying back languidly in the nude, looking a very fine piece of masculinity indeed, except that where his genitals might have been there was an area etched out with the message SORRY. It is hard to believe that had Mrs du Feu continued in office that such a mutilated picture would have appeared.

She had a busy time. She appeared in court both in Sydney and Melbourne on behalf of counter culture journals that had been accused of obscenity. Then she had her own little battles with censorship. She made a quick dash to New Zealand, gave a lecture to students at Auckland University then found herself on three charges, two for having used 'indecent' words and the third 'obscene.'

The subtle difference between indecency and obscenity was most interesting. Indecency was an eight letter word and the woman who lodged the complaint told the magistrate she did not know what it meant. However Dr Greer was quick to provide enlightenment. 'It means literally the excrement of a male animal.', she said. As for the four letter Anglo Saxon 'obscenity' she explained: 'I do not consider it to be obscene. The word is considered obscene because it refers to something we disdain.' She was freed on the indecency charges, guilty on the obscenity — $40 or three weeks jail, plus costs. At first she announced she would go to jail rather than pay money. She was opposed to fines because they discriminated between the rich and the poor, but after conferring with friends she changed her mind. 'I haven't three weeks of my life to spare,' so the money was paid.

The next act of censorship was the banning of a Four Corners program on ABC television. Four Corners intended to present an edited version of a public meeting which took place in Sydney Town Hall on 2 March. The Leader of the Opposition, Gough Whitlam, complained that the scrapping of the show cost the ABC $6000.

Two clerics, the Reverends Norman Webb and Bruce Smith were on the platform at the Town hall, plus Dr Colin Clarke of Monash University and Dr Greer. The subject was abortion and this night Dr Greer had plenty of opposition. Yet if television was too faint hearted ABC radio was there and it did broadcast forty-five minutes of Germaine Greer's speech. The Sunday Observer said: 'It's true she used a few phrases which might have disturbed any listener whose mind was firmly locked back in the nineteenth century. She said that some people think "I am some sort of intellectual super-whore" and of her own use of contraceptives she said, "There is the small nuisance of having to say 'Wait a minute dear' and patter off to the bathroom to get the foam — there is all too much fiddling and faddling — and trying to remember where you left the damn thing."' The three thousand in the audience were obviously impressed judging by the cheers, according to the Observer.

On 22 March she left Australia with the message: 'I won't come back at least under the present Government — not even at gunpoint. I'm just fed up with the Australian institutions which have all been going along the same tracks for so long — so complacent, so inefficiently run it's unbelievable.'

She put it more bluntly in the Sunday Review: 'You could say I'm leaving for my health. One more day of Australian newspapers and I'll have a plastic bag instead of a colon. I don't know how the rest of you stand it. You must have bowels of iron and hearts of oak.'

She received hundreds of letters during her visit many of them sufficiently obscene to make a New Zealand magistrate's hair fall out but she was rather taken by the gentle character who presented her with a red rose tied with a satin ribbon, marked 'From me and my mates.'

So she went off to Bangladesh announcing that she planned to expose the story of the 300,000 women who had been raped and dishonored and couldn't go back to their villages. There were some very brief visits back to Australia. She was back home to celebrate the triumphant win of Gough Whitlam in December 1972. She returned again in December 1975 in an attempt to help the Whitlam campaign. Labor officials this time were a little reluctant to engage her services. They thought the brilliant, agile tongue of this female reformer might be counter productive.

Back before the Whitlam Government fell the Australian Film Commission offered Germaine Greer a $100,000 subsidy for six-part television series on human reproduction. It was part of International Women's Year and it was to be filmed in Italy, Iran, India and Northern Nigeria. Twenty-five thousand dollars depended on one segment being filmed on Aboriginal women.

The Australian commented: 'At the risk of being undermining of the trendy Dr Greer in particular, and sexist in general, we suggest the $100,000 could be better spent.' The offer did not survive a change of Government. In May 1976 a spokesman for the Australian Film Commission said Dr Greer had allowed the loan to lapse. Germaine Greer was very disappointed. 'How can they rescind something which was already allocated,' she said. 'We're scheduled to begin filming the pilot show in January'. But that was the end of the production of the series on human reproduction.

Her life now was very different. No longer did she lecture at Warwick University and no longer did her public statements have quite the same explosive edge. She had a large Regency period house in North Kensington, London, a villa set in six hectares of Tuscan Italy and there was the occasional visit to the United States for a lecture tour at the rate of $1600 or more for a two hour lecture. Her long-awaited second book was on the history of women painters.

Christmas 1977 she was in London fighting to have a baby, undergoing gynaecological surgery. Despite some waspish comments from the female columnists on the Daily Express and Daily Mail she said: 'I can't imagine what interest people could have in the state of my tubes.' But she had certainly gone from Australia forever. Her loyal admirer Claudia Wright expressed the thoughts of many, particularly the young, when she said:

'To me she is the Australian woman of the seventies... the prototype Australian woman I have been longing to see, intelligent, gutsy, honest... aware of people and their problems... ready to fight where others are afraid. She is a born performer... she is herself. And she had produced a book that is the best feminist publication so far... a book that is scholarly and witty. Whether you think she is a lib shrew... plain... scruffy... vulgar with her use of four and eight letter words, whether you say she needs lipstick, mascara, spangles and hair pieces to impress you... or identify with you... you won't meet her style again soon.'
