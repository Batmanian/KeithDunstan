Xavier (Alfred Francis) Herbert

22 min read.

First published 1980

XAVIER HERBERT says that from his earliest days he has heard the cry of 'Ratbag'. When he was thirteen he developed a passion for chemistry. Rather than dash round with his mates during the school recess he did chores for the science teacher... RATBAG.

In 1925, now a qualified chemist, he was studying in Melbourne to become a doctor. He dropped that and announced: 'I'd rather earn one pound a week as a writer than ten pounds doing anything else.'

'You're a ratbag boy,' said his father.

Soon it was time to gain experience, so he walked from Cairns to Darwin via the Gulf Country, very hard going in 1927. RATBAG, RATBAG, RATBAG, all the way.

In 1927 as Government pharmacist in Darwin, he decided to learn deepsea diving from the Japanese Pearlers... 'RATBAG' 'RATBAG'.

After World War II his great 300,000 word kaleidoscopic novel Capricornia was a best seller around the world. Dr Evatt gave an autographed copy to President Roosevelt and he wanted Herbert to take a Government job in the U.S.

Instead Herbert dodged all the ballyhoo, bought a blitz buggy and with his wife Sadie drove to Daintree River, exactly 2000 miles north of Sydney where he lived in the wilderness for two and half years. The rumor got about in civilised parts he had become a hermit, living in a cave. 'Ratbag of Ratbags.'

Beatrice Davis, executive editor of Angus & Robertson wrote to him 'You might be a literary genius — but you're a mug.'

'I preferred the title Mug Genius,' said Herbert.

Then the word spread around that the magnum opus on which he had been working for nine years was approaching monster proportions... It couldn't be true that it was longer than War and Peace — 700,000 words.

No it wasn't true, it was considerably longer than that, 820,000 words. A number of reputable publishers, who should have known better, fled in fear when the suggestion was made that they should publish it.

'The man must be a Ratbag.'

In the winter of 1972 I heard that Xavier Herbert was approaching the completion of an immense novel, which was bigger and greater than Capricornia — Poor Fellow My Country. So I flew to Cairns in North Queensland. There nobody seemed to have heard of him. Even though Capricornia had just been re-published his books were not available in any of the book shops. There was just one ancient, battered copy of Capricornia in the Cairns Library. Only the local newspaper knew where he was. He could be found in a little wooden cottage at Redlynch, ten kilometres north of Cairns. The warning was that he was difficult.

First I had to make contact through a friend. Then second I had to make an 8 p.m. appointment with his wife, Sadie. This was the famous Sadie Norden, the Jewish milliner he met in London. Herbert in London was broke and ready to hock his typewriter for £6. It was Sadie who listened fascinated to his stories, talked him into writing Capricornia, and supported him in a garret while he did it.

Sadie was small, white-haired, gentle, with all the wisdom of her race and I felt this was the real test. If I didn't get past her I got nowhere. She was the real protector.

Slowly she relaxed. She pointed out that Xavier was on the very last chapter and he was finding it extremely tortuous. It was terribly important not to interrupt his concentration. He worked to a strange time table. Already he had been in bed for an hour.

'Come and I'll show you,' she said, 'but you must be very quiet.'

We tiptoed outside where she pointed to an ancient galvanised-iron shed flanked by coconut palms. 'He's in there sleeping and that's where he works,' she whispered.

She told me to come back some days later at 11 a.m. and I could talk to him while he was doing his exercises. When I returned to his little wooden house at the appointed time there he was at the door, grey hair, neat clipped moustache, sun-tanned and very erect. He could have been a retired Colonel and but for the sandshoes, bare legs and shorts, yes, he could even have been a Lord Casey.

He called himself 'Old Horrible'. He called his book the Octopus, the tentacles of the Octopus every minute were threatening to throttle him, and he had a non stop gift for producing remarkable revelations.

The first thing I noticed on entering the house was the shattered propeller off a Gypsy Major engine on the wall.

'Yes, that was the result of a smash in a cane field. I was the unofficial chief flying instructor at Cairns. I took up flying as an escape really, as a means of avoiding the Octopus. The crash when it came was almost subconsciously planned. I walked away from it and that was enough to convince me there was no escape any more, no getting out of it.'

We walked on through the house, looking at this and that, including a large television set. 'This is all for Sadie,' he said. 'I don't believe in comforts,' and he took me out to the ancient iron shed I had seen on my previous visit. Inside there was a tool bench, the gadgets of a handyman plus a few spartan luxuries, a chair, a table and a camp stretcher.

'All you can see here can be carted away and placed in the Land Rover. I'm a bushie you see. In the first three or four years of writing I would go out into the desert for three or four weeks at a time. No tobacco, no alcohol, no sex, a celibate. I had my electric plant and my frypan, didn't need anything else. I could concentrate out there. I needed the loneliness and the silence. Sometimes I would work sixteen hours at a stretch.

'No I plan my day on a 24-hour clock and I keep a log of everything I do. I go to bed at 7.30 every night and I set these devices.' He pointed to an odd-looking Heath Robinson gadget. It was a gramophone turntable fitted with studs that gave off a strange rhythmical scraping noise. There was an electric buzzer also which whirred like a tired cicada.

Once this device was in action no longer did he hear the motor cars on the highway or the engine noises from the nearby Redlynch station. 'Yes, they keep down the noises for me,' he continued, 'then I sleep until my body tells me I have had enough. That may be between midnight and 2 a.m. Then I get up and start work.'

His working spot was the deck chair confronted by a low table. So leaning forward he typed on the most venerably ancient Remington portable I have seen. It was one of those with the long pop-up keys. Everything from Capricornia to Poor Fellow My Country has been typed on that.

He bought the typewriter in 1927 and since then, with no such luxury as an overhaul, the unfortunate thing had typed millions of words. I suggested that it should be offered back to Remington as the mechanical marvel of the century.

'I did,' he said, 'but they weren't interested.'

He explained the labour which had kept him on the Octopus since 1965. First he did his notes in longhand, then he typed a first draft, then a second, then a third. 'Everything I write has to be perfect, as good as I can possibly make it,' he said. 'I want to write an Australian classic' and he waved his arms — he's a good arm waver — 'it may not be that, but it has to be to ME. Understand?'

It was then that he revealed the prodigious size of his work. 'Length is not the important thing. I write very tersely. No publisher is going to cut any of this. I will listen to advice, certainly. If it's any good. But if they choose not to publish it, that's their affair. I've done the work, that's what's important to me.'

His contempt for publishers was awesome. They were all theives, rogues, parasites, trying to do the writer down. Critics were worse. 'When Capricornia first appeared in 1934 I remember a review by Brian Penton in the Daily Telegraph. It was headed "For Your Dustbin." In the Autumn 1972 issue of Overland he wrote 'Soldiers Women was well and truly pissed upon by a lot of critics. That did not worry me of course. What stopped me from reading reviews is that for some people pissing on things bigger than themselves is compulsive as for puppy dogs.'

He became more and more eloquent on this theme. 'That's my rule. I don't read anything any more that's written about me, particularly stuff by journalists. You can write what you like but I won't read it. Sadie probably will, but I won't.'

'How many copies of the Octopus do you have?' I said nervously.

'Only one,' he replied.

This is what I feared and the panic hit me immediately. 'What would happen if there was a fire in the shed and it was destroyed?' I asked.

'My God, I'd hang myself,' he said. 'Sadie used to cart if off and put it in a safe deposit at the bank, but that became too inconvenient. I'll show you what I've done.'

He had taken an old gas stove, lined it with fibre glass, so that ingeniously the whole thing was fire proof. On the front of the stove there was still a little temperature gauge. He opened the stove as if he were about to cook the week-end roast of lamb and there was the manuscript on the stove racks, pile after pile of quarto paper, filling the entire space.

'That's my life work, my whole reason for being,' he said. 'Capricornia was just a botch, the work of a boy. I know its faults better than anyone, a book written by an amateur. This is the real book, the one I should have written. You see any author only ever writes one book.'

Talk I have never heard a man talk like him, with such vehemence. Patricia Rolfe once described his style in the Bulletin: 'He is when the sun is hot and the flies are bad, a terrible old windbag. But most of the time he is entirely absorbing. You know that never in your life again will you hear anyone talk in just that way about something — about the aspirations of Australia Felix, about the True Commonwealth, with withering scorn about "colonial crawlers" and the "lousy Aussie", with fading passion about what might have been — and you hang on every word. He is the whole tribal memory, the last of his kind.'

One wondered how he gained this extraordinary sympathy for the Aborigines. 'When I was a boy about seven in Western Australia I saw them,' he said. 'They had come out of the desert and they were ill-clad and starving. Why? Because the white man had taken their land and their water. They were treated as nigger loafers. The railway stations would have "Wanted" notices in a row on the walls. The pictures would always be of black fellers. I didn't understand then but I knew something was terribly wrong. That situation when I was seven is still the same more than sixty years later.'

He told of the days when he was Superintendent of Aborigines in Darwin from 1935 to 1936. 'One time', he said, 'I got so outraged at their treatment I stood in the main street of Darwin and shouted out "White Bastards." That didn't make me very popular.' Ratbag again.

He doesn't believe that any of Australia belongs to the white man and refers to his house as 'the place stolen for me by the War Service Homes Division.'

'You see,' he said, waving his arms again, 'they don't understand the black feller. They think he's a lazy no-hoper. When he goes off and gets drunk they say "Ahhh — there you see. What did I tell you?" But the Aborigine will never work for the white man. When he gets drunk that's his way of getting back.'

The black fellers look upon the Herbert War Service home as a haven. When they get kicked out of the pubs round about or are refused admittance they settle in to the Herbert ménage. There was a pair of ear muffs on the back of the chair by the television set. I asked him why: 'That's to keep out the noise when they drink and have a sing song.'

Gently I steered the talk back to his carefully charted twenty-four hours. He had only two meals daily — a hearty breakfast and a hearty dinner. After starting work at 1 a.m. he finished around 11 a.m. He did some exercises on the back lawn which included some punching of a heavy boxing bag.

Next on the list was to get out the Land Rover and go down the road for several miles through the ripening sugar which stood nine feet high, a lush growth of immense proportions.

He had laid down a mile track and he had three stones, one for each mile that he would run. 'If I don't use stones,' he said, 'I forget how far I have run and I could go on six or seven miles.'

So off he went at a steady jog and when he returned he lay down on a mat. 'Are you noticing how quickly my breathing is returning to normal?' he said. 'That is a sign of fitness. Now this running is very important. It gets the blood moving and it is amazing how it helps the ideas to come. As I run I can plan all the next night's writing.'

It was more than two years before I saw him again. The Octopus had been completed nine months earlier and he was about to leave for Sydney to sign the contract with Collins. The Octopus was all packed into a suitcase. You can imagine the fear now over the manuscript. 'They told me,' he said, 'that it doesn't matter about me if the plane goes down, but make sure there's a parachute on the manuscript.'

There was a change, all the old careful programme had gone. Only that very day had he started his running again, the first time in nine months.

'After I finished the manuscript I was very ill indeed. A complete collapse. After nine years work I suppose it was to be expected. It was the intensity of the thing. When I write the adrenalin starts to move. What I am writing excites me to such an extent that if I look in the mirror I can see that my face is flushed and I actually get tingling in the fingers. If that happens every night for nine years something has to give.'

In Sydney the man who had hidden himself in the North for most of his life, blossomed. He gave a series of provocative interviews. One television crew had the 74-year-old author standing on his head looking at the Sydney Opera House. He explained that this was the way everyone should see it. This, the most beautiful of man's creations, when seen upside down in the early morning light, appeared to be floating in the sky.

The manuscript was a vast pile of 2000 sheets of foolscap and it all had to be re-typed. A typist was put to work on the massive job, type, type, type, fifty pages a session. It was the equivalent of ten normal sized novels.

While the editor of Collins publishing house excitedly digested this pile Herbert lived at a hotel in King Cross and every morning he ran several miles to their office in Clarence Street, Sydney. Yes, fully clothed he ran, then in the early afternoon he stretched out on an office desk and slept for an hour. Very strange behaviour some thought. Ratbag.

Professor Harry Heseltine has described Xavier Herbert as an enigma, one of the most extraordinarily complex writers Australia has produced. On the surface he is the all-consuming extrovert, a man with overwhelming self assurance. Yet underneath there is a humility and a shyness. He revels in the love of things physical, in being the swashbuckling legendary man of action, but his true interests are intellectual and his sensitivities are microthin.

Xavier Herbert was born in Geraldton, West Australia, on May 15, 1901, but as he puts it, his parents neglected to get married. In 1975 Sadie Herbert, as a matter of principle, decided they should apply for the old age pension. The Social Services Department demanded to see his birth certificate. There was no such document. They sent him a form, calling for dates of his schooling, period of military service, names of parents and such.

'I told them the lot,' said Xavier Herbert, 'but it's unbelievably complicated. You see my mother originally named me Alfred Scammell Herbert and as a youth I was always called Alfie, a name I couldn't stand. Seeing I was a bit of a bastard I decided I could call myself anything I liked. Seeing Francis Xavier was the patron saint of Australia I picked on that. And so I was confirmed Francis Xavier Herbert.

'So you see I had a different name at school and I ran away from my first school after a year. What's more, I put my age back so I could get into the Army. I did it again when applying for a driving licence.'

All this left the crazy world of bureaucracy baffled and when Sadie applied for her pension they said they would need proof of Xavier's age. 'How can I give it?' she said, 'when officially he is not there.'

Herbert said that his real name, if taken from his mother, should have been Lacy. Jeremy Delacy was one of the central characters in Poor Fellow My Country. Whimsically Herbert said: 'I have often thought what a lovely name Francis Xavier Fitzherbert would be. I have just realised that I have a perfect right to it. Does not Fitz mean bastard? Am I not a Herbert bastard? And have there not been some famous Fitzes in history, among them Fitzherberts? So herewith I do declare my identity.'

However Who's Who in Australia does not drop the Alfred. It lists him HERBERT (Albert Francis) Xavier, novelist who still works occasionally in a hospital pharmacy.

His father was an engine driver with the Railways and often Xavier was miserably unhappy. In his book Disturbing Element he told how his mother favored another child from an earlier marriage. He was the unwanted addition, the family fool, a liar, a coward and his father would say, 'Yer nothing but a disturbing element.'

His whole life from then reads like a saga, an attempt to prove his toughness and manliness. His range of occupations spreads across prospector, stockrider, deepsea diver, motor cyclist, airman, timber cutter and even journalist.

He says originally he went north because he wanted to escape the falseness of city life. He wanted to find the Australia as it was when the convicts first arrived. So as a young man, fresh from the disaster of medical school, he went to Darwin, population then, 1200, sought out the editor of the local sheet, asked for a job and there on the first day together they drank O.P. rum and lime juice. The editor told him: 'This is the Real Australia, boy. Everybody here's a bastard, knows it, and glories in it. That's being Australian.'

Herbert liked that. It fitted in entirely with his Ethos idea. It made him bold enough to tell the editor that it was his dream to become a novelist. The next day the editor provided him with an outburst: 'The history of the novel is littered with the wrecks of novelists. No matter how successful any novelist ever became he always ended up badly, hideously. The financially unsuccessful ended in poor-houses or jails, the others in dead-houses as suicides, or in mad-houses, or at their writing desks, too tired and beaten to go on. Give it up while you've got a chance, lad... for once you get into it you'll never get out. It's madness. Give it up and I'll make you a first class newspaperman... otherwise, get!'

Herbert says he got. Eventually Capricornia came out of it, but it meant ten years of penury and for this reason whenever anyone mentioned the book he always called it the Bastard Son.

He wandered in the north for four years then arrived in England in 1931 right at the time of the Great Depression. He wrote what he called a tough little book titled Black Velvet. Of course, Black Velvet is the euphemism, the nice name the white man uses for his black sex.

He couldn't find a publisher. After reading it they wouldn't even see him. He forced himself on one such gentleman, a little old fellow in morning clothes: 'How dah you sah, insult us, a firm of our standing, by asking us to publish a book dealing with the relations between white men and black women!

He was down and out, penniless, to the point of selling his typewriter and his benefactress, the one who saved him, was Sadie Norden, who was working as a milliner. 'Don't get the idea,' he said, 'that she was some poor hungry-hearted little creature hard up for love... she was an exceedingly beautiful girl... a Jewess... and with the uncanny astuteness of her race, she saw under my hairy hide.'

Herbert says he was completely Henry Lawson's Bastard from the Bush. 'Would you let a woman keep ya, would you give up work for good?' And the bastard from the bush replied; 'my bloody oath I would.'

Sadie Norden talked him into writing the novel that became Capricornia and it was she to whom it was dedicated.

The discipline he imposed on himself was remarkable. He said; 'towards the end I got the habit of working thirty-two-hour stretches, after which I would sleep a straight sixteen. I found that the best, because my method was to do the first draft in a sort of somnambulistic state. I would do this after I'd been awake for sixteen hours. I would start at midnight and go on till four in the afternoon, when I would retire. Next morning at about eight I would take what I'd drafted the night before and rewrite it, till midnight. For physical exercise I walked on my hands, with my feet on the sloping walls, and did double mickies on the floor. I was afraid to go walking because of the tendency to go to sleep while doing so. One time I did a stretch of thirty-eight hours, with just the two-minute catnap here and there, and slept out a full nineteen hours after it to wake with that lovely feeling of outpouring.

'I wrote it in pencil, so that the hair wore off my right arm and the skin would have worn off too, only I took to wearing a mitten I made from an old sock. Curiously, during the second half of a session, midnight to four p.m., I would write a large hand, and next day rewrite in the tiniest script.'

Herbert returned to Australia and in 1933 he met P.R. Stephensen, who was operating the Endeavour Press and he accepted Capricornia for publication. Everything was ready to go, all 300,000 words were in type when the company went bankrupt and the type was destroyed.

Herbert was so upset he went bush, back to the north where he spent six months living with the Aborigines and a year as Superintendent of Aborigines in Darwin. But Herbert's methods scarcely agreed with the lines laid down by officialdom, he resigned and went mining for tin and tantalite. Broke once more he even took a job laboring on the Darwin waterfront.

Then when he had almost forgotten that he ever wrote the book, Stephensen recovered from his troubles and in Febraury 1937 it was published. On 31 March 1938 it was announced as the winner of the Commonwealth Sesquicentenary Literary Competition. Prize, £250.

Herbert was in Darwin. He wrote to Miles Franklin: 'When the news came I was stunned for a moment; but only for a moment; for I promptly bought a case of beer and called in all the bums and bagmen and Greeks and yeller fellers about, and got well and truly tanked.'

Capricornia sold 60,000 copies, was printed around the world in a dozen languages and continues to be printed as an Australian classic. Herbert suddenly discovered he was famous. 'I was even rich for a while, owned a home with three dunnies, and none of them down the backyard, either, like all others I have had.'

During World War 2 he was in the Army mainly in Northern Australia and Pacific zones. He says he landed in all sorts of trouble through wanting to fight the Japanese his own way. 'When the danger was over' he said, 'in 1943, I began transferring my belligerence to my superiors and I was more or less thrown out of the Army. No-one would have me but British Intelligence, which wanted ratbags to fight the Australian Army. The sore of the Brisbane Line was still raw. I had to flee for my life through calling the C.O. a Military Donkey. Then I got into the A.I.F. by subterfuge.'

After the war he returned with Sadie to the North and eventually they settled at Redlynch, near the beautiful Black Mountain which he came to consider almost his personal property. His writing was sporadic, too sporadic for some of his admirers. He produced Seven Emus in 1959, Larger than Life, 1963, a collection of short stories; Disturbing Element, 1963, an autobiography, and in 1961 there was Soldiers' Women a story which told what happened to women in war when men left them at home.

In between writing he flew aeroplanes, chopped timber and continued his work as a pharmacist. He loved to work as a locum in pharmacies when the owners went off on holidays. Sometimes they did not always appreciate his activities. Xavier Herbert would consider them inefficient and re-arrange their entire pharmacy while they were away.

The magnum opus, the Octopus, Poor Fellow My Country was published in 1975. It cost $20 a copy, contained 1463 pages, weighed over four pounds and was a very impressive volume indeed.

Herbert had reason again to be angry with reviewers, but he scorned them. The standard gag was 'Poor Fellow My Reader' and some admitted openly that they didn't read it. The Melbourne Herald critic after 556 pages gave up and said: 'The ABC is said to have commissioned a 100-part serial from a synopsis of the novel's first 50 pages. Newspaper reviewers have applied for Commonwealth literary grants so that they can hire research assistants to keep track of the characters. A university is offering a Ph D to the first person who claims, hand on heart, that he has read every one of the novel's 850,000 words.'

Yet other critics described it as a masterpiece, the great Australian classic, Professor Harry Heseltine described it as more closely resembling Tolstoy's War and Peace than any other novel ever written in Australia. It sold out its first edition of 14,000 copies in the week and it won the Miles Franklin award, the top literary award in Australia. The University of Queensland and the University of Newcastle both gave him honorary doctorates.

The last big act was to accept the Miles Franklin award. That was not a great success. Big adulatory occasions do not suit Xavier Herbert and the old antipathies came to the surface. He called the chairman 'a silly old fool' and said: 'occasions like this make me sick. I should never have accepted it.'

Over and over again he repeated that this was the end, his life work was done. There would be no more writing. He said: 'Poor Fellow My Country if it is a cry for me is also a cry for the blacks, for Australia. My Australia — not what it has become — but what it should have been.'

He said now he would like to die somewhere in the bush, in the outback. 'I would like to die ceremoniously, like an Aboriginal. I won't let Svenson (the local undertaker) get me. I don't want to lie in a cemetery with all those strangers about. I intend to die in the wilderness somewhere, if I can find any, if it lasts long enough. I'll know when my time has come, and I'll go into a cave or something. Then I'll dynamite the opening. It will be my tomb and no one will find it.'
