Clement Lindley Wragge

10 min read.

First published 11 Nov 1980

Some called himn cyclone Wragge, Inclement Wragge or simply Wet Wragge. The U.S. Navy has received the credit for giving names to cyclones back in World War II, but Clement Wragge was ahead of the Americans by a good half a century. The Americans used female names and placed them in alphabetical order on the theory that, after all, a cyclone was a very female thing, awe-inspiring when aroused and very difficult to chart its future course.

Clement Wragge was different. When running his Queensland Weather Bureau he named his cyclones in honor of dusky maidens of the Pacific, for Xerxes, Hannibal and Blucher and particularly after members of Parliament. The 1902 Parliamentary leaders, Drake, Barton and Alfred Deakin all became cyclones.

It was not always easy with Inclement Wragge to tell whether it was a beautiful compliment or the acme in insults. If a depression was providing good wetting drought breaking rains then the name of a cyclone would be the equivalent of conferring a knighthood, but look out when Cyclone Wragge was angry. In 1902 Wragge announced Cyclone Conroy after Mr A.H. Conroy, the member for Werriwa. The warning went out 'Conroy, looking nasty, is coming along the coast'... 'Conroy black and treacherous is likely to cross the Southern District' and another time Conroy was even in 'a wicked humor'. Naturally Conroy didn't like this and called him an 'advertising scientist'.

Wragge was tall, lean, and he had all the passion about him of the prophet. He hadn't the least interest in mundane things, such as clothes which he considered a waste of time, and he wore huge hobnailed boots, so the approach of Clement Wragge was a majestic affair. You could hear him half a block away. Yet his friends would tell of his intense concentration. It was possible to pass him shoulder to shoulder without even seeing them.

One friend described his clothing as 'reach me downs' and sometimes it was hard to believe this was Wragge the eminent meteorologist. He was always walking, tramping many miles into the mountains. On one excursion for his natural history collections he ran out of glass jars and spirit. So he put his entymological specimens in his bowler hat. This was most satisfactory until he arrived at the local railway station and met a charming lady. Naturally he had to lift his hat. The rainfall of bugs and crawling objects was a surprise to all concerned.

He built a house in the Brisbane suburb of Taringa and he named it 'Capemba', an Aboriginal word meaning 'Place of Water'. That was another of his battles, Aboriginal names instead of spurious imported names.

His study was a work of art filled with scientific instruments, many of them automatic. At the touch of a button he could check the wind strength outside. His bed was a ship's bunk and there was an Aboriginal's skull at his foot. On one of his trips to the Gulf of Carpentaria, he brought back a collection of skeletons and one was apt to strike these in surprising corners around the house. He preserved the sanctity of his study by using it as a nest for his pet carpet snake. It was harmless, but nevertheless it made the study remarkably unattractive to the children.

Apart from native shields, spears and such, Wragge decorated his house everywhere with Union Jacks and Arms of the Empire, he was a devoted loyalist.

It was the garden that was famous, as local journalists described it 'a lush Garden of Eden created out of a wasteland'. Wragge had been known to work there, digging with pick and shovel until 3 a.m. on moonlight nights. Every part of that garden was named, but this time not Aboriginal. He had the Via Amatoria over Ovid's Bridge. He had the Gate of Adonis, the Bath of Venus, Diana's Basin and various features named after Hector, Aeneas, Ulysses, King Priam and all the various muses of Homer and Virgil.

Clement Wragge was born at Stourbridge, Worcester, England on 18 September, 1852. His father was a solicitor, but both his parents died before he was five and he was brought up by his grandmother. She stirred his mind about geography and the universe, so he had built his own telescope by the time he was 12.

He could be described perhaps as a drop-out law student. The law was too dull for him and when he was an articled clerk he fled to sea. In 1873 he spent six months tramping by foot around New South Wales and Queensland, then in 1874 he went on to the the United States and interviewed Brigham Young in Salt Lake City.

He was enchanted by Mormonism, enchanted by the new settlement and by the streets thronged with the pretty girl wives. He even considered joining the sect, but fear of his grandmother checked that. She threatened to be on the next boat to rescue him from 'those hussies'.

One of his names was 'Wraggie the human meteor' and the next area to be struck was Adelaide. He worked in the South Australian Surveyor-General's Department for four years and while there he railed against all the wasted water which poured down to the sea through the east coast rivers. All this, he said, should be diverted back over the ranges and collected in a great inland sea in Central Australia. When no politician would listen to him he returned to England, and so became interested in meteorology.

He thought it was time someone studied the weather on Ben Nevis, Britain's highest mountain, 4406 feet. During the winter of 1881-2 he climbed the mountain every day for five months. It was an endurance feat almost beyond belief. He set out daily at 4.30 a.m. with the object of getting to the summit by nine and took scientific readings all the way there and back.

Five days out of six the weather was appalling and frequently the instruments were frozen to their supports. During the last month he almost lost his life through the worst gale of the 19th Century. Yet still he managed to get up and down. The Scottish Meteorological Society was so impressed it gave £4,000 for a permanent observatory on Ben Nevis.

By now Clement Wragge was making a name for himself. He married and migrated to Australia. First he established Government observatories at Adelaide and Mt. Lofty, then he transferred to Queensland as Government Meteorologist and founded the Brisbane Weather Bureau. This is when he started his breezy, even jolly weather forecasts. He had studied the methods of an Austrian, Eduard Bruckner, and working on weather cycles he began to issue long range forecasts. From that day to this there have been those who have sworn by the brilliance of his forecasts; that he foretold the famous 1896 cyclone, that he forecast the 1944 drought which took place 20 years after his death. Clement Wragge himself stated that he was 75% correct in all his forecasts.

Maybe the worst drought ever to hit Australia took place in 1902 and naturally he was asked constantly when it would end. Wragge decided to do something about it personally.

He had heard that vineyards in Italy had continually been wrecked by hailstorms until they used a device known as the 'Stiger Vortex' gun. The explosions from the gun created rain before the water had a chance to turn into ice.

Wragge imported plans for the gun and local newspapers raised the money to have six built at an engineering shop in Margaret St. Brisbane. They were made of heavy metal, 15 feet long and looked like ice cream cones standing on their tails.

Charleville was the worst hit drought area, so he decided to go there for his great experiment and the Queensland Railways enthusiastically carried the guns free of charge. Wragge became more and more confident and he announced: 'The Warrego will behold the unique spectacle of a regular bombardment. Jupiter Tonans and his brother Pluvius will blush with astonishment'.

They waited patiently until September 26, 1902, when clouds piled high over Charleville and conditions seemed perfect. They put the guns in two rows of three, three quarters of a mile between each, and gave them charges of 7½ ounces of powder. At noon they opened fire with ten charges from each gun. Just one or two drops fell, a faint precipitation, then two hours later there was a light shower.

The natural conclusion from this was, if a light bombardment produced a light shower what would happen if they created a complete battle of Waterloo. So the guns boomed and boomed so much that two of them burst under the strain. But this time not a drop fell and the general opinion was the whole experiment was embarrassing.

All this happened at a most delicate time. For years he had shown contempt for most other meteorological activity in Australia. As far back as 1888 he had said there were no climatological stations in Australia except those in Queensland. He even sent his forecasts for New South Wales to Sydney newspapers without previous reference to Sydney's weather bureau. What's more he captioned his weather maps: 'Meteorology of Australia; Chief Weather Bureau, Brisbane'.

Come Federation, it seemed a certainty that he would get the job of Commonwealth Meteorologist, but he did not. He had offended too many people — his brilliance was too hard to take. Then because of the new Commonwealth, the Queensland Weather Bureau ceased to exist and the Wragge collection of rainfall statistics and other data went to Mr J.B. Henderson, the Hydraulic Engineer.

For a time he battled to carry on with a small Commonwealth subsidy. This petered out when New South Wales refused to pay so the 'boss weather bureau', as he called it, ceased to exist. For a year he brought out an astonishing magazine called simply Wragge. It contained a bit of everything, information on the universe, recipes for sponge cake and scones, attacks on the shortcomings of white Australia and a diary of Clement Wragge's early travels. Each edition was headed: 'FOR GOD, KING, EMPIRE AND PEOPLE'.

He went all over Australia, gave lectures for one shilling and two shillings admittance and he gave more than 2000 of them. Each lecture always finished with a lantern slide of the King and the Union Jack.

In 1910 he settled in New Zealand at Birkenhead, Auckland where once again he built a beautiful tropical garden, later known as Waiata Tropical Gardens, and people came from all over New Zealand to see. He died of a stroke on 10 December 1922.

He was a genius who was never properly understood by his peers. The late Inigo Jones wrote of him: 'His was in many ways the mind of a mystic and he held the view that we are here under control of powers and beings utterly beyond our conception and he also believed that of our mental activities nothing is ever lost but that these mystic beings most carefully protect the children of the mind through every vicissitude and also contrive that these activities shall be continued'. Inigo Jones also believed this passionately because he was Clement Wragge's favorite pupil and he took over his work at the Queensland Crohamhurst Observatory.

Wragge followed the theories of the Austrian scientist Eduard Bruckner, who propounded the famous 35 year weather cycle. He had his followers among farmers and seamen who said his long range weather forecasts were uncanny in their accuracy. Inigo Jones used a similar system and the successor to Jones, Lennox Walker, is still using them today.

In his day he was called a fool, a charlatan by some and by others a genius. Whatever we think about him we should be grateful for the manner in which he gave personality to cyclones.
