Hugh Donald McIntosh

15 min read.

First published 1980

Profile of 'Huge Deal' McIntosh, the Sydney fight promoter who staged the 1908 Burns-Johnson world heavyweight title bout, made and lost a fortune, and later tried to launch a chain of London milk bars.

THE GREAT NELLIE STEWART SAID THAT Hugh McIntosh was 'a man of little less than medium height, broad in the shoulders, cheery in the eye, hiding under a rattling loquacity the fact that he is as shy as a girl.'

Well maybe he was. Maybe Nellie Stewart had the key to his character. McIntosh often worked 15 hours a day, betraying a passion to acquire everything he saw. He bought hugely expensive houses, cars, and showered lavish presents on friends. Was all this done because really, deep down, he was as shy as a girl?

However, frequently he covered his shyness with surprising skill. In the early 'twenties a friend introduced him to H.G. Wells at the Royal Automobile Club in Pall Mall. McIntosh opened the conversation with the line: 'I don't like your political views'.

'What books or articles of mine have you read?' enquired Wells.

'Not one', answered McIntosh.

Melbourne Punch records that on this unpromising note they went in to lunch and remained together in interested conversation till five o'clock.

Nellie Stewart was right in that he was below medium height, but he had huge shoulders and he was built like a truck. He had close cropped hair, black eyebrows and a jaw of such strength it looked capable of crunching through brick walls. Then just to prove he was not entirely disassociated with the theatre he had the fashionable Ronald Colman moustache.

In his early days he made much use of his strength. He worked as a waiter for a caterer who supplied Sydney's Masonic Hall and the clientele was very tough. Young McIntosh displayed considerable skill as a chucker-out. For example, a member of the Slaughterman's Union began to throw around the plates. Hugh D. picked him up and dropped him down several flights of stairs. This feat was sufficient for him to graduate as head waiter.

He was Hugh Donald, but the popular name for him was 'Huge Deal'.

Of course his most famous huge deal was the staging of three heavyweight title fights in 1908. He did this within four months and made a fortune out of every one, yet there has not been a world heavyweight title fight in Australia since. He was smart. He realised that the American Fleet on a triumphant tour of the Pacific was visiting Sydney and there would be 12,000 sailors in town with money to spend.

Who did he need to entertain the sailors? Why not their idol, Tommy Burns, champion of the world, so he cabled Burns and offered him £4000 plus expenses to defend his title in two fights. This was an unbelievable sum to offer a boxer in 1908. 'People thought I had gone crazy,' said Hugh D.

McIntosh then realised he had no place to stage the fight, so he went to a Kings Cross ironmonger named Furness, who owned a large piece of land at Rushcutters Bay. It was an old market garden. McIntosh didn't reveal much — he just confided that he wanted to 'make a few bob out of the sailors', and he managed to lease it for two years at a weekly rental of £2.

Later landlord Furness saw his tenant on the building site, dressed in an expensive suit and smoking a cigar. When he asked what was going on McIntosh replied: 'I am building the greatest open-air stadium in the world and my show for the sailors is a fight between Tommy Burns and Bill Squires for the world title'.

He met Burns in Adelaide; and he said: 'I remember my first action on greeting him was to borrow £20. As we left the boat I discovered that I had no money to buy our rail tickets, and so I turned to Burns for a loan. His face was a picture as he poured 20 golden sovereigns into my hands. Here was the promoter who had enticed him to Australia with the promise of a £4000 contract already touching him for £20. "Gee," he said slowly, "he's touched me for twenty of the best already".'

When they moved on to Melbourne he booked Burns into the august Hotel Menzies under his real French-Canadian name, Noah Busso, as the management would never have considered accepting a prize fighter under their roof.

When the crowds began to gather outside the hotel as if he were a modern day pop idol, the startled manager asked: 'Pardon me, but who is this Mr Noah Busso?'

'Ah,' said McIntosh impressively, 'Mr Busso is a very well known athlete.'

'Really,' said the manager politely.

'Yes,' said McIntosh fleeing out the door, 'as a matter of fact he is Tommy Burns, heavyweight champion of the world.' But by this time they were well settled in and it was too late to throw them out.

The first opponent for Burns was a tough, hard-hitting miner named Bill Squires. They had met twice before, in California and in Paris. Both times Burns won with ease.

The fight was to take place in the biggest stadium in the world and it was important not to leave anything to chance. McIntosh took over every unlet place of amusement in Sydney during Fleet Week, every theatre, every dance hall and even the boats on the harbor. The result was that a very big proportion of the money spent in Sydney that week went to 'Huge Deal' McIntosh.

As it turned out the U.S. Fleet deliberately spurned the fight. Only two sailors turned up at Rushcutter's Bay and they were drunk. Someone had spread the word that because of the previous two fights the whole thing was a fix. But Huge D. didn't have to worry, 20,000 filled the Stadium to capacity and he had a gate of 13,600.

Tommy Burns knocked out Squires in the 13th round and the whole promotion was such a success immediately McIntosh hurried Burns to Melbourne for another world title fight against Bill Lang, the Victorian champion. The bout took place on September 3rd in a Stadium at City Road, South Melbourne before a crowd of 10,000. In the second round Lang dropped Burns with a wild left hook to the jaw and down he went for what seemed a scandalously long count of 15 seconds, Harry Nathan was the referee and for ever after Lang thought he had been robbed of a world title.

Tommy only a short man had very long arms and he was a formidable opponent. He got to his feet, held off Lang for several rounds, then in the sixth he took over. He knocked down Lang twice then finished him off with a crushing right to the jaw.

Things were now going beautifully so McIntosh offered Tommy Burns a fight with the black giant, the formidable Jack Johnson. In 1908 a fight in the United States between a black man and a white man was almost a racial impossibility. Johnson already had chased Burns around the world seeking a title fight, insulting him, abusing him, calling him a coward.

McIntosh was carefully offhand when he asked Tommy for his terms for a Johnson fight. The National Sporting Club in London already had tried him out and at that time Burns said '£6000'. The Club rejected the figure as absurd, so Tommy thought he was safe when he said £6000 again.

But McIntosh was delighted. Immediately he said: 'Done'.

Johnson agreed to fight for £1000 and even this seemed a figure of fantasy. He was sceptical whether anyone in a country as remote as Australia could pay it. He had fought in Australia before for £30 and £40 purses.

McIntosh took an immediate dislike to Johnson. He didn't like his mouth full of gold teeth and he didn't like his arrogant, swaggering manner. He claimed even his mouth full of glittering gold teeth jarred on his nerves.

Johnson already had heard that Burns and McIntosh were good friends so when the pair met on the wharf at Melbourne, Johnson looked at McIntosh with sneering indifference and left his outstretched hand hanging in the air.

One of their more dramatic encounters was on the train to Sydney. On an earlier tour to Australia, Johnson assaulted his trainer so McIntosh decided to warn him that he didn't want any similar unwelcome publicity while he was under contract to him.

Johnson looked at his fist and said: 'If any dirty sucker double crosses me I'll knock him cold.'

McIntosh claims that he saw red, jumped to his feet and replied: 'You black swine. If ever you lift your hand to me I'll blow your black head off your body. They'll identify you by your clothes.'

McIntosh had a revolver in his pocket and now he knew Johnson's one weakness. He was frightened of no man in a physical encounter but he was terrified of firearms. So McIntosh made use of this. Just before the fight when Johnson refused to go into the ring unless he was paid more money, McIntosh once again threatened him with his revolver. 'Get into the ring', he roared, 'or you'll be carried out on a stretcher.' It worked.

The nagging battles never ceased. There was the problem of the referee. Every name that came up Johnson rejected. He suggested Snowy Baker, the greatest amateur athlete Australia ever produced. 'He's a blonde and I don't like blondes', said Johnson.

'I'm not asking for a black referee, but I know whatever referee I get I'll lose three points in every ten on account of the color of mah skin.'

The situation had reached a hopeless impasse when Johnson said suddenly, 'Waal Mr Mac, I've made up mah mind about the referee. I've chosen you.'

McIntosh had never refereed a fight, let alone a world title affair. 'You're crazy,' he said.

'O.K., the fight's off,' replied Johnson.

So that was it, Hugh D. McIntosh became referee, fight promoter, all in one.

The rest of the story is well known. In the ring for the world's first black-white heavyweight title contest, they made an awe-inspiring pair. Burns with his black forelock hanging forward barely came up to the top of Johnson's shoulders.

Johnson hit Burns with a terrible upper cut flush on the jaw in the first round and after that it was virtually no contest.

But Johnson was determined to make Burns pay for every misdeed by a white man since slaves were imported to the U.S.A. He thrashed him slowly and carefully, round by round until he was a bleeding, helpless hulk, calling out all the time 'Come on Tahmmmy. They told me you were a fighter.'

The police, not Hugh D. stopped the fight in the 14th round. McIntosh claimed that he made £2000 a minute out of that fight, and a total profit of £100,000. Tommy Burns had thought McIntosh would never get back his contract fee of £6000, but there was a cinematograph film of the entire bout. That alone made £70,000.

Now he was very rich indeed. He bought Belhaven overlooking Rose Bay, a large house with what were described as 'real plate glass windows', superb library and he installed an electric organ at a cost of £5000.

He had three Pierce-Arrows cars with his own coat of arms on the side. He became a friend of the Premier of New South Wales, W.A. Holman, bought the Sunday Times, bought the Tivoli Theatre and entertained all the visiting great, Galli Curci, Anna Pavlova, Fritz Kreisler, Benno Moseiwitsch.

Hugh D. McIntosh was born in Sydney on 10 September, 1876. His parents were Scottish. His father had been in the Indian Mutiny and had won a medal with five clasps, which always remained one of Hugh D.'s treasures, yet this was a father he never really knew. He died when he was four.

Hugh D. went to school at the Marist Brother's College, but when he was nine he ran away to Broken Hill and earned his living by picking ore. He did this for two years and developed a loathing for mines and everything associated with them. Next he got a job as a 'generally useful lad' working for a doctor and this old medical practitioner developed such a liking for him he decided to put him through medical school.

The breaks were not going his way. The old doctor died so McIntosh was on his own again. He became a farm labourer, engine driver, baker's boy, stage hand, and of course waiter.

When he became head waiter at the catering establishment, the caterer died so he married the caterer's widow, Hariette Ann Mosman. So at 21 he had a business, a wife and three daughters. He remained loyal to that family and years later the husbands of his three adopted daughters worked for him in his various businesses.

It is not easy to keep track of all the McIntosh enterprises. He sold pies outside sporting grounds until he had an army of pie vendors working for him at racecourses, beaches, football grounds and parks. He had the leading catering business in Sydney when he sold out to Sargents in 1906.

He ran a physical culture school and tried his hand at cycling promotion, a sport that always had fascinated him, particularly when he noted that a good event could attract crowds of more than 6000. For a one-mile sprint at the Sydney Cricket Ground he offered a prize of 1000 sovereigns, the sort of prize that would not be available even 70 years later.

He brought out the American negro Major Taylor, then the fastest cyclist in the world, Plugger Bill Martin and many others. The New South Wales League of Wheelmen was so impressed with his skills it made him paid secretary.

Cyclists at this time caused even more trouble than prize fighters. McIntosh was able to practice his old chucker-out skills. He used to keep on his desk a roll of music — a song titled Oh How Delightful.

In moments of crisis Hugh D. would tap Oh How Delightful on the table and the heavy thud would reveal that inside the roll there was a solid bar of iron and his reputation was such, they knew he would not hesitate to use it.

From cycling he went to fight promotion and before long McIntosh estimated that he had a bank balance of a quarter of a million poinds. He was lavish in his generosity. He gave motor cars to his friends. He gave chorus girls diamond-studded watches. He gave £5 tips to waiters and gold cigarette cases to acquaintances.

In 1928 he sold Belhaven in Sydney, took his £5000 electric organ with him, and bought Broome Park, near Canterbury Kent, a ducal manor, formerly the home of Field Marshal Earl Kitchener.

It was a 17th century manor with 600 acres, a main hall 90 feet long, panelled in oak, library, boudoir, museum, a Garter room; nine principal bedrooms, six other bedrooms, three bathrooms, and ten rooms for the servants plus two bathrooms on the second floor.

His parkland had terraces, fountains, sunken gardens, summer houses, rose walks and here at his week-end parties he entertained the nobility and all the great of England. He enjoyed the life of a squire of Kent, family prayers night and morning, divine worship on the Sabbath, tea at the vicarage and he was even Church-warden at the local Church. Legend has it however that the Vicar became suspicious about his orthodoxy when he brought the members of his week-end party to Matins. These included Charlie Chaplin, Earl Russell, Pola Negri and John Lane.

All this was costing him around £1200 a week. He had a series of unsuccessful fight promotions. His Sunday Times, ailing through bad management and poor quality journalism, failed in 1930 and suddenly everything was collapsing around Hugh D. McIntosh.

In January 1932 he was declared bankrupt with debts of £283,515. There was a long list of debts to all his friends, £251 to a taxi service, £484 to Menzies Hotel, Melbourne, £20,000 to Tivoli Theatre and he declared that in England he had nothing left, only debts of £14,000.

So Huge Deal appeared to be finished.

He lived quietly now in a small rented cottage in Robinson Road, Centennial Park, Sydney. He tried his hand again at fight promotion, he tried running a guest house, but little was heard of Hugh D. again until 1936 and now the newspapers were full of stories.

He was in London and he was planning to launch 500 milk bars. There was a big launching celebration. 'I am on my way to making a million out of milk,' announced Hugh D. The Milk Board in Victoria with its 'Big M' planned a similar campaign in 1978. Hugh D was just 42 years ahead of his time.

He was expecting two million customers a week, and the message through all his propaganda was that marvellous milk was the solution to all ills. He said there were now more than a hundred different flavorings and the most popular was the 'shake' made from British strawberries. All classes patronised his bars from Cabinet Ministers to street cleaners.

Confidently he announced: 'The Benefit of milk bars to the industry cannot be exaggerated. If the present rate of expansion is maintained Britain will need a million more cows.'

He promoted cycling aces at Wembley with the stars training on milk, but it wasn't all that easy. He did actually launch twelve milk bars, but there were set backs. On Friday 13th of November his milk bar at Hammersmith had this problem:

'... most unfortunate Friday 13th at this branck. About 8.30 during the busy time a customer was sick all over the counter. Miss Jones caught her foot and knocked over two gallons of milk. Dixon slipped in the kitchen and dropped a soup pot about a quarter full of tomato soup. At 11.30 two men started to fight in the bar, and one smashed a glass over the other's head cutting his ear badly. Police were immediately notified. Milk splashed all over the wall and broken glass flew.'

'That's the kind of thing that makes a man superstitious,' commented Hugh D.

The milk bars continued until November 1938, then they too folded. Somehow the average Britain still preferred his warm beer to warm milk. McIntosh said it was all through expanding too quickly and having not enough capital.

He was back where he started. Huge Deal died four years later, on February 2, 1942. The man who had been very nearly the richest in Australia, was now penniless. His old friends contributed to pay his funeral expenses.
