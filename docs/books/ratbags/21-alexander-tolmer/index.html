Alexander Tolmer

27 min read.

First published 1980

SOUTH AUSTRALIA has had some controversial and colorful commissioners of police. Furthermore it has sacked some of them, but of all those sacked gentlemen surely none would be the equal of the redoubtable Alexander Tolmer.

Tolmer had considerable skills. He was bilingual in English and French, fluent in Portugese, sufficiently skilled as a painter to have one of his pictures presented to the Queen, good enough on the violin to perform at an occasional concert, a better swimmer than Byron and absolutely marvellous at getting himself into exciting, dangerous situations. He had the daring do of a comic strip hero and he once claimed that he wasn't like a cat which merely had nine lives. Alexander Tolmer had a hundred of them.

He could write, he was scathingly articulate to his own detriment and for a fellow who was only nine and a half stone and five feet nine inches, barely large enough for a policeman, he was splendid with his fists. Indeed he adored to use his knuckles and he was not above conducting discipline among the troops in the old-fashioned style. He was arrogant, impulsive and a brilliant all-round hero.

As for his looks, he had one favorite picture which appeared by the fly-leaf of his two-volume autobiography. There's Tolmer with more hair than a University student, eloquent curls and waves, sideburns, noble moustache — he looks like a cross between Mark Twain and Buffalo Bill.

Of course, there were some interesting ingredients in his background. His grandfather was German and he emigrated to Normandy where he married a French woman. His father went to school in Paris with the theory that he should become a priest. As Tolmer put it, he did attain 'the dignity of an abbe' but found the life too awful and he joined the French Army. While Napoleon was exiled on Elba he made the mistake of backing the Royalists, so when the Emperor returned in 1815 Tolmer and his wife fled to England.

The same year young Alexander was born, an 'Englishman'. His mother died two months later.

His father settled at Plymouth, a teacher, a professor of languages. He married again to give Alexander an English mother, and from then on his schooling alternated between England and France. He said that when he was at school in Plymouth or Maidstone the children laughed at him and taunted him with cries of 'Froggy.' When he went to school at Rouen the French boys jeered with such names as 'God-dam' and 'Rost-bif.' On both sides of the Channel he solved this problem with an explosion of fists which was an early part of his education.

At fifteen he ran away to sea and he had a shockingly frigid, miserable time as a ship's boy aboard a collier in the North Sea. He thought the captain was going to be so kindly and let him sleep in his cabin; instead he kicked him down the hatchway so young Tolmer spent the night huddled in a corner sobbing bitterly.

Next his father sent him to the Reverend H. Boyce's school at Edgware to train to be a language teacher. Here the treatment was almost as bad as on the ship. He was made to live on milk and water, so he waited for an afternoon's half holiday, and with only five shillings in his pocket, caught the coach to London. There he joined the Army and off he went to Portugal with Colonel Bacon's Lancers, a British force raised to help Queen Maria II regain her throne.

At seventeen he was involved in a vicious nasty war. He became skilful as a horseman and lancer, rose to corporal on fivepence halfpenny a day and was wounded three times. The worst was on 10 October 1833 at Campo Gronda. A musket ball went into his chest, narrowly missed his heart and passed through glancing off his shoulder blade.

The doctor was only half interested. 'Now what's the matter with you?' he said. So he stuffed lint into the wound with his little finger, bound him up front back with strips of diachylon plaster and departed as quickly as he could, while young Tolmer thought his last days were at hand.

His next move was into a Convent Hospital and with the shrieks and groans of the wounded all around him he said 'my sufferings were beyond description.' The theory was that the grateful Portugese Government would pay off the touring English with Portugese citizenship plus a block of land in Brazil. The land never eventuated and young Tolmer was terribly affronted when he was paid off and discharged with an order for £20. This he had to cash with a money lender at 25 percent discount. To make matters worse on the way home he caught smallpox and he was such a shocking sight when he arrived home his stepmother threw up her hands and cried: 'Oh, my God, you have been blown up with gun powder.'

One shouldn't suggest that Tolmer did not always enjoy his Portugese adventure. He was fond of reading Byron and he was fascinated by the Byronic achievement of swimming the Hellespont back in May 1810, but what intrigued him even further was the knowledge that Byron also swam the Tagus. On 21 February 1821 Byron wrote to a friend: 'Any young man, in good and tolerable skill in swimming might succeed in it from either side. I was three hours in swimming across the Tagus, which is more hazardous being two hours longer than the Hellespont.'

The Tagus is the great estuary at Lisbon, so Tolmer for a bet, challenged his fellow soldiers. He claims that one after another they gave in. One man kept up with him until they were half way across, then he too dropped off. But on he went across to the other side, and so determined was he to outdo Byron, he swam back again. In some areas the water was frigid, but worse than that he had to compete with an ebb tide, which swept him in the wrong direction. Before he landed at the Alcantara Steps he was in danger of drowning and he had to be pulled out by a fisherman. Even so, in his reminiscences he said that he would like to see his feat formally acknowledged by the public press '...because the distance which I swam almost doubled that which Lord Byron accomplished at the same spot, and consequently I must have been about six hours in the water'

There was the further embarrassment that when he did arrive naked on shore his colleagues had presumed he was dead and had taken his clothes, so he had to wrap himself in a sail until they came back for him.

What would a parent do with such a volatile son? His father still had dreams that he should follow him as a language teacher, so he despatched him to Monsieur Libourel, who ran an academy at Guines near Calais. The worthy gentleman also ran Limourel's seminary for young ladies, mostly English, so young Tolmer did little study but excelled socially. He said: 'I was a great favourite with the ladies to whom I would relate some of my stirring adventures and hair breadth escapes.'

He lasted there only six months and returned to England to join the Army once more, this time the 16th Lancers at Maidstone, Kent. His father did not have the money to pay for a commission, but Tolmer was keen and he almost earned a track record for promotion. By the time he was 21 he was a sergeant and acting adjutant. He thought he had a brilliant future in the Army until one day there was a regimental inspection by His Royal Highness, the Duke of Cambridge. He overheard this exchange between the Duke and his Commanding Officer, Colonel Brotherton.

'What is that man's name?'

'Whiteman, your Royal Highness.'

'Is he the son of Whiteman who was shot at Waterloo by the same ball which carried off the Marquis of Anglesey's leg?'

'Yes, your Royal Highness.'

'Then, as you have a vacant adjutantcy you will oblige me by conferring the appointment upon him.'

Tolmer was shattered. No longer did he see any future in the Army. Only a few months earlier he had suffered a confrontation with Mr T.W. Carter of Kent because he wished to marry his daughter Mary. Carter had grander plans for Mary and he made it clear to Alexander that an Army man could not support a wife and family without private means. So Tolmer and Mary eloped. 'Stolen kisses are the sweetest', he said, and he banked on the idea that Carter was an amiable man and eventually he would calm down.

He did, but he suffered some disastrous investments and along with his wife went to an early death. In the midst of the unhappiness the Tolmers accepted the advice of the family banker and emigrated to South Australia. They sailed on 4 September 1839 aboard the Brankamore, 350 tons, a ship so appalling that the voyage took five months. One of the passengers was Mrs John Batman, wife of the founder of Melbourne, who was going home after a visit to England.

Tolmer had all sorts of adventures on the way but perhaps the most spectacular was his encounter with the First Mate. The daily diet of salt pork and tinned beef was frightful. That didn't worry Tolmer, he had been used to it in Portugal, but it did worry Mary Tolmer who was nursing a baby. She looked pale and ill. They were travelling on what they called an 'intermediate' ticket and under terms of the contract they were to receive a bottle of porter a day. Suddenly this was cut out. When Tolmer complained he was told stocks were finished, but then he noticed the superior cabin class passengers were still getting their ration.

One hot sultry day in the topics his wife was particularly ill, so he went straight to the Captain, demanded porter for his wife and said he was willing to pay for it. The Captain said 'No', the porter was for cabin class passengers only. Then Tolmer accused him of conspiring with the agents to defraud the passengers.

The Captain wouldn't take any more. He said to his mate: 'Mr Errington — give him a damned good thrashing.'

Obviously Errington could hardly wait, before Tolmer had even lifted his hands he received a blow on his head. He was quick enough though to duck the next one, then with a counter punch he landed Errington flat on the deck.

The crew delighted to see their over-bearing first officer suffering such humiliation cheered and howled. Tolmer carefully positioned himself with his back to the wheel and waited for the next onslaught. When it came it wasn't very difficult... 'His blows were ineffectual and I soon found that he was completely at my mercy.'

Tolmer knocked him down repeatedly. Errington's eyes were almost closed and blood was streaming from his nose, but still he did not give up. Finally Tolmer gave him a terrible blow under the chin and as the mate fell he hit his head on the ship's gun. The crew carried him off the deck unconscious and he did not appear again for three weeks.

The furious Captain Smith ordered Tolmer below to his cabin and told him to stay there until further notice. At the end of three weeks Tolmer could not stand the foul air below any longer, so up he came and immediately he was attacked by the ship's carpenter and the second mate, each armed with a cutlass. He knocked one down with his fist and gave the other a kick in the stomach which sent him rolling on the deck. No-one tried to keep him below after that and Mrs Tolmer did get her porter.

He had a good arrival in Adelaide. His old commanding officer, Colonel Brotherton, served with Governor Gawler, so the Tolmers went straight to Government House armed with a letter of introduction. Tolmer was just what Gawler was looking for — here was an instructor in cavalry, so there was an immediate job for him as sub-inspector of mounted police.

Gawler was keen to know whether Tolmer could discipline a mob of unruly troopers. He need hardly have asked. He could maintain order with his own fists and anybody who cared to question his power was welcome to try.

Tolmer used to rub himself with emu oil. He said it was a marvellous cure for rheumatism or contracted muscles brought on by exposure. It was good too for promoting growth when losing one's hair. He said that but for emu oil 'both my arms would not have stood the hand-to-hand fights and hard knocks I have had in capturing bush rangers and other like ruffians.'

He did concede that at certain seasons the emu was very fat and the oil gained by frying the fat in a pot had a most offensive smell.

At twenty-five Tolmer was an inspector and rarely at rest — he was for ever combing unexplored countryside for escaped convicts, murderers, thieves, protecting farmers from marauding blacks, seeking out smugglers, smashing illicit stills.

He was involved in several punitive expeditions against the Aborigines. In July 1840 the brig Maria while on its way to Tasmania foundered at the mouth of a creek near the present town of Kingston. A search party looking for survivors found the terribly mangled bodies of adults and children half hidden by the sand dunes. Eventually they discovered all twenty-six passengers and crew had been slaughtered. Governor Gawler ordered the Police Chief O'Halloran to set out, find the blacks who had done the job and upon being convinced of their guilt, not more than three of them were to be executed in front of their tribesmen.

Major O'Halloran, Tolmer and twenty troopers set out in the direction of Goolwa and the lonely beaches of the Coorong. They found members of the Big Murray tribe and according to Tolmer, without exception they all wore European clothing, some of them stained with blood and there were women's shifts that had spear holes about the breast.

After much questioning the tribesmen pushed forward two men whom they said had done the murdering. The troopers built some gallows of sheoak which they erected over the graves of the Maria passengers. They put nooses around the necks of the two Aborigines and stood them on boxes. The crude execution method was to kick away the boxes, but according to Tolmer the drop was insufficient.

So there was a dreadful scene with men still dangling, half-choking, toes touching the sand and their eyes glaring up at the cross beam. A seaman stepped forward and said: 'I beg your pardon, Major, but I'll soon hang them if you'll let me.'

'Do, Barber, anything, but be quick.' This time they used the cross beam of the whale boat and the operation was completely efficient. However to complete the lesson Major O'Halloran made this announcement to the presumably uncomprehending Murray tribe. He began: 'Black men, this is the white's punishment for murder, the next time white men are killed in this country more punishment will be given. Let none of you take these bodies down, they must hang until they fall to pieces. We are now friends...'

The execution without trial caused fury and controversy in the pages of the Adelaide Register. Tolmer called the critics 'misguided philanthropists' but the criticism continued in England even after Governor Gawler's recall and some even attempted to have him tried for murder.

Tolmer was extraordinarily diverse in his colonial activities. He has the credit for pioneering a mail route to the Eastern colonies. Some historians even call him the father of trotting in South Australia. In 1845 he called on the pioneer grazier, Edward Henty at his property on the Wannon River. Tolmer was captivated by a mottled grey mare named Norah. Henty told him the mare ought to be worth 500 guineas, but the horse was a crazy killer. His men wouldn't go near her and he'd be lucky if ever she brought as much as £30.

How could Tolmer resist a challenge like that. Immediately he produced his £30 and became the new owner. So started the long trip to Adelaide with the difficult Norah, One of his subduing methods was this. He put a halter over the mare's neck, then armed his troopers with Mallee sticks. Whenever Norah made an attack they clouted her over the nose.

By the time they reached Adelaide Norah was almost amiable and he managed to break her in sufficiently to enter her for Adelaide's first steeplechase event on 23 September 1846 over three miles with eleven jumps. Norah had received such a build up naturally she was favorite for the race. The result was a disaster. The horse in front of her baulked at the first jump and quietly got into a ditch. Norah followed this lead and did exactly the same.

All Adelaide found this the joke of the year, the grand deflation of Alexander Tolmer. He lay low for a week then decided to brave it out in a gathering at the Royal Exchange Hotel. Amongst those present was George Coppin the comedian and theatrical entrepreneur and a horse owner named Paxton, an Adelaide chemist.

Paxton said: 'Your horse can't gallop, can't jump, and is not worth a damn.'

'Well', replied Tolmer, hitting out wildly, 'maybe she can't gallop or jump, but at least she can trot.'

This line caused great laughter.

'I bet twenty pounds she can trot seven miles in half an hour,' shouted the embarrassed Tolmer. The amazing bet was accepted, but there was nothing shy about the Inspector. He wrote that two days later 'carelessly' he walked into the Exchange Hotel to hear Paxton say: 'Well Tolmer, how is the mare getting on? You'll never do it you know, so you may as well throw in the sponge and pay forfeit.' Tolmer laughed. He said rather than do that he would double the bets, so with other side stakes the total sum came to £60, a mighty bet in the eighteen forties.

The plan was to ride from Frome Road Bridge to Port Bridge the following morning and he wanted the whole thing in absolute secret because he didn't want his mare upset. But when he went out to buy a cigar he realised the great challenge was being discussed on every street corner, so he went back to the hotel and announced a change of plans: 'All right, we'll do it in half an hour from now.'

He brought Norah to the starting point and already it seemed over ambitious. The distance he planned to cover in thirty minutes was closer to eight miles than seven, so he would have to average better than fourteen miles an hour. 'I patted Norah on the neck,' he said, 'threw away my cigar and was off.'

Once Norah broke at the sight of some other horses and under the laws of trotting Tolmer had to stop, turn her around, and start again. It happened again when she was frightened by a dog, yet she made it — she trotted the distance in twenty-seven minutes twelve seconds.

Tolmer was ecstatic. Back he went to the Royal Exchange Hotel and he had a fine night pulling the corks out of champagne bottles. After all the drinking he went round to the hotel stable, carelessly flashed his lantern inside and Norah took fright. She bolted and galloped wildly through Adelaide's street until finally she injured herself badly trying to leap the rails of a bridge.

Paxton kept pestering for another challenge to get his money. 'Your mare did eight miles under thirty minutes, I admit, but she couldn't do sixteen miles in under sixty minutes. She's not that good.'

Tolmer had to remain humble until the police veterinary surgeon passed Norah as fit, then to the cheers of the Exchange Bar, he said, Yes, he would do it. Paxton replied: 'Good. We'll double the bet. Will we make it £120?'

That was a tough one. Tolmer did not have £120, so he had to put his mare on the line, £120 versus Norah.

Now the whole affair was much more serious. He asked the Governor, Colonel Robe, for two weeks off official duties and settled down to steady training. His luck was appalling. It rained and rained and rained until there was not a solid piece of earth left in Adelaide. It all looked hopeless until on a visit to Glenelg, he thought why not? — the beach at low tide would make a good trotting track.

Norah's form improved rapidly until for short stretches she was capable of twenty-two miles an hour. Tolmer at last fixed the time, 10 a.m., low tide, two days hence. That was fine except on race eve there was a hurricane, one of the worst in the history of South Australia.

The road to Adelaide was a sheet of water and the once fine beach almost disappeared. It was still raining and Tolmer hoped that Paxton would never make it to Glenelg, but he did, and he insisted that the race go on as arranged. Norah had to race sometimes through soft sand, sometimes knee deep in water and often through seaweed. Tolmer heard someone shout: 'I'll lay a hundred to one against the mare.' Yet on the outgoing leg he did better than expected — twenty-seven minutes four seconds. Going back was much tougher, a south-westerly and he had to protect his face against hailstones. Yet when he reached the starting post proudly he went on to the seventeen mile, which was Tolmer proving a point. He did the extra mile and the time was fifty-five minutes two seconds. He claimed it was the best trotting feat by a horse anywhere in the world. Paxton was shocked and disappointed, particularly as he had brought along a saddle and bridle to ride Norah home.

But there was a tough sequel to the tale. An artillery officer on leave from India offered £500 for Norah, delivered safely in Calcutta. This was a fortune to Tolmer. He went to great trouble and expense to make sure her stall on the ship was well padded, that she was properly slung.

Some time later he received a message from the shipping company: 'We regret to advise that many horses were lost in bad weather off the Leuwin. The mare Norah was one of the first to go.'

Tolmer was a man of spectacular courage, his two volume memoirs have an adventure on every page. Kangaroo Island was a favorite South Australian haven for smugglers and escapers of all kinds. In 1844 Governor Grey received an impudent message from a deserter named George Gilkes. Gilkes told a sea captain: 'Tell the Governor that you have seen Gilkes, and if he likes to send his police the first shot will be over their heads and the next through.'

The Governor immediately sent his footman for Inspector Tolmer, ordered him to requisition a boat and immediately make for Kangaroo Island. It was a desolate place then — inhabitants were few and houses isolated. Tolmer's technique was not to ask questions but to kick a door open and march straight in. After a number of false starts he received a surprising bonus, he caught a runaway convict named 'Gentleman' John McDonald.

On the Western River, still hunting for Gilkes, he kicked in the door of a hut only to find inside a terrified cat and in the midst of the rush Gentleman John escaped. Tolmer did a fifty mile march to Old Jacobs hut where he had hoped to find Gilkes and exhausted he went to sleep on the floor. He awakened to find McDonald standing over him and saying: 'I'll shoot you with your own gun, you -'

Tolmer said he grabbed the muzzle, pushed it away from his head and kicked him in the stomach. A fight went on for fifteen or twenty minutes, in which he repeatedly knocked McDonald to the ground. 'The last blow,' he said, 'I struck with such force that I knocked the third knuckle of my third finger right in, and it remains so to this day.' It was just after this that Tolmer's men captured Gilkes and other desperados, the result, total triumph for Tolmer. The Governor was pleased. Tolmer became Acting Police Commissioner, then in 1852, aged only thirty-seven Alexander Tolmer was confirmed as Commissioner of Police.

His captures were good but the achievement which made him a public hero was as leader of the Gold Escort. The discovery of gold in Victoria had an extraordinary affect on the neighboring colony. By December 1851 South Australia was desperate. The towns and villages were almost empty, stripped of male inhabitants. The copper and tin mines were left without miners and the shops of Adelaide were closing one after another. There was no money and the colony was close to bankruptcy.

Tolmer came up with an ingenious scheme. Half Adelaide was at the Victorian diggings and all the gold was flowing straight to Melbourne. Why not form a police gold escort, contact the South Australian diggers, and bring back their gold directly and safely to Adelaide. If they followed traditional routes it would be over 500 miles, but if they went in a direct line across country, Adelaide to the big find at Mount Alexander, then it was only 310 miles. The first reaction to the Tolmer suggestion was almost ridicule. The Colonial Secretary, Lieutenant-Colonel Finnis wrote back with a counter measure. Seeing the colony was enduring a depression he ordered a reduction in the size of the police force by fifty men before the end of the month, a neat act which would not only cripple the force but make the escort impossible.

Tolmer when he wanted something did not mind short cuts, something which did not add to his official popularity. He went over the Colonial Secretary's head to Sir Henry Young, the Lieutenant-Governor. When the Governor asked how soon he could start, the never bashful Tolmer replied: 'In two or three days.'

It could hardly be described as a dramatic escort. He had three constables, a native, a spring cart and a small string of horses, but he went with high speed. The scrub in places was thicker than he expected, so the final route was 338 miles, yet he reached the diggings in eight days.

His reception from the Victorians at the Mount Alexander diggings was not entirely warm. As one historian put it, here he was 'diverting some opulence away from booming, vulgar Melbourne.' While he was gathering gold from the South Australian diggers he met the Victorian Colonial Secretary, William Lonsdale, who told him coldly he had received no notification whatever that the gold escort had an official role on behalf of the South Australian Government.

Later when Lonsdale asked how much gold he had gathered from the diggers Tolmer said: 'Is the question put officially?'

'Certainly.'

'Then Sir,' continued Tolmer, 'as you refuse to acknowledge my official position under the South Australian Government, I decline to give you the information.'

Tolmer paid seventy-two shilling an ounce for assayed gold which was better by 12 shillings than the Melbourne price, so he set off home with gold and notes worth 21,000 pounds deposited by 318 diggers.

Nothing serious happened on the way back but this incident indicates the Tolmer style. He had heard that the Four Posts Hotel at Glenorchy was a notorious hide-out for thieves and murderers. So on approaching the hotel he called out in a loud voice: 'HALT! FRONT! DRESS!' Immediately out came half a dozen of the most cut-throat looking characters he had ever seen. He told the innkeeper to bring out a bottle of his best cognac, told him to draw and cork and give each Police Trooper a glass as he called him forward from the ranks.

As Tolmer tells the story: 'Whilst this was going on I said jocosely to the knot of ruffians, "Well lads, when are you going to shoot me eh? I always wear this laced cap, you see! Mind you don't miss me; and if you don't shoot the driver as well, you'll never get the gold."'

He then shouted: 'Files right! Advance guard to the front. March! Trot!' Tolmer was convinced that this brilliant display of discipline frightened them out of an attack.

There was a hero's welcome back to Adelaide which started all the way from Crafers. There were eighteen gold escorts before the service was discontinued in December 1853. Tolmer led the first three of them. The third was the most difficult and dangerous of all when he had to battle rain and flood. He almost drowned on one occasion recovering six bags of gold from Deep Creek. The escorts carried 327,491 ounces of gold and there is no question the Tolmer initiative saved Adelaide. The grateful citizens presented him with a gold watch and 100 guineas, but the official attitude was the reverse.

The Gold Escort returns to Adelaide were like the triumphs of Roman Generals, cheering, banners of welcome across the road, yet on 29 November 1853 Tolmer received a curt, savage note from Finnis, the Colonial Secretary advising that 'the Governor has directed me to inform you that he is compelled to relieve you of your office as Commissioner of Police, and at the same time his Excellency replaces you in your former situation and rank as inspector.'

Tolmer was stunned, he could hardly believe what he read. When he received his promotion to Commissioner back in August '52 the Governor told him this was a rise that was 'well earned.' Gloomily Tolmer wrote that the number of times he risked his life on the Gold Escort he deserved a pension of £500 a year, instead he was getting the sack.

Of course, those in power were jealous of the Tolmer triumphs, but there were other reasons for his fall. One has only to read his memoirs to note that his failing was his extraordinary personality, which others could not understand. He had a gift for making enemies.

There was the night at a hotel in Mount Barker, when three guests at a dinner party refused to drink a toast to the Governor, Captain Grey. Grey had not been a popular Governor, but according to Tolmer, he still deserved respect because he represented the Queen.

The battle started when in reply to Tolmer's toast a dinner guest named Guy Hide said: 'I'd see him damned first.' Tolmer grabbed Hide by the scruff of the neck, the seat of the pants and threw him right across the table smashing the glasses and the port decanters. When the second man objected he hit him between the eyes with his fist and so an all in brawl commenced until the three objectors were thrown outside. Tolmer eventually agreed to let them in again on condition that they bought six bottles of champagne.

In 1849 while Acting Police Commissioner he was also Acting Police Magistrate, an impossible combination and Tolmer more than anybody was sensitive to its dangers. A lawyer, Richard Hanson, criticised him over his double hat job and Tolmer exploded, and attacked with threats of contempt of court. That was a big mistake, Hanson was a future Attorney-General.

He was a great advice giver, an intruder into other men's departments. One hardly would have thought it was police work but he drew up plans for a bridge across the Torrens. He demonstrated that the Tolmer bridge would cost £4000. The bridge submitted by the Surveyor General, Captain Freeling, cost £20,000. That was major mistake number two. Freeling was to be the chairman of an inquiry into the police force.

The trouble really started because Tolmer was bored utterly by administration and life at a desk — he was the out-of-doors extrovert adventurer and liked to do things himself. His long forays into the bush, his months on Gold Escort work left the Force in a dreadful state of inefficiency.

Tolmer put the blame on those he left behind. In September 1853 he suspended his deputy, Inspector Stuart. He made sixteen charges which included insubordination, failure to carry out order, disrespect and also petty things like increasing without authority the number of fires at the department from 14 to 27.

The Board of Inquiry under Freeling found mostly in favour of Stuart. That was it — Tolmer was fired. The new Police Commissioner, described as a Johnny-come-lately, was Captain P. Egerton Warburton, formerly of the Indian Army and two years older than Tolmer.

What else could Tolmer do? He stayed on humbled as an Inspector, then on New Year's Day 1855, he won a newly created job, Superintendent of Police on £359 a year plus a house allowance of £50. P. Egerton Warburton and Tolmer fell out immediately and to such a degree that Tolmer complained of persecution to the Legislative Council. On 27 February he received a formal letter saying that the office of Superintendent of Police had been abolished and his services were no longer required. He was told that he had been a disruptive influence and he should find some other job in the public service 'where less tact and discretion might be required than in the Police Force'.

The Adelaide Register was on his side. It said that the charges were 'trumpery in the extreme' — there were petitions, there were deputations, there were even signed expressions of faith from his own troopers, but out he went with no compensation.

He was so short of money he had to withdraw his sons from school. They were foundation students at St. Peter's College, Adelaide. He was unemployed and at times almost in a state of poverty. He tried trading with a small cutter between Miland and Goolwa, but he could not make a profit. He made a disastrous attempt at farming, picked the wrong season and lost his money.

There was one more attempt to achieve glory — he tried to win the reward offered for the first south-north crossing of the Continent. His rival was John McDouall Stuart and the prize offered was £2000. 'I have no doubt,' he said, 'this achieved I will be knighted by the Queen and receive an appointment under the Imperial Government.' He mortgaged his house at Norwood and put every penny he had into the expedition. On 29 September 1859 he set out with a party of eight, including four Aboriginals and there were twenty-one horses.

The whole expedition was a humiliating failure. His expedition coincided with the worst drought and his horses died of thirst. He appealed to the Government for assistance with stores and more horses but the answer was a firm 'No'.

In 1863 the Government gave him a job as Crown Ranger at £200 a year, but now he was prepared to accept anything just to live. He claimed that he was struggling for bare subsistence. In 1877 he was transferred as sub-inspector of credit lands, salary £330 and he retired in 1885 with a gratuity of £1000 which he invested in the young Broken Hills Proprietry mining company. At last luck had turned his way and he had a little money to spare.

In 1887 he returned to England for a holiday and paraded with the men of his old regiment in a grand military review to celebrate Queen Victoria's Jubilee. Furthermore he wore the Order of the Tower and the Sword that he won in Portugal back in 1833. Queen Victoria heard about this officer who was wearing a non British medal and ordered its removal. Tolmer didn't give up here, he made his own petition to Her Majesty, asking permission to wear the medal when in uniform.

Tolmer was battling against authority even at the end. He died of uraemia on 7 March 1890 and was survived by his wife and a large family.
