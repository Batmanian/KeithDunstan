Lillian Frank

13 min read.

First published 1980

Profile of Melbourne hairdresser and social identity Lillian Frank, from her childhood as a Jewish refugee fleeing wartime Burma to her reign as the Queen of Melbourne Cup fashion and OBE-honoured charity fundraiser.

LILLIAN HAS A DEEP VOICE, Continental accent, big eyes, expressive features and as she talks her bejewelled hands make great arcs in the air. When she wants something, when she is putting over an idea, she is as irresistible as the tide.

On the dull, social scene Lillian stands out like the Eiffel Tower on the Nullarbor Plain.

In Melbourne if you want to raise a million dollars for a charity — you ask Lillian.

If you want someone to put zap into a talk-back radio program — you ask Lillian.

If you want to score a way-out picture on the front pages of the Sun, the Age, and the Australian, all on the one morning — no-one else can do it but Lillian.

Lillian is the Queen of Lillian's hair dressing salon in Trak Arcade, Toorak Village. They call her the Grand Dame, the Prima Donna of hair dressing. If we have a society register there's hardly a Mercedes Benz, Jaguar, Rolls-Royce-driving greying scalp — male or female — that hasn't been under her fingers.

Lillian is warm-hearted, as fast on her feet as a real estate broker and about as shy as Eva Gabor. She adores publicity, not really for business reasons. She loves it for itself, for the pure joy of the hunt. She is just a splendid exhibitionist who is utterly hooked.

Columnists, fashion editors, photographers, on dreary opening nights, on Derby Day, the Oaks, anywhere, have breathed a hundred times: 'Thank God for Lillian.' I mean, you can always depend on her. Take the opening night of the musical Hair in May, 1971. Some of the guests looked odd. Most of the men were in dresses and most of the females were in pants, but the truth is that from the fashion writer's point of view, there were few good photographs among the women.

Thank God for Lillian. She arrived in a sensational zebra outfit. Sensational? — No, wait a minute, she had eye lashes seven a half inches long, top and bottom. They were pasted on to her forehead and her cheeks.

Then on top of her own lustrous hair she had TWO wigs, spreading out all around — more hair than a pop singer. 'Darlingggg,' she said in triumph, 'I made seven front pages.'

The writer for a pop magazine was sitting behind her and he was furious — couldn't see anything. He wasn't game to use the word 'fucking', but in his story about the affair he kept referring to 'trucking Lillian' and her 'trucking hair-do' and how he had to get the management to 'trucking well' move him to another seat.

Lillian's antics have now become almost part of the Melbourne Cup legend. In 1969 a sharp-clawed columnist wrote: 'When Lillian sweeps through the members' gates the interstate press is relieved. If no other fizzy ladies in frightening fashion turn up — at least we can rely on Lillian, they sigh.

'"I have been reigning Queen of Cup Week for fifteen years, darling, and I've never been to the races without being photographed never," she says with the satisfaction of a cannibal counting heads. "And I have never been upstaged — never."'

The interesting thing is that unlike the afore-mentioned Eva Gabor, she's an old-fashioned girl. She never wears anything topless, see-through or anything like that. One time she went all in beige, a new special color and she had a lace top.

'Ahh,' she said, 'I was verree naive. The Herald photographer said "You look very nice, I want a different angle. You stay there and I'll get up on the grandstand steps."

'In the bar they were saying "Ha, look at that woman" and my husband Richard came running out furious. But he got my picture with all my cleavage.'

Which were the years that one remembers? — certainly 1969. Lillian has always been the terrible prelude to what female fashions are about to bring. In '69 we had yet to see leather in all its tanned shorthorn proportions. Lillian turned out all in leather with this vast ankle-length coat trimmed in blue fox. 'It was eighty degrees, and darling I nearly died. I lost three pounds,' but it was worth it. I was in all the papers — even the black and white sketches.' But Lillian was shrewd. After the second or third race, when the fashion photographers had given up the hunt, Lillian changed into something light and sensible.

She was also first into boots. 'Look, boots hadn't arrived in Melbourne yet. I had to go to a little bootmaker and tell him what I wanted. So he had to make a special last just for one pair. Ohhh those boots came right up to the knee. The publicity, the photographs! They photographed me with the Russian Ambassador to make the Russian double.

'A year later a fashion editor came to me and said, "Lillian, last year we all giggled at you. I was too soon. Now everybody is wearing them."

'Another time, in 1966, I wanted the plastic look, the newest thing, an outfit inspired by the French designer, Paco Rabanne. It was composed entirely of black and white plastic discs, 2200 of them with 8000 silver rings. I had to go to a toy factory to get those discs, then I had to go to a hardware business to get the rings to link them all together.'

Seven front pages again. But the dress did not go to waste. Afterwards she turned it into a shower curtain.

She is a most resourceful lady. There was the famous rose outfit — was it for the Oaks? Anyway she has a pink coat, a pink lapel covered in pink roses, a hat full of pink roses and roses all over her umbrella.

She said people often asked her what she did with all those roses. 'Well, I will tell you. I took them off the coat, left it with a plain lapel and wore it for years. Then I used the roses to make a cover for a toilet seat.'

Columnist Claudia once wrote: 'This doesn't cut ice with the stuffy ladies in their proper silk coats and Thomas Harrison hats in the members' reserve. I have often longed to photograph the faces of the haughty members' wives as Lillian and some of the other exaggerated ladies promenade the lawns. They, of course, are as much in competition for the camera as Lillian.'

Does back-biting, does bitchery worry her?

'No,' she said, 'you have a sense of humour and you have so many friends, it doesn't matter. I tell you, what's the point in worrying about it. If somebody is nasty to you — forget it. It will hurt you more to worry about it. But that reminds me, I didn't tell you about my most beautiful year — the white fur coat.

'That was my first fur coat. I nagged Richard until I got it then I decided to go the whole hog — a full length white mink coat. Beautiful. There hadn't been one in Melbourne before. Oh yes, all the female claws came out.

'I went in it to the Cup and I overhead some women walking behind me "Of course that's not the best mink. They are male skins, I can tell. Female skins are much better." Another lady said: "Lillian this is a beautiful fabric, synthetic of course, but what sort?"

'Then there was the lady who said to Richard, "I can understand Lillian wanting her first mink coat but white should be her LAST, not her first."'

Lillian says the coat has gone through many changes but she is still wearing it.

Lillian was born in Rangoon, Burma. Her parents were Jewish. Her father was born in Turkey and her mother, an Iraki came from Baghdad. Her mother belonged to the Solomon family, who were coffee merchants and a household name in Burma. They not only grew the coffee, but had 77 coffee-selling outlets.

There were six children, four girls and two boys. 'Father was a school teacher,' said Lillian, 'and a bit of a nomad. He was a very clever man and he spoke eleven languages. I used to speak Arabic, English, Burmese, Hindustani and when I was being a bit stubborn I used to answer in French.

But do you know how my father met my mother. He was giving her private lessons in English. According to family legend it was all arranged for her to marry another merchant, but they eloped. There was a great fuss. The brothers were furious. They beat dad up when he returned. Eventually though he went into the family coffee business.

'Then the Japanese invaded. Somehow my father thought it would be all right. Somehow the British would look after everything. Then he heard that the Japanese soldiers were raping the women. Well, with four daughters! They were only forty miles away when we decided to go. My mother had so much jewellery, so many beautiful things. Most of it had to be left behind.

'We walked sixty or seventy miles and then caught a boat. We were very nearly the last people out of Burma. Our boat was torpedoed and we were just able to limp into Assam. From there we went to Calcutta and then on to London.'

She went to school for three years in Bombay, did a hair dressing course in London, and came to Australia in 1953.

When did she develop this thousand watt personality. 'Darling, I was never any different. Even when I was nine I would get the lead parts in all the school plays. My voice was terrible and my parents would wince when they heard me on opening nights. But still I got the parts. In Burma every year they had a poppy day, a very big thing. The girl who sold the most poppies won a special prize. Yes, little Lillian, putting her head on one side was very good at it and I won. The prize was to have tea with the Governor.

'I have never been able to drink tea and when I met the great man I had to behave and drink this tea. I will never forget trying to choke it down. It was very funny.'

So she tried her energy on the Melbourne scene and went to work for Elizabeth Arden. 'I asked for £25 a week, they gave me £9 and I was on trial. Things were unbelievable in those days. They had never heard of tips, never heard of rollers. There was a new hair-do in London called Tweezy Weezy. We got a schoolgirl, did her hair in beige and then we collected milk bottle tops. That was the only way to get silver paper and we did the tips. Helmut Newton took the pictures and next day we had the middle pages of the Sun.

'I got my £25 and it meant so much more business for Elizabeth Arden they had to put on two more junior girls.

I was — how do you call it — incorrigible, shrewd, cunning. I did a champagne rinse — they had never heard of that before — the picture was in the Argus. Then when the Olympic Games were on in 1956 we dressed a model in a Roman toga — and you know the five Olympic rings? We made her hair into five rings all different colors. In those days we couldn't get the colors, so we used vegetable dyes. Five front pages we had out of that — WOW!'

When the Russians put a man into space Lillian did a hair-do in the shape of a Sputnik, complete with antennae. When Jackie Kennedy came into fame as wife of John F. Kennedy, President of the United States she did the Jackie Kennedy look.

'I always liked to pick on the topical thing and I did this on myself, a lovely black page boy. The picture appeared in Truth. Oh dear, it shared the front page with the headline HUSBAND BASHES WIFE. Of course, people don't read and immediately jumped to the one conclusion. Richard was so cross. He never lived it down.'

She met and married Richard Frank in Melbourne. He had an even more terrifying escape than Lillian. Born in Warsaw, almost every country he went to seemed to be over-run. In turn he escaped to Lithuania, Japan, Shanghai. He said: 'I have been bombed by the Germans, Russians, Japanese, British, Americans — and even the Poles.'

When he arrived in Melbourne he started as a shoe salesman in Myers, then worked as a waiter, started his own coffee shop and so he progressed until he became one of the city's most successful restauranteurs with 'Petty Sessions' in Collins Street.

Lillian stayed with Elizabeth Arden several years then went into partnership with Antonio Zappalla in the city under the name of Lillian and Antonio. In 1969 Lillian and Antonio went their separate ways and she opened Lillian's in Toorak.

The opening was another Lillian spectacular done on behalf of the Baker Research Institute appeal. She had a Turkey breakfast which included for refreshment, 'Turkish blood' — champagne and burgundy. Then the top society names took part in the fashion parade. According to the Age Mrs Rodney Morell, Mrs Adrian Gibson, the Prime Minister's secretary Ainsley Gotto, Mrs Peter Lynch, Barbara Guest, Mrs Andrew Peacock, Mrs Ian Rice 'drifted down the stage wearing fabulous clothes and the latest hair styles. Mrs John Scarborough appeared in long, blonde hair and a black broadtail coat with monkey fur flicking round her ankles.'

Some girls appeared in literally nothing, but the climax came, continued the Age, when four black stalwarts from the local gymnasium carried a bier down the stairs. 'It was no mean task because seated cross-legged on the bier was a gorgeous girl swathed in Cellophane tied with yards and yards of bright cotton. Then lo! From the bier rose Mrs Nicky Holt draped in yards of floating black chiffon which ill-concealed the stocking-clad body underneath.' The salon was open.

When Lillian was around the salon was never dull or quiet. One time her friend Claudia Wright was doing her radio talk back show on 3AW. She was talking about kept mistresses and a Sydney journalist had claimed that top Australian members of Parliament and even Cabinet Ministers kept mistresses in Canberra.

Lillian said: 'I had a salon full of women, we were listening and when we became bored we turned off the hair blowers and I spoke to Claudia. I muffled my voice and told her that I was the kept mistress of two influential Australians because I had been hurt by my husband. I wasn't serious. I know a lot of women who have affairs but usually a mistress is a temporary relationship.'

Claudia was annoyed. She guessed it was Lillian having a little fun and although she did not name her on the programme she said on air: 'Go strangle yourself on one of your mink coats.'

Lillian has had her triumphs. In 1967 the BBC was celebrating the launching of color television and it did a series of 12 programmes on Australia. One of the twelve was on Richard and Lillian Frank. A camera team followed them for ten days and her segment was titled 'She Rather Stood Out In A Crowd.' That was Richard's comment when he first met her.

The same year she went on a tour to Paris, Rome, New York and London on a trip sponsored by L'Oreal of Paris. The sensation of that show was a lovely model nearly six feet tall, who stepped out in a black and white bikini made entirely of human hair.

Lillian has lost count of the number of door knocks she has performed, the number of charities she has helped. She received the OBE for her efforts in 1977.

But one of the strangest escapades was in 1978. She became the publicity agent, the ideas expert, for a world title fight during Melbourne's Moomba Festival.

When asked about this she said: 'You see, I know Rocky Mattioli, the world champion. He took out one of the girls in my salon, a very nice boy. And I like boxing Darling. I don't like it when they hurt each other, but I think it is very exciting. I used to adore TV Ringside. Four or five of my friends have money in this fight and they asked me to give them a hand.

'Yes, we are taking the sweat thing out of boxing. There will be champagne and caviar at the ringside and there will be a Meet-the-Champion reception after the fight. All the wives MUST come along. Why should a world title fight be an all-male affair.'

Melbourne columnist Ian Mackay commented; 'what's a nice girl like Lillian doing mixed up in a world title fight?'

What indeed. At least its a change from the Melbourne Cup.
