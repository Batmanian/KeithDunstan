Percy Wills Cerutty

22 min read.

First published 1980

Profile of athletics coach Percy Cerutty, the Stotan philosopher of Portsea Camp who drove Herb Elliott to Olympic gold on raw oatmeal, sandhill sprints and a diet of pain, publicity and provocation.

HERB ELLIOTT'S BOOK The Golden Mile carries this little saga on Percy Cerutty's Portsea Camp. It was written by one of the inmates, Bill Stacey.

A runner stood at the Pearly Gates,
His face was worn and old,
He bravely asked the man of fate
Admission to the fold.
'What have you done' St Peter said,
'To seek admission here?'
'I trained at Portsea, that was my task,
For many and many a year'.
Then wide the gates did open,
The angels clanged their bell,
'Come in and take a harp,' he said.
'You've had enough of hell'.

One day in June 1959 I called at Percy Cerutty's camp at 6.30 a.m. This was a most civilised hour to call on Mr Cerutty, already training activity was going apace and we went down to the beach to the acknowledged rack of pain, the sand dune course. Now the Everest of sand dunes at Portsea was a glistening white peak, eighty feet high with a gradient that seemed worse than the Matterhorn.

How unnerving it was to be confronted at the bottom by the master, the most unorthodox coach Australia had seen. At 64 he had curly white hair, a fine moustache and a tiny tuft of beard just under his bottom lip. If you tried to pinch a piece of flesh anywhere on his body it wouldn't have worked. He looked like an ageing romantic lead of the nineteen thirties, who had been progressively starved.

But his manner was electric. He had the look in his pale blue eyes that reminded one of the super evangelist. Shouting against the icy wind he said: 'Russell Mockridge holds the record. He's been up there in eleven seconds'.

'Russell Mockridge?'

'Why not? A professional cyclist has to be FIT. But Herb Elliott has the endurance record. He has been up and down at high speed forty-two consecutive times'. His ice blue eyes were now glaring at me. 'Come on you can do it too. RUN not walk'.

Already he was away, half hopping, half running, bony elbows swinging high, stick-like legs making incredible angles against the sand. 'Clench your fists, work your arms,' he shouted. 'Come on. Come on. You can do it. Uh-uh-uh'.

The theory behind it was this — here was the most agonising form of locomotion that could be devised for a human. The feet sank into the soft fine sand way beyond the ankle, so not only was the angle of climb appalling but one always appeared to be pushing through an unyielding bog. Cerutty believed that once free of such restrictions the feet would take off with all the speed of one of those petroleum ads.

Once at the top the idea was to stand there, admire the view and appear as if one's breath would not blow out a candle. Mine would have put out the Great Fire of London. Percy pointed out the tracks up and over the sand dunes where the athletes had been training in the early morning. 'The old system was to train until it hurts then give it away.' He curled his lip to indicate how effete he thought that was. 'Now we realise that only when the pain begins is the training starting to take effect.'

Herb Elliott wrote: 'Thrust against pain', Percy once told me. 'Pain is the purifier. He introduced me to every book every written about Francis of Assisi and said: 'Walk towards suffering. Love suffering. Embrace it.

After running round the sand dunes Percy then called on the most purifying act of all, strip off all clothes and run naked into the sea. Always this had been part of the Cerutty system. It was important for an athlete to spend part of the day in the nude. 'No matter how cold it is you must expose your body to the air at least once a day'. This toughening taught the body how to provide its own protection against chills.

The idea hadn't been entirely loved at Portsea. One year an ardent moralist observed their nude athletics through a telescope then complained to the police.

He had a name for this way of life. They were Stotans and a Stotan was a cross between a Stoic and Spartan. Later Percy gave me a roneoed copy of the Spartan creed:

1. Realization that, as Wordsworth the poet says, 'Life is real, life is earnest', which denotes there is no time for wasteful ideas and pursuits.

2. In place of wasteful hobbies there commences a period of supervised and systematic physical training, together with instruction in the art of living fully. This replaces previously undirected life.

3. Swimming will be done all the year round. It is obligatory to swim in the open at least once every month. This especially strengthens the will and builds resistance to quitting the task ahead.

4. The programme implies the cessation of late hours. Amusements both social and entertaining, should be reduced to a minimum and then only in the nature of relaxation from strenuous work. This was their schedule for the day:

7 a.m. Five mile run before breakfast.
8 a.m. Health food breakfast.
9 a.m. Swimming and surfing, horses, chopping wood.
Noon. Training and lectures at Portsea Oval followed by another swim.
2 p.m. Lunch, fish and fresh fruit.
3 p.m. Siesta.
5 p.m. Ten mile run along dirt roads, ending at beach.
7 p.m. Tea and general discussion led by Percy on wide variety of subjects.

Another thing I learned about the Stotans, Percy did not approve of rubbing down. There was something terrible about one male rubbing down another. He wasn't even keen on hand shaking. All this was laid down in his book:

'Here at Portsea, I teach the athletes never to permit another to touch them, much less support them. We do not like all this hand-shaking and back-slapping. The human personality is sacred and to me the only thing we can claim that is our own. So I resent other males making physical contact in any way. I have no such reservations with the female of the species, of course. I have seen lads drop exhausted at Portsea. We leave them where they drop. They recover and walk in, or they stop there. No other hand is permitted to assist, commiserate or congratulate.'

Breakfast was a little late, at around 8.30 a.m. and here he proved himself more than a decade ahead of the muesli eaters and all the trendy health food shops. He provided raw oatmeal, nuts, dried apricots and raisins, plus sliced banana.

When one suggested that the dish was a little dry and might be improved by milk there was a terrible look of scorn. 'Milk is for babies, not grown men like you'.

Milk was one of his favorite themes. He told a group of Adelaide University Students: 'Milk psychologically has a terrible effect on the male animal. It should be banned very early in life. Addiction to milk make a boy mother-drawn. The deep parts of his consciousness retain the link with his mother and once he has the taste for milk he will never develop properly as an individual'. That was why Australia was becoming a matriarchal society. He even suggested: 'I would close every dairy farm in the country and put the farmers on a pension'.

He talked about live food and dead food. Live food was fresh fruit and vegetables. 'If a bloke eats cooked food all his cells are replaced by hopeless ones'. At 73 he announced: 'I live where others die. You ask me how I do it? Well, I never eat pies, buns, pastries and that sort of junk. And I don't smoke. Let the smokers and the gluttons die. Nicotine should be made free so the idiot smokers can die all the quicker, and leave living to us who know how to use it'.

In our school days we used to see the crew and the first Eighteen fed on a diet of thick steaks. 'Steaks — utterly ridiculous', said Percy. 'For meat choose, fish, fowl and chicken and with that I have a lettuce leaf, two or three black olives, perhaps a pickled onion and perhaps a tomato... and as for cooked vegetables, fancy putting that stuff in your guts'.

He once told Harry Gordon: 'The trouble is that I was born two thousand years too late. I should have been a messenger in the Roman Army, that was my real vocation. If they needed a man to take a message across mountains and rivers, through fire and flood and drought, I would have been their man and I would have got through every time'.

Percy was no Roman, he was born in humble, working class Prahran. He grew up a thin, sickly young man and it was only single-minded determination that made him a good runner. Malcolm Boyd was the star miler of the day and he beat him in 4min 32sec. He did get an invitation to train for the 1920 Olympic Games, but his health was not good enough, and besides he was filled with self doubts. He married a girl who lived in the same street in Malvern, and became a telephone technician for the Melbourne Post Office. In 1939 he had a nervous breakdown and as he recalled later, it was a terrible time. He couldn't perform simple tasks, he couldn't even drive a car, so he went on six months sick leave.

This gave him a waiting time, a period when he could think out a new life. He spent hours wandering along the banks of the Yarra and on Sundays he went to church where he had once been a chorister. Just to help him get back his nerve he started swimming at the St Kilda Baths. The big test was diving. He tackled higher and higher dives until he was brave enough to go off from the top of the high tower. It took him two years to conquer that one.

He read philosophy, religion, and he put himself through all sorts of private endurance tests like climbing mountains, and weight lifting. He had a theory that a man should be able to lift his own weight. Out on his balcony he lifted a 108lb weight 62 times, straining, pushing, face all screwed up, until Mrs Cerutty cried: 'Stop, stop! You'll kill yourself'.

At forty-eight he started marathon running, and he did over a 1000 miles a year in training. He was fifty-one when he set a Victorian native record for 26 miles 385 yd in 2 hr 58 min. One of his most astonishing runs was in 1945 when he was fifty. He did 100 miles in 23 hrs 45 min, then he ran another mile just to make sure there had been no mistake that he had done the 100 in under 24 hours. He began at 8 a.m. near Cann River, by the 292 mile post on Princes Highway, and after having run all night, completed 50 miles at 6.47 a.m. His only break was a forty-eight-minute stop at Orbost for a bath and breakfast. His feet became so sore he was just able to hobble the last twenty miles.

Percy decided that he would not go back to the Post Office to become a nonentity, he would become a coach and he made an elaborate study of how animals use their muscles. He studied gazelles, apes, panthers, leopards and he even looked at the stuffed model of Phar Lap at the Melbourne museum.

He said: 'Unable to sleep I started going to Caulfield racecourse in the early mornings. There I watched the horses exercising. I talked to trainers, stableboys, and jockeys, and learned most of what I know about athletics and how to run'.

At Portsea he started with a collection of packing cases and finished with a well-ordered training camp. A great stream of people passed through, not merely runners but sportsmen of all kinds — John Landy, Herb Elliott, Alby Thomas, Jimmy Carruthers, Tom Hafey, Margaret Court, Betty Cuthbert, Russell Mockridge, Hayden Bunton jnr. and Don Macmillan were some of the names.

Athletes like Dave Stephens, Les Perry, Mike Agostini and ultimately John Landy found his volatile, egocentric, steam power personality too much to bear. His followers, athletic officials and particularly newspaper editors were never sure whether he was a crackpot or a genius.

But of course the triumph, the star of his life was Herb Elliott. Herb accepted all the dried oatmeal, pounding up and down sandhills, leaping into the sea, Aristotle, Pliny and H.G. Wells. In order to improve his mind Percy even pressed into Herb's hand a copy of the Sermon on the Mount.

The Cerutty system was to needle, goad, insult. When John Landy was showing what Percy believed was insufficient killer spirit he would run behind him and shout 'imposter'. Part of the goad treatment was to make public announcements in the Press. In September 1959 a year before the Rome Olympics he claimed that Herb Elliott had lost the will to run. He said: 'He'll have a terrific job making Australia's team for Rome. Herb has got lazy. On his rare visits to Portsea he has just wasted his time. I have written to Herb one of the most heated notes I have written to anyone'.

Elliott did rebel and even laugh in Percy's face but he always remained a disciple and he wrote: "Percy helped me to world records not so much by improving my technique but by releasing in my mind and soul a power that I only vaguely thought existed".

The climax, the triumph of Cerutty's life was Herb Elliott's gold medal for the 1500 metres at the Rome Olympics. On the great day Percy made a pact with Herb. He would stand by the track and if Elliott was close to world record time he would wave a towel over his head. As it turned out this was not as easy as he anticipated. There were 90,000 cheering spectators and Percy was just a tiny blob in the crowd.

As Herb Elliott strode out in front, winning with ridiculous ease, 600 metres to go, it was obvious that he would break a world record. Percy burst out of the crowd, jumped a four foot moat, tore off his shirt, and waved it over his head. Two burly Italian policemen, cocked hats, braid and all, went after him. One grabbed him round the waist, but Percy fought him and still waved. Then the two manhandled him off the ground.

Elliott won by nearly 20 metres. His time was 3min 35.6sec, 0.4 below his previous record. Percy said: "I loved him as few men can really love their sons, since Herb was both son and mate... As Herb approached the exit I walked out on the grass verdure to greet him. This time no comic opera police intervened. I put my arms around his shoulders and kissed his cheeks, the only way I ever knew to show my pleasure and affinity".

Twelve days later Elliott was running in Sweden and the crowd sat back fully expecting another world record, but Elliott had suffered an exhausting few weeks with races in Rome, London and Cardiff. He won this 1500 metres in 3min 38.4sec, but it was a narrow thing. He was only inches in front of the Swedish champion Dan Waern.

Elliott commented: 'I just did not have it. When I wanted to sprint I could not.'

One Swedish official was very unhappy. He complained to Cerutty: 'You promised us a world record. You announced to the Swedish newspapers that nothing could stop Elliott from running a world record'.

Cerutty grabbed him by the beard and replied: 'You've just seen the third fastest 1500 metres in history. Elliott has run the three fastest miles ever and the three fastest 1500s. Yet you have the audacity to complain when he doesn't do more. Maybe you should provide him with more opposition. Next time I won't just pull your beard I'll punch your nose'.

The official stunned said: 'But you promised...'

Cerutty then said disdainfully: 'Even the greatest prophets can't always be right' and he walked away.

One never knew what to expect when travelling with Percy. Herb Elliott tells the story of a civic reception given to the Australian team in Ireland after John Landy had broken the Irish mile record. There were so many of these functions and they never varied. At a garden party prior to the formalities the introductions were going very nicely, when all attention seemed to be focussed on a very strange happening in the centre of the lawn — Percy was standing on his head. They asked him why he did it and he said: 'I like to be different'.

The rows at the Olympic and Commonwealth Games Villages were not only regular — they were expected. Percy always considered it his right to move in and out as he pleased and even live with the athletes. At Cardiff he happily settled in, wore a green and white Australian team track suit, which he had no right to wear and breakfasted with the athletes. Bill Young, the Australian team manager was so angry he said: 'If I have any more trouble from Cerutty I'll personally frog-march him straight out the gate".

To Percy publicity was like a habit-forming drug. He found it irresistible and he could not get enough of it. He was always writing letters to the newspapers, sending messages to columnists, and he would accept any invitation to go on television. Once a microphone or typewriter was turned in his direction he could be guaranteed to put on a good show.

One of his more spectacular efforts was the day he appeared on Brisbane's Meet the Press. Reg Leonard was the chairman. The questioning went like this: 'What is more important in training, diet or exercise?'

'Both diet and exercise are important to living a full life. Most news editors die before they are fifty. I'm a fighter and not merely a talker'.

'Do you think this living on rugged lines really does any good?'

'I've lived like it all my life and will continue to live like it until I die. At school I had more fist fights than any other boy. I've had more fights than any other man I know'.

'How will Jimmy Carruthers fare in his return to the ring?'

'Give Jimmy a go', said Percy and challenging Reg Leonard he said, 'You get into the ring and see how you go. I'll clean you up'.

Reg Leonard: 'You might get a surprise if you did'.

'I would not get a surprise, I would eat you', answered Percy.

It all finished with Percy saying to Leonard: 'I'll pull your nose for you... it might be the first time on television, but I'll do it'.

The next day he told reporters: 'If the incident had developed into a fight I would have killed the four of them. When I go into action, I am a fighting tornado until I am stopped'.

His comments on women in sport provided a constant flow of copy for twenty years and he had many a marvellous interview with an aggressive female reporter. In New Zealand he said: 'The ancient Greeks banned women from the original Olympic Games and the same principle should apply today. Any sport for women should have been kept in the Victorian era, when no one took it seriously. Their efforts are ridiculous and sometimes lowering when compared with the performances of men... They cannot hope to keep it up without surrendering their sex. The effect of super effort on hormone balance is well known... Some women are little more than twenty-two stone monstrosities thrown up as women by other countries. If women are to train as arduously as men they will have muscular stringy limbs, hard faces, arrested bust development and unwomanly shapes. Who wants straight-legged, narrow-hipped, big-shouldered, powerful women, aggressive and ferocious in physique and attitude?'

There were angry cries all around the world. Desmond Hackett, an English sports columnist accused him of being a cad and a bounder. He said that Percy's springtime fancies obviously turned only to thoughts of stop watches and he had never run his eye over the graceful lines of Britain's female athletes.

He relented in later years and particularly he showed his admiration for Betty Cuthbert and some fine photographs were published depicting him running his eye over Betty's graceful legs. Frequently he announced his love for all women and even at 79 he said he had a better sex life than men 50 years his junior.

He was ingenious at getting himself into print. He loved to taunt the footballers. He claimed that at sixty-three years of age he could beat any Melbourne footballer over the mile. He challenged any of them to run the mile in five minutes.; 'I ran a batch of Western Australian footballers into the ground after only three-quarters of a mile... no footballer has proved me wrong. I'd say that only 25 per cent of our footballers are as fit as I am. You can have your weather-beaten cowboys, for gameness give me bike riders'.

No footballer did prove him wrong. The best time any of them recorded was 5 min 10 sec. Several times, including Richmond called him, hoping that the Cerutty steam would fire them to better things.

And there was always a new Cerutty story. Harry Hopman, coach of Davis Cup tennis players told the story that when Percy Cerutty returned from the 1952 Olympic Games he booked in to the Botanical Hotel but he had a whim to spend his first night back on Australian soil under the stars in the Domain where he had run hundreds of miles in training.

So he took his rucksack, sleeping bag and tent and wandered about till he found a quiet spot near Government House gates. He was awakened at 1 a.m. by a constable who assumed he was a deadbeat without money for a bed.

Percy was in trouble. The constable had never heard the name Cerutty. Percy was in his running-gear so he had no driving licence or anything that would prove who he was. Fortunately he had some money, which saved him from a vagrancy charge and the policeman let him go. He looked too dangerous a customer to have near Government House, but the constable allowed him to sleep further down the Domain, which was handy for Percy when he started his run at 7 a.m.

This made news and again Percy thrived on it. Percy always boasted of his energy and his sexual drives, his superb way with women. Yet domestically he was often a gentle, kindly character. He married twice. From the first marriage there was a son and this boy never accepted the tough Stotan regime of his father. His marriage to Dorothy was an on-off affair with continual partings and re-unitings, and finally in 1954 there was a divorce.

In 1955 Percy married again to Nancy, born in Reading, England. She had two daughters, Thelma and Elaine. They met when Percy came to speak at her adult education class. In those days she was cheerful, rotund and quite un-Stotan. But she did learn to eat raw oats and raisins, go for long hikes, jump on the trampoline and she was the manager, the cook and most of all the mother to the hundreds of athletes who went through the Portsea camp.

At a time when he should have been quietly retiring Percy pushed on. At seventy he ran to the top of Tasmania's Mt. Wellington, 4166 feet. He left Hobart Post Office at 7 a.m. and he was on top of the mountain by 10 a.m.

'It was a 10 mile trip and I walked and jogged', said Percy. 'I was happy with the time. I did the same thing 20 years ago and it took me one and a half hours, which was not bad for a fifty-year-old'.

Even so at seventy-four he was not running as often as in the old days. He indulged more in long walks and he confessed that he was a little tired of running up and down sandhills. On 18 February 1969 a press release went to all newspaper offices. He had gone into business partnership with Michael A. Riddell, managing director of Michael Riddell International Pty., Ltd.

The press release started with quotes from Herb Elliott and an American writer, Dave Clark, who had spent a year at the Portsea Camp. The Elliott quote described Percy Cerutty as 'a genius, a man whose intellect and perception far exceeds the bounds of sport. Ninety-five per cent of what Percy taught me at Portsea had nothing to do with sport or athletics'.

Dave Clarke: 'I must admit that I do honestly feel that Percy Cerutty could, in many ways, be the most important man in the world today, because I feel that the people of the world would find this man's ideas and teachings more essential for survival itself, than the ideas or teachings of any other man living or dead'.

The press release went on to say: 'Does Australia realise, understand, and appreciate the worth of Percy Cerutty's genius; or do we have to wait 20 to 200 years after his death to wake up? Percy Cerutty is not only an internationally renowned sporting coach, sportsman, and maker of champions, he is also a PHILOSOPHER, WRITER (author of six books published world-wide), TEACHER, POET, LECTURER, TELEVISION PERSONALITY — an intellectually brilliant 74-year-old, who is now commencing a new phase of his life as that of a businessman."

Percy suggested that he would become a millionaire. 'I'm out to make money, big money — and I'll do it' he said. The press release said that he would write books, publish his poetry and essays, have a national television programme, have a lecture series and perhaps even publish a magazine.

Percy did not become a millionaire and he did not achieve all these aims. At seventy-nine he announced that doctors had confirmed his heart, lungs and blood pressure were those of a very fit man half his age and he said it was a certainty that he would live to be a hundred.

On his eightieth birthday he said: 'I am not dying. I'm more alive than ever. I can still run a mile on the track, how many at my age can do that? — No they just die. I never met a doctor with real brains — they are all trained by idiots in universities. They nearly killed me when I was in my late thirties with the drugs they prescribed.

"Now I weigh nine stone four pounds and I don't eat much, but I'm alive. No man needs to have a heart attack. It's the bloody food he eats and the smoking. I would like to earn hundreds of thousands of dollars to get back to America and other places to help ordinary businessmen keep alive'.

Was Percy Cerutty a genius or a crackpot, the indicator fell more towards the first than the second. He sacrificed to be different. At the end of his book Sport Is My Life he wrote: 'I merely followed 'the gleam' as it seemed to be in front of me. That has led me, this 'mad' gleam, these 'crazy' ideas and philosophies, away from my business ambitions, my contentment with the status quo, in my own life, or as I observed it around me. That it led me to deliberately experience poverty, loneliness, and the masochistic pleasures of lifting thousands of tons of iron fixed to barbells, of running thousands of miles in the heat, the cold, even the snow, of traversing the mountain fastnesses of my country, and the streets and countrysides of many cities and many countries of the world — and all, or most, after the age of forty-five years — when as Paul suggests, I had found, through sickness and misery, pain and disillusionments, that the "ideas of the child were useless"'.

On 14 August, 1975, eight months after his eightieth birthday, at the Portsea Camp his very energetic old heart finally gave up.

His death notices told of his sense of humor: 'I hope I'll be convicted of adultery at one hundred", he used to say. And his philosophy went beyond the mere business of record breaking.

Perhaps his favorite line was: 'What's the use of a body that can run a four-minute mile if the brain can't appreciate the beauty of a sunset across the sea'.
