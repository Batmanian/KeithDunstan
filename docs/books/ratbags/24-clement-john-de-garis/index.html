Clement John De Garis

22 min read.

First published 1980

Profile of Clement John De Garis, the brilliant Mildura publicist behind the Sun-Rayed dried fruits campaign, whose spectacular schemes at Pyap and Kendenup collapsed into bankruptcy, a faked drowning, and eventual suicide in 1926.

CLEMENT JOHN DE GARIS, just possibly, was the most brilliant public relations man Australia has seen. He had a charming smile, clear eyes, classic good looks and the figure of a schoolboy athlete. Always his clothes were superbly cut, and he had a spellbinding spiel, which made men want to believe in him, follow him, and most remarkable of all, give him money. Bank managers gave it to him in tens of thousands.

Yet it would be unfair to say that he had an insatiable desire for money. The devil in C.J. De Garis was an insatiable desire for recognition. More than anything he wanted to be noticed. He adored publicity. He wanted the world to know about his exploits and his extraordinary achievements and this he did very well. The initials 'C.J.' were instantly recognisable and between 1920 and 1926 they were rarely out of the headlines.

C.J. was born in Mildura in 1884 and unquestionably he inherited some of his Evangelistic zeal from his Wesleyan father, a dignified bearded gentleman, who did almost everything in the irrigation colony. He left the church to become an auctioneer, estate agent, storekeeper and packing shed proprietor. Young Clement learned about business very early, working in his father's store, then he gained the polish of the entrepreneur by spending five years at Wesley College, Melbourne.

His philosophy was 'Difficulties were made to overcome' and he didn't believe in wasting a second. As soon as he came from school he became a partner in his father's firm and within two years he was managing director. He was just 26 when he made a foray down to Melbourne and turned his sunny smile on the Melbourne bankers. He charmed them to such a degree they gave him an overdraft of £15,000 and he used this to build the Sarnia Packing Pty. Ltd. a packing shed, which made obsolete all the old methods in Mildura.

Here was a packing shed for dried fruits which used modern assembly line methods. He convinced growers that they should sell their crops to him and Sarnia was an instant success.

Two years later C.J. went to Melbourne again and this time he talked the bankers into giving him an overdraft of £22,000. It was a salesman's effort of considerable merit. He wanted to buy the old Pyap Village Estate. The South Australian Government already had lost £25,000 on Pyap. It was a depressing 150 acres of what appeared to be dead, browned, grape vines. De Garis happily told the bankers he would convert 'a wilderness into a garden'. His own investment in Pyap was only £1,300.

He was attacked by the skeptics. They said if the South Australian Government could not make Pyap bloom like a rose, how could this hare-brained young man revive it? But his system was this, he spent big on installing a very advanced overhead spraying system and the supposedly dead vines all revived and produced heavy crops of grapes.

He established what would be described today as a commune or kibbutz. He built stone cottages for 85 settlers, put in a school, a library, appointed a business manager and introduced an extraordinary idea for keeping up population in the area. He was several generations ahead of welfare-minded Governments. He provided a baby bonus system — every mother who produced a baby on the Pyap Estate received a cash bonus of three guineas.

Now everything he touched was a success. He started a furniture company, a garage, launched the Sunraysia Daily Newspaper, but his most fascinating exercise was the way he pushed sultanas, currants and lexias down the throats of almost every Australian.

In October 1918 the Australian Dried Fruits Association appointed him director of publicity and from then on they observed his activities with almost shuddering amazement.

His brief was to spend an eighth of a penny per £1 for each estimated crop. C.J. in his first year spent £31,000 and went over his budget by £11,000. The trouble was the growers had long ignored the Australian market, London was so easy. Over there their crops could fetch better prices, furthermore in 1919 an armada of ships was bringing the A.I.F. home from the Great War. Shipping space going the other way was convenient and cheap.

The happy growers, ignored all set quotas, produced more and more raisins, but suddenly the ships were no longer available. There was a record harvest along the Murray and there was nowhere for it to go, except at dumping prices to the distilleries.

Many of the growers thought they were ruined and sold their farms to De Garis. He bought them on condition that first they signed a document, which stated that he had advised them against selling.

From then on he started a public relations drive which makes all modern campaigns seem pallid by comparison.

First he went to all the big cities and put up his teasers, hand bills and hoardings which announced: WANTED A NAME.

This teasing of public curiosity was new, every lamp post, every telegraph pole, every vacant brick wall carried this extraordinary message. Then, just when everybody was talking about it, C.J. revealed the secret.

He wanted a name for Mildura dried fruits and he was prepared to pay £50 for the best name. He received elaborate names that went to a 100 words, he received passionate verse but the winner was Sun-Rayed.

He splashed that name everywhere. He put it on kites over football grounds, on slides in movie theatres, on tram tickets, on balloons, he sent circulars all round the country through the mail. He did his job so well mothers even began naming their children Lexia, and children at fancy dress balls dressed up as sultanas.

He had the Sun-Rayed recipe book, he commissioned the Sun-Rayed Waltz and he even had the Sun-Rayed kettle holder.

His greatest idea was the big stunt. He called a public gathering in Mildura, revealing nothing before hand, except that something 'huge' was about to happen. The Big Stunt turned out to be another competition, £1000 first prize plus a further £1500 for 1600 other prizes, very big money for the nineteen twenties.

The contest was nationwide and contestants had to guess accurately the number of raisins and lexias in one pound packets and the number of sultanas in a two pound packet.

Three Justices of the Peace right there in the theatre weighed out a pound of currants, a pound of lexias and two pounds of sultanas. They sealed them in their packets and a local bank manager locked them in a vault.

The next week-end half Australia was buying currants, lexias and sultanas and counting them on the kitchen table. The mail was so great at the Mildura Post Office they had to double the staff. There was even a suggestion: 'Why not send all the Mildura mail to C.J.'s office then he could send back any that was not his. It would save a lot of time.' The post office did not agree to that idea.

It seemed that nothing could go wrong for C.J. De Garis. He opened dried fruit cafes in every capital city. These he staffed with Mildura girls which he described as the most beautiful in Australia. It was all working — in three years dried fruit sales increased by 5000 tons and fruit lands increased in value by £4,500,000.

Newspapers printed pictures of his office desk. It was something to see. It had dictophones, telephones, buzzers and colored lights, gadgetry no-one in Mildura had ever seen before.

He had a stable of fast cars and these he used to drive at high speed. One of his old friends wrote: 'I knew Mr. De Garis as a keen motorist, whose high-speed tours through wild bush land I have not seen bettered. His many record-breaking non-stop runs from Adelaide to Mildura were notoriously hair raising.

Eventually cars were not fast enough. One day an ex-Army pilot came to see him. Lieutenant F.S. Briggs. He had flown the Prime Minister, Mr W.M. Hughes, across the English Channel for the treaty negotiations at Versailles. Briggs explained that by flying he cut down a 15 hour car trip down to three hours and there was very little difference in the operating cost.

This was in 1920 when there were no airlines, when flying had to be a hazardous adventure in a machine of gummed down fabric held together with wires and braces. So in December of that year they made the first non stop flight between the capital cities. They flew from Perth to Melbourne and then in one single dramatic hop to Sydney, a remarkable feat for those times.

The aeroplane they used was a De Havilland DH4, and naturally it had the name 'Sunraysed'. The Argus described it as a light bomber, built just before the Armistice in 1918. It had a 375 horse power Rolls-Royce engine, the same type used to power the Vickers Vimy which carried Sir Ross Smith on his famous flight out from England.

On January 16, 1921 they claimed a world record, the longest flight ever in a single day. They left Brisbane at first light, 6.05 a.m., and landed by the light of flares at Broadmeadows, near Melbourne at 7.45 p.m. The Argus marvelled: 'Breakfast in Brisbane, morning tea in Grafton, lunch in Sydney, afternoon tea in Cootamundra and dinner in Melbourne'. The average speed was 105 m.p.h.

He carried messages for the Governor-General, Lord Forster, the Lieutenant-Governor, Sir William Irvine, the Prime Minister, Mr Hughes, and maybe this was the highlight of his career.

In March 1920 he had a bank balance of £72,000 but by May 1922 he declared he was flat 'broke' and he was borrowing large sums at 12 per cent. He fancied himself as a writer. He wrote plays, he wrote short stories. One play titled 'F.F.F.' lost £2000 and a publishing house which he operated went into the red for another £3000.

But the real source of his problems was a settlement in West Australia called Kendenup. Sometimes with a sad smile people preferred to call it Endenup. De Garis found it on one of his transcontinental flights. It was 200 miles from Perth and immediately C.J. began to dream that here was another Pyap, another Mildura even, 50,000 acres of wilderness that could be turned into a Garden of Eden.

His Melbourne bankers advised against it. They thought it a dangerous idea. Indeed his regular financial backers refused to put up any money on the grounds that Kendenup would take him away from Mildura.

But the affair that was to do him the greatest damage was peculiarly bizarre. A man who called himself J.G. Madison arrived in Mildura. He had a public relations style to rival C.J. himself. He wore an astrakhan coat, he had an American accent and with super confidence he arranged to address the Mayor and the citizens of Mildura at a public meeting. He told them that here was an area which should become a new Australian State. The citizens of Mildura, of course loved this idea, and quickly saw their town as a new State capital.

Madison announced that on their behalf he would approach the Prime Minister and the Imperial Government in London, all he required was a fee twice what De Garis was getting.

The meeting was adjourned until the following Saturday and he knew exactly how to handle his country audience. He opened it with prayer: 'Almighty God, our Heavenly father, Thou seest my life before Thee, an open book'. He told them that he was the brain that would lead Mildura to its destiny.

However this time De Garis was present. At the end of the meeting he asked Madison to answer two questions:

1. What accomplishments could he offer by way of reference?

2. Where had Madison been for the past two years?

Of course, he had been in jail. D.J. destroyed Madison for the moment, but he had made a very dangerous enemy. His name was not Madison, actually it was George Henry Cochrane but the next pseudonym he used was Grant Hervey and he settled in Mildura as the editor of a small newspaper.

In October, 1921 Hervey visited Melbourne with a packet of large posters which he pasted on buildings all round Melbourne. They read: GREAT SMASH KENDENUP RUINED. C.J.DE GARIS BANKRUPT.

De Garis tried to counter with a newspaper advertisement: 'Rewards. £1000 will be paid for information leading to the conviction of the person or persons who posted up untrue posters affecting me. This information required immediately. Reward will be paid on conviction to the first person supplying the necessary information.'

Hervey went around Melbourne in a large car with sheets of canvas on the side and back. Here in large red letters there was a series of questions and answers on the so-called financial situation in Kendenup. Said De Garis: 'The statements are absolutely false. I give an emphatic denial. I have been overwhelmed with expressions of confidence and goodwill.'

On the previous Thursday Hervey ran an attack on De Garis in his newspaper, the Mildura and Merbein Sun. When he returned home from Melbourne the local citizens were waiting for him. Nigh on 100 men and 20 motor cars assembled outside his house in Sarnia Avenue, but they found house barricaded and defended by the local police. But they bided their time.

At midday they learned that Hervey had telephoned for a car to take him to Ouyen, so several car loads of men went to a spot on Deakin Avenue, known as the Stovepipe, three kilometres from Mildura where the bridge went over an irrigation canal. When Hervey's car slowed down one of the men ran forward, grabbed Hervey and pulled him from the car. The others bound and gagged him, and with Hervey offering violent resistance off they went to the airport on the west side of Mildura. One of the attackers had his nose injured by one of Hervey's flying kicks.

When they got him to the airport they stripped him naked, and tarred him from head to foot, even putting tar on his hair. Then they covered him in feathers.

Having completed their small work of art they took away his clothes and left him to find his own naked way home.

Hervey took police action and 16 men were charged with conspiracy. However defence counsel was able to prove that De Garis had nothing to do with organising the tarring and feathering, it was a spontaneous local effort. Already Hervey had been in jail for forgery and there had been numerous acts of blackmail. One of his tricks was to force his clients into signing advertising contracts at four times the normal rate under threat of what he might write about them in his newspaper.

However, the 16 defendants were committed for trial and the case came on in Ballarat, where Mr Justice McArthur continually referred to 'the creature Hervey' and accused him of revolting hypocrisy. Maybe this was inspired by the lines from the witness box: 'I looked at them quietly for a moment and then knelt and prayed. I asked God to forgive them for their stupidity, ignorance and foolishness. They all appeared thunderstruck and stood there in silence.' Then somebody called 'Look out! The Johns are coming! Do a get.'

The 16 accused received a three months suspended jail sentence and were fined £25 each. All this appeared to be a great victory for De Garis but Hervey actually had done his job. No longer was it possible to sell debentures in the Kendenup project and the West Australian Government appointed a Royal Commission to examine what was going on there.

C.J.'s next move was to make a spectacular dash to the United States and he returned with all kinds of written promises from American financial institutions. Australian banks lent him money on the strength of this, but the American help never arrived, and in 1923 Kendenup went into bankruptcy. De Garis swore that he would spend the rest of his life paying off the debenture holders.

His next move to form the Melbourne Subdivision Company and his idea was a whole series of glorious housing schemes right through the outer suburbs. His methods were 50 years ahead of their time. In September 1924 the company put 2600 allotments up for sale at Corio Bay, near Geelong. The brochure said 'Corio is the place where the Bay, the Railroad and the Highway meet.' C.J. hired a special train and prospective buyers were invited on a free trip to inspect the land. Over 1100 accepted the idea of the free ride and aboard the train they received such gifts as a bottle of raisin butter, a packet of sultanas, a packet of cigarettes and a cigar.

There were 100 cars and several drags waiting to take them on tour at Corio and they received dinner. All sorts of local dignitaries were present and they were told that in 100 years the population of Geelong would be 500,000 and this new suburb would extend all the way to the You Yangs.

Had this been his only business adventure undoubtedly he would have become a millionaire, but the West Australian investment sapped away every penny. On January 5, 1925 his big black Packhard was found abandoned at Mentone beach. In the car was a new suit with hat and boots, 100 shares in De Garis Kendenup Co., several pieces of paper on which he had been composing poetry and the message: 'Whoever finds these clothes and this car please communicate with Mrs. C.J. De Garis, Wahgunyah, Rosebud and tell her to look in the small drawer of the chest of drawers in the bedroom.'

De Garis clearly wanted everyone to know he was going. He wrote over 60 personal letters to his friends. He had been married twice. His first marriage at 21 was to Rene Vera Corbould, a Mildura girl. There were three daughters from this marriage, then he married his secretary Violet Austin.

The letter to his wife began: 'Sweetheart Vysie: Enclosed letter has been written for some days, I have decided on drowning as the pleasantest and surest death. I am eager now for it all to be over. I am now drowning myself at Rosebud because I do not want the place to be associated with a tragedy in your memory. It has so many happy recollections... Hence fully dressed on Monday morning, but with my togs underneath, I will motor along the bay to some spot where I will soon be out of my depth — and end it all. It is the first time I have been glad that I cannot swim, for the end will only be a matter of moments, and everyone who has been nearly drowned tells me that it is a beautiful panorama of past events from which they dislike being awakened. Therefore my angel do not worry over me...'

He wrote another letter to his Reconstruction Committee and creditors, which went to thousands of words. It began: 'When this letter reaches you on Monday I will have passed beyond human reach, as I have definitely and irrevocably decided to end this terrific burden, which has sapped my mentality and warped my sense of right and wrong to such an extent that, except for my few hours at home (which have always been delightful), the life has become a perfect misery, and I had become a source of menace to my best friends, because of the warping of my financial sense, and my sudden incapacity to concentrate of the work in hand; I to whom work had always been a wonderful pleasure...

'If anyone ever tells you that the man who commits suicide is a coward you can emphatically deny it. It takes more courage to die than to live, and I have only determined to die because I see by that means I benefit everybody and remove an obstruction.'

For days the police searched the foreshore near Rosebud but there was no sign of a body. Then the police noticed something peculiar. The Packhard was the very latest model and had what the Press described as a new safety device, a switch key. Surely De Garis would not have carried this key with him into the water. So they issued an official description of him which was issued to police in all States. 'Age: 40, height 5ft 9in. Athletic build, square shoulders, clean shaven, brown hair, turning grey and thin at the temples. He was dressed neatly but a description of his clothes cannot be given.'

On January 8 the police issued a warrant for his arrest and Truth carried the derisive headline DE GARIS GETAWAY WITH TIDY NEST EGG OF BORROWED QUIDLETS — Frenzied Financier's Fantastic Finance Finished.

The same day the Age carried a remarkable story from its Sydney representative. A man who was obviously De Garis had booked into a hotel at Mortlake, a little suburb eight miles from Sydney. 'He was a well-rounded, stoutish man and wore American glasses. He had much money and ordered a room. Having made himself comfortable, the new guest in a strong American twang, said he had come to investigate the areas of land around the district. He said he represented a syndicate that took a great interest in the eradication of prickly pear and the growth of passion and like fruits. He would do much for the district.'

He was lavish in his tips, half a crown under the plate at every meal and he gave five shillings to the cook.

He gained the tubby look by putting blankets under his shirt, he was wearing a wig, and the American accent was a little too obvious. However by the time the police moved in De Garis already had boarded a boat for New Zealand.

He was arrested as soon as he stepped off the liner Maheno at Auckland. He was travelling under the name of Leslie and even had it branded in big letters on his luggage. The press all described him as being in splendid health and the most buoyant of moods. 'As a matter of fact,' he said, 'the incident has been a most beneficial holiday to me — the only restful holiday I've had for years.'

De Garis was arrested for having issued a false cheque, but in court he was able to prove simply that the cheque had gone to the wrong bank. He had enough money to cover it, so after promising to pay the costs of the two detectives who had gone to Auckland to fetch him home, out he went a free man.

Once more he was super confident. He told reporters that he was writing a book about his adventures. Furthermore a film director in Sydney had made him a tempting offer to play the lead in a movie. This would tell the tale of the De Garis achievements. 'Of course,' he said, 'the most thrilling part of it will be what happened between Rosebud and Auckland. That part of it will provide plenty of excitement.'

Somehow by extraordinary juggling, and friends who still had faith in him, De Garis managed to keep afloat. One such friend was Robert M. Anderson of Mildura. He gave him a book of signed cheques so that he could operate a Melbourne investment account so De Garis used Anderson's money to cover his own cheques.

Eventually even this ran out and the Bank of New South Wales brought an action against Anderson for £8300 that had been overdrawn. Mr Owen Dixon K.C. with a young barrister, R.G. Menzies, appeared for the defence and Mr John Latham K.C. appeared for the Bank of New South Wales. De Garis was really the man on trial, although he appeared only as a witness. His extraordinary charm triumphed for him even here in Court.

Mr Justice Weigall said he was impressed favourably by De Garis and his manner did not suggest any falsehood. He couldn't make such favourable comment about the bank witness. So the Anderson — De Garis team won the case with costs.

It was a small victory. The judge said in Court that De Garis dealt in perilous finance, and as the months passed by in 1926, it became more and more perilous. In March he admitted that he had £420,000 liabilities and no assets.

All the time he had the dream that Kendenup would save him, that this was oil country and he would become the Australian oil King. It was in the beginning of August that he gave repeated hints that sensational news was at hand, an oil scheme in Western Australia, worth £9 million. In a letter to one of his creditors he said: 'Instead of a small area bearing oil the existence of a huge oil basin has been established. Eight testing bores have demonstrated this fact beyond any question of doubt.'

He expected news by the hour from New York, but it never came. The excuse was that there had been big oil gushers in California so the Australian investment no longer was necessary.

On Tuesday August 17, De Garis was alone in his 18-room house at Mornington which was on the Esplanade overlooking the Bay. He went into the kitchen, plugged the keyholes and all the spaces around the doors with rag and turned the gas full on.

He put his head on a tea cosy, his mouth over the stove and there he died. The day before he arranged for a plumber to call at his house at noon on the Tuesday. It was important De Garis said.

When the plumber arrived at Dunoon he found a note for him gummed on the front door:

'Dear Mr Scott — sorry to drag you into this, but you are a plumber, and a plumber is needed. There might be danger to anyone else. The gas must be cut off outside the house. The kitchen is closed, and is full of gas, and you will find me there. All gas must be cut off, and the door opened carefully before entering. No matches must be used. Better ring police, also my wife — poor girl she has no idea.'

In the kitchen there were some notes on the margin of a newspaper:

'Fell down on big job yesterday and paying penalty today.'

To his wife and child he wrote:

'My darling Vi and Vee. God bless you. My end is getting nearer. It is a slow business. Fondest love. Hugs and Kisses. Daddy.'

Then there was a lost message written in a very shaky hand: 'My car, Kissel, is at Peninsular garage, Mornington. Get Jim to help by driving it wherever it is needed. C.J.'

There was a sheet on which De Garis had written the scores while listening to an England — Australia Test Match against England. Arthur Mailey had taken 6 wickets for 136.

Then there was a china cat, labelled 'The Good Luck Bringer.' It had been smashed from its pedestal and was overlooking the body of its owner.

So De Garis died and countless investors lost their money, yet even right up to his death he continued to inspire loyalty from his friends and there was sympathy for a man who did try the impossible and failed.

The Adelaide Sun, for example, in the midst of indebtedness pointed out that he was battling to clear his name by paying creditors out of his own personal earnings.

'There were 176,000 (Kendenup) debentures issued' said the Sun, and De Garis has set himself the task of redeeming the lot at their full face value within five years.

'There is a lesson for Australia and the world. It is equal to the best traditions of Anzac Day. A lone figure, battling against overwhelming odds.'

Then Kendenup eventually did prosper. The original number of settlers dwindled, but eventually it became a prosperous area of apple orchardists and sheep farmers. In 1948 the Kendenup Farmers and Agricultural Society formed a De Garis Memorial Association. The Society president, Mr F. Walton announced that it would be 'most unjust' if the name of De Garis, founder of the community, were ever forgotten.
