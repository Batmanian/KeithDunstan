Prince Leonard of Hutt

16 min read.

First published 1980

Profile of Len Casley, the West Australian wheat farmer who seceded from Australia over a wheat quota dispute in 1970 to found the Hutt River Principality, styling himself Prince Leonard and later declaring a brief 'war' on the Commonwealth.

I FIRST MET His Royal Highness Prince Leonard of Hutt just before Christmas 1974. There were tourist buses which went all the way to the Royal Province, but there was just a bare 24 hours to make the excursion. This was a rest day when the immortals Dennis Lillee and Jeff Thomson were bowling against the Englishmen in the Second Test, an occasion not to be missed. Hutt River Province was 574 kilometres north of Perth, so clearly the best way to get there was to fly by commercial aircraft to Geraldton, then get a taxi over the final 100.

The taxi was not necessary — bless him — the Prince was there to pick me up at the airport. He was a small, dark-haired man in horn-rimmed glasses. He was wearing a short-sleeved shirt, brown trousers, sandals and — begging his pardon — he had an accent that could have come from across the border of Hutt River Province — like Australia. His face was showing the tan and the wear that a face gets on the oldest, driest continent on earth and one could see that the royal hands had been used to hard work.

The car was not chauffeur driven, it was covered in dust and its humble make was far from new. We drove out on a red dirt road, leaving a billowing red cloud behind the rear wheels. The temperature was just on 40 degrees Celsius and the sun burned out of a perfect stainless blue sky. Prince Leonard agreed that it could be hot in these parts. It was frequently around 40 in December, it was 40 or over all January and it was 40 for most of February.

Indeed the car was making its own coughing, spluttering protest against the heat and three times we stopped while Prince Leonard opened the bonnet, delved into the entrails and got it going again. One felt that another Prince, such as Prince Rainier of Monaco or even Prince Andrew of Great Britain would not have handled the situation with such sure knowledge.

I looked across at him beside me in the driving seat, very amiable, relaxed, he could have been a tradesman driving up from Perth, but I did not call him Len Casley. This was the interesting thing — it seemed more natural to address him always as Prince Leonard.

As we drove he told his story. He and his wife were both West Australians. His father had been a fireman on the railways at Kalgoorlie. His wife came from Fremantle — Shirley Butler she was — and for sixteen years they had a place at Watsonia, about 320 kilometres East of Perth. Also they had worked some copper and lead mines, in from Onslow. All in all, what with some real estate investments in Perth, they had done pretty well.

So they put their money into 7500 hectares around Hutt River. In 1969 Len and his sons cleared 5660 hectares and produced 14,700 bushels of wheat. Things looked great, that is, until the Wheat Quota Board stepped in. What they did was nigh unbelievable.

The quotas were based on the average production over seven years. But the Casleys were new, they had not produced any wheat for the previous six. That little point made no difference to the Board. It divided one year by seven and finally fixed their quota at a miserable 1647 bushels.

'Look,' he said, gripping the steering wheel a little harder, 'to get back a proper quota, to grow what I was growing, I would have needed another 1,800,000 acres (728,460 hectares) which would have cost us $52 million.'

Surely there was an appeal? If this was his first year of wheat growing it was just so obvious there had to be an adjustment.

Oh no, the W.A. Wheat Quota Bill was a beauty. 'This bill,' continued Prince Leonard, 'had two clauses which just flattened us. No appeals allowed against any quotas granted, and if there are any losses as a result of your quota, no compensation.'

Certainly he protested. He protested to the Wheat Quota Board, the Premier and the Governor of Western Australia, Sir Douglas Kendrew. The Wheat Board didn't even answer, nor did the Premier, but the Governor did and he passed the matter on for ministerial advice. So back came the inevitable answer, no alteration to the Casley wheat quota.

Len Casley seemed to know his way around remarkably well for a wheat farmer. He then worked out what acreage he would need to get back a quota which would enable him to survive and he came up with that figure 1,800,000 acres worth $52 million. So instead of asking for money he put on a Territorial Compensation Claim and lodged it with the Governor.

Two weeks later there was a bill introduced into Parliament which enabled the Government to resume any rural lands. 'It came so closely after the claim,' said Prince Leonard, 'it looked as if the bill was being prepared directly to deal with us. We were very frightened.'

The day of decision was on the eve of the passing of the Third Reading of the bill. There was an emergency family meeting and that is when they decided to secede. They did it in fine style. On 21 April 1970 they served formal notice of secession on the Premier, Sir David Brand; the Governor, Sir Douglas Kendrew; the Acting Prime Minister of Australia, Mr John McEwen, and the Governor-General of Australia, Sir Paul Hasluck.

They seceded from Australia but they still offered their sovereignty to Her Majesty, Queen Elizabeth. What happened next? Well, they did receive a reply from Sir Paul Hasluck. He said in effect: 'Don't do anything until you hear from the Commonwealth.' Then Mr McEwan, acting Prime Minister, wrote to Sir Paul that it would be unconstitutional for him to intervene.

From this Prince Leonard took it quite clearly that he had formally and legally seceded.

Meanwhile, as we drove, the foreign country of West Australia seemed to extend for ever — flat, dusty, and great waves of heat did curious things with mirages and blurred the horizon. One time an emu looked at us in astonishment, then sped off with huge high-kicking strides. There were spots of remarkable beauty. Prince Leonard pointed out the white grevillea in bloom and there were trees covered in orange flowers. 'We call that the orange Christmas tree,' he said, 'it blooms at Christmas.'

As we talked one gathered that the reception from the West Australian Government was chilly and what with the arrival in Canberra of a new Prime Minister, Mr Billy McMahon there was utter lack of recognition.

So what to do? He went back to his Australian Law and discovered that under the Treason Act anyone assisting a de facto prince attaining his office cannot be charged with treason and anyone hindering a de facto prince in the charge of his princely duties could be charged with treason.

That was it. They had another meeting, declared the province a Principality and decided that the Administrator, Mr Len Casley, would receive the title of Prince, hence HRH Prince Leonard of Hutt. Mrs Casley, of course, became Princess Shirley.

The Prince set about organising his Government. His son, the Hon Ian, became Minister for Postal Services; his son the Hon Wayne became Minister for Foreign Affairs and his son the Hon Richard became Treasurer.

Just as he was telling me this we came to a sign HUTT RIVER PRINCIPALITY BORDER ENTRY. 'You are now leaving West Australia' said Prince Leonard with a faint smile of triumph.

'My God, I haven't got a visa.'

'I think you will be all right. We don't make visas compulsory, but most people like to have them. They love them as souvenirs to prove they have been here.'

Frankly the Provincial township was a little disappointing. One did not expect Rhine castles, battlements etc., but a draw bridge would have helped.

There were half a dozen brick and cement buildings, three flag poles, and a large slab announcing Independence Day, April 21, 1970. Over yonder was an impressive craggy bluff, Mr Secession and Prince Leonard pointed out that Independence Day was, of course, a public holiday for the thirty people who lived in the province.

The Hutt River flag was large and imposing, rather more impressive than the one I remembered at Government House, Canberra. It contained white for 'Perfection and Purity', a bull's head for 'Rural Background', red for 'Liberty', gold for 'Honour', scales for 'Justice' and an eagle for 'Freedom'.

Regrettably I did not meet Princess Shirley. She was away in Perth and the Minister for Postal Services, the Treasurer and the Minister for Foreign Affairs were off tending to the Provincial sheep and cattle.

It was not a hectic day for tourists — too hot. But I was assured that the tourists came through at a rate of better than 60,000 a year, even 1500 in a day at the height of the season, and the nagging thought occurred, 'What did they come to see', and the answer was, of course, the Prince himself. Ron Saw explained it all in the Bulletin.

'He waits as the buses grumble in... "What do we call him when we meet him? We will meet him won't we?" And what do they call him? "Prince Leonard usually," he says. "Sometimes Your Highness." I think they just like to hear themselves saying it. And they like to shake hands. You hear them say, I shook hands with the Prince, and their faces glow.'

The tourist buses had yet to arrive so we went on the grand tour. The finest feature was the Chapel of Nairn which had pews for sixty people, an electric organ and two Regal Chairs, of course for Prince Leonard and Princess Shirley. Then the chapel was decorated with romantic, infinitely noble, religious paintings. These were by the artist Frank Pash and there were others by another West Australian artist, Robert Pope.

According to an official brochure, on 12 April 1973, for services rendered, Prince Leonard created Robert Pope the Earl of Phoenix and Frank Pash the Earl of Nain. And there were more. A few weeks later Margaret Sheen became the Earl of Astron (no male Chauvinism in Hutt) and John Whatley the Baron of Tucson. They all received magnificent coat of arms. I was particularly taken with Lord Whatley's piece of heraldry. He is an American and he fills the role of Postmaster-General. His shield depicts a parachute (he has done 250 free falls as a skydiver) a black swan, the rising sun and on top there is a bison to symbolise his work with the U.S. National Park Board.

There have been many investitures since; the desire for Hutt River knighthoods is very keen. Many a disc jockey in Perth is desperate to have one.

Sir Richard Marshall became South Australian Consul for Hutt River and received the honor of flying the provincial flag over his Consulate, formerly the Marshall private residence. Yes, Prince Leonard made clear that there were agents-general and consulates in Melbourne, Sydney, Hobart, Brisbane, Canberra, London, Vienna, Hong Kong, South Africa, Canada and New Zealand.

Grand occasions like investitures take place in the chapel and one can see they are fine indeed. Everyone wears dress clothes. The emblems are attached to gold sashes which are borne in on velvet cushions. Then Prince Leonard in classic style creates his knights with a large antique navy sword: 'Arise Sir Richard,' or whoever.

It is fascinating to note that once a gentleman becomes Royal the spin-offs are almost endless. A number of firms have been most happy to receive the Royal Charter. Angle Vale Wineries in South Australia, for example, are official suppliers of wines to the Royal Household. Willocks Store in Geraldton is official clothier to the Royal Family of Hutt Province. More recently Pioneer Electronics became Royal Suppliers of Electronic Equipment to the Hutt River Principality. Pioneer fitted a sound system to Prince Leonard's 'state vehicle'.

We moved on to the Postal Department. Immediately after secession the Minister for Foreign Affairs wrote to Mr McMahon suggesting that Australia align its Foreign Policy with Hutt River. (This was important because Foreign Minister Wayne already had been nearly drafted into the Australian Army). The Treasurer wrote to his counterpart, Bill Sneddon, asking if the Federal Treasury would print Hutt River banknotes and the Minister for Postal Services wrote to the PMG asking for a quote on printing stamps. The reaction could hardly be described as enthusiastic. The PMG was particularly difficult and for three months refused to handle mail to Hutt River. But Prince Leonard did get his stamps and one would suggest that they are considerably more attractive than those of his nearest neighbor, the Commonwealth of Australia. The Prince gets 200 letters a week asking for his stamps and his first day issues have been sought by philatelists all over the world.

Alas, the PMG would not handle his mail. For a time he salvaged Hutt River dignity by putting on Cocos Island stamps, recognised in Australia, plus his own stamps.

The Provincial currency is in bank notes, 20 cents up to $2 and this is legal tender within Hutt River borders. Nigh on seventy percent of it is used in the restaurant or the souvenir shop, but for the rest it disappears for ever. People buy it for fun, just to say they have been there. It is a beautiful deal for Hutt River Province, a licence to print money that really works.

We went into the souvenir shop and there was a splendid array of artifacts. One could buy Hutt River T shirts, Hutt River wall plaques, Hutt River hand-made belts, handbags, sandals, postcards, ball point pens, but no Hutt River ash trays. 'Certainly not. You never stub cigarettes on heraldry' according to Prince Leonard.

The variety of activities is remarkable. One could purchase copies of the Hutt River Province National Anthem. In cold print it compared favorably with Australia's Advance Australia Fair. The first verse:

There's a giant lying in the sun
Where people welcome everyone.
It's a land where dreams can all come true
and people strive until they do
Where wealth in rivers wide can flow
and there is room enough to grow.

For those who prefer things a little more stirring then there is a record titled The Ballad of Hutt River. The whole Casley saga is there complete with chorus:

'Tis of the rebel Casley a story will be told,
A man who valued freedom more than any sum of gold.
He settled in Hutt River, it was his chosen land.
'Twas there the rebel farmers will fight and make a stand.

The stand of the rebel Casley has been a brave one and he has met a curious mixture of bureaucratic aggression and tacit acceptance. Tourist authorities in West Australia rejoice in his existence. In 1973 the Commonwealth Information Services recorded a half hour interview to encourage tourism to Australia and in 1975 the West Australian Government invited him to a trade fair.

Members of Hutt River Province are not on the electoral roll and pay no income tax. The Royal subjects receive no Commonwealth benefits, so Prince Leonard pays his own social services, rather better than they would get elsewhere, he suggests slyly. But the battle is still on. On 15 February 1977 a Perth magistrate ruled that he was not immune to Australian taxation laws and he was fined $4 and $50 costs for failing to give information to the Income Tax department. In January 1978 for the same reason he spent a night in Geraldton Jail.

Indeed the rebel Casley has suffered blow after blow. Mr McMahon, when he was Prime Minister wrote to the West Australian Premier, Mr Tonkin: 'I would like to make it quite clear that the Commonwealth Government does not recognise either legally or in any other way the existence of a self-styled province of this nature nor can I envisage any circumstances in which it would do so.'

Mr Fraser's Government gave him an equally frigid response. And there have been humiliations like the day Wayne Casley refused to register for National Service, was fined $40 and had to ask to be deported back to his country. Then there was the time Prince Leonard was fined for selling wine within the Province.

He was upset about this, a monstrous piece of interference. He described it as 'an aggressive act by a neighboring power.'

He is at a disadvantage; his foreign neighbor surrounds him on all borders, but he needles back rather well. In 1975 he offered to set up Hutt Province as an agency for registering overseas ships in the manner of Panama or Liberia. He offered ten percent below standard world rates. Some people argued that Hutt River was a curious place to become a registry for ships — it looked about as nautical as the Todd River in Alice Springs, but Prince Leonard replied that Switzerland did not have a port either, but it still registered shipping. The Federal Government warned international ship owners to ignore the Hutt River offer.

But if he could not get anywhere on the water maybe he could get somewhere in the air. In Melbourne, Mr Dirck Hagers, who called himself the Hutt River Minister for Aviation, announced the new Prince Leonard Airlines. He said Prince Leonard had bought three Comet jets for $2 million. An airport would cost $300,000 and the plan was to operate a bi-weekly service to Mauritius. There were excellent pictures in the newspapers of the lovely Comet jets with Prince Leonard Airlines down the side, but there was an instant No from Canberra and one newspaper even had the headline:

PHUTT GOES HUTT AIRLINE.

Prince Leonard speaking from Perth said that this was the sort of bureaucratic interference which caused his Province to declare war on Australia on 2 December 1977. Surprise was his weapon. At 5.10 p.m. he sent the Governor-General a telegram. It read: 'I declare that a state of war exists between the Principality and the Commonwealth of Australia. All diplomatic relations are severed.'

One could have expected an emotional statement from Mr Fraser, similar to that which Mr R.G. Menzies made in 1939, but the silence from Canberra was profound.

Prince Leonard's forces were somewhat limited. He had an Air Force — one single engined Lake Buccaneer aircraft and his flagship, a rather illustrious 40-foot international racing yacht berthed at North Fremantle. He had no Army.

Over the week-end the Commonwealth of Australia had not reacted by sending paratroops, F111s; nor had there been a ground invasion by Leopard tanks. The truth was there had been no reaction whatever. So at 3.30 p.m. on the Sunday Prince Leonard sent Sir John Kerr another telegram:

'I declare the state of war has ceased. My Government will accept diplomatic relations with the Commonwealth in order to try to advance mutual co-operation as is befitting sovereign countries in their international relations. I trust you will call on your government to respect the laws of war. Sovereignty is automatic to a State undefeated in a state of war.'

A Queensland student of the laws of war passed on the idea that Sovereignty was automatic to a State undefeated in war, and that it was necessary only to wage that war for 24 hours. Hutt River celebrated its war victory with a carnival and the triumphant Prince Leonard sent off his application for membership in the United Nations. Barry Robertson of the Australian gave a war correspondent's note. He said Sir John Kerr left Australia immediately after receiving Prince Leonard's telegram. However it was not to be construed that this had anything to do with Prince Leonard's war. He was finishing his term of office and Sir Zelman Cowen was taking over as Governor-General.

Meanwhile Prince Leonard vastly expanded his territory. It was at this time that his Agent-General in Melbourne, travel agent Graham Orr, went on one of Dick Smith's non stop round trips to the South Pole aboard a Qantas Boeing 747. He felt this was sufficient to claim a sizeable chunk of the Antarctic Continent for Hutt River Province.

Prince Leonard sees no limit to the future of his Principality. Fifty years hence he believes it will be a $260 million investment with multi-storey hotels, a casino, radio and television stations, airport, hospitals, a lake with water skiers and power boats.

In January 1978 David McNicoll in the Bulletin said: 'If some firm action had been taken early, these bucolic oddballs would now be back happily dagging sheep instead of going on like inhabitants of an institution for the deranged.'

Institution for the deranged? Bucolic oddballs? one wonders. Some newspapers claim that already Prince Leonard of Hutt is a millionaire and one could be forgiven for having the idea that possibly this son of a railwayman is the smartest man in West Australia. Or should that be put another way? — the smartest man surrounded by West Australia.
