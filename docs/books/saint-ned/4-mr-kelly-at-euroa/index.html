Mr Kelly at Euroa

5 min read.

First published 1980

Follows the Kelly gang's first bank robbery, a genial, almost theatrical siege of Younghusband's Faithful's Creek Station and the Euroa branch of the National Bank in which Kelly charmed his hostages, dressed his gang in new clothes bought with stolen money, and rode off with £2060.

All Victoria was now agog with the outrage at Stringybark Creek. The Williamstown Advertiser painted a garish picture of the villain. Already he probably had twenty murders to his credit and as for his morals "He spends his money among harlots in whose sweet society he is now basking."¹

The theatre had almost the immediacy of today's television. Within days there was an extraordinary show at the Princess Theatre titled The Vultures of the Wombat Ranges. It was a triumph of good over evil with the police exterminating the Kelly gang in the last act. Yet the Age was shocked, Ned came out of it as a hero and there were shouts of "Kelly" with uproarious applause.²

Immediately the Victorian government passed a Felons' Apprehension Bill and any civilian could shoot the gang on sight and any person suspected of giving them aid could be arrested. It was a declaration of war but for the next 18 months it was Kelly who won every skirmish.

On Monday, 9 December, 1878 they went to Euroa, a little place of 300 people on the main north-eastern railway line between Seymour and Benalla. It all started at Younghusband's Faithful's Creek Station just seven kilometres out of town. In almost genial style Kelly held up staff and passers-by. As station hands, staff and passers-by came along the gang locked them in the store. There were some city businessmen up on a shooting holiday—two people in the store for the night. A touring hawker, James Gloster, came by with his cart and this was a marvellous windfall. Kelly and his brother held up Gloster at gunpoint, then raided his cart and dressed themselves out beautifully as bush dandies in brand-new clothes, complete with cravats and handkerchiefs. Then they doused themselves with scent.³ Gloster went into the lockup with all the others.

Everyone was allowed to come out for breakfast and the women were allowed to come and go almost as they pleased. Kelly chopped down the telegraph poles to cut off communication into Euroa and just as he did this four fettlers came along and demanded to know what the devil he was doing. They went into the store too. It was becoming quite a gathering.

Joe Byrne stayed behind to guard the prisoners while the others set off to hold up the Euroa branch of the National Bank. It was the easiest of holdups. They relieved the bank of £2060, which Ned put into a sugar bag. The manager, Mr Scott, allegedly as a delaying venture, produced a bottle of whisky and they all had a drink together.

Next came the astonishing part of the venture. Kelly announced that it was a nice day so he would take them all out on a ride to Faithful's Creek. Scott protested that he had a wife, seven children and two servant girls. "We'll take them too," said Kelly. Scott was alarmed and insisted that he treat Mrs Scott with respect. He needn't have been alarmed Mrs Scott was utterly charmed by the good looking bushranger. She admired his manly bearing and said: "Surely, Mr Kelly, you don't say you are the man who has been outlawed."⁴

They departed in the most extraordinary procession, Steve Hart was on horseback, Dan Kelly was in a spring cart with the tellers, the servant girls and some of the children. Mrs Scott drove the manager's own buggy with the rest of the children, then Ned rode in a waggon with the bank manager and the money.

They arrived at the station at around 6 pm and there was no hurry to be off. Ned ordered the female staff at Faithful's Station to prepare dinner for thirty. After a good meal Dan Kelly and Steve Hart entertained the Kelly guests with an exhibition of riding. Yet if the gang was kindly in its demeanour the threats were always there. Kelly made a boastful speech about his activities and what he proposed to do with the police. He told his prisoners that if any of them left under three hours they would be shot, then off they went singing in the direction of the Strathbogie Ranges.

Kelly was an extrovert who hugely enjoyed being the centre of attention. Before he left Faithful's Creek, he left a long letter addressed to Mr Cameron M.L.A. It finished with a series of threats which had all the air of an old fashioned curse.

"If my people do not get justice, and those innocents released from prison, I shall be forced to seek revenge of everything the human race for the future.

"If the public do not see justice done I will seek revenge for the name and character which has been given to me and my relations, while God gives me strength to pull a trigger."

Why of all people did Kelly choose Mr Cameron? He was a member of the Legislative Assembly and he had opposed the new Felons' Apprehension Bill, which the government introduced purely to cope with the horde of Kelly sympathisers. Anyone who helped the Kellys, anyone who gave false information could be put in jail. Cameron was no sympathiser, he just thought the bill was an assault on freedom of the individual. Yet Kelly must have seen him as an ally.

The news when it arrived in Melbourne caused a sensation. It seemed beyond belief. While an army of police was hunting for the Kelly gang, calmly in the middle of the afternoon they take over a town of 300 inhabitants, then unmolested they conduct a happy picnic at a local sheep station. The reward was now £4000 and the Chief Commissioner of Police, Captain Standish himself went to investigate.

However, if the incident at Euroa had some absurd overtones, the Jerilderie affair was worse.
