The Legend

6 min read.

First published 1980

Opens 'Saint Ned' by surveying a century of Ned Kelly worship, from Sidney Nolan's paintings and Arthur Boyd's sculpture to rival historians' verdicts, and argues Kelly's outlaw defiance of an entire police force answers a distinctly Australian craving for a hero who outwits authority.

One hundred years have gone by and that strange steel helmet of Ned Kelly looms over Australia like a mystic tombstone. Was he a savage thug or was he the complete, romantic hero? We are no more sure now than we were then.

Sometimes I get the feeling that Ned Kelly seeps through the very pores of Australians. As early as 1942 a devoted historian produced a bibliography of all the major works on Ned Kelly and they came to forty-two.¹ They have continued since then in a gushing stream. I have counted seventeen full length books on Kelly and his gang.

There have been three major movies, a rock opera, two plays, a ballet, a jazz suite, several novels, a ballad, innumerable songs, accounts in blank verse, re-enactments on television and he has inspired many great Australian painters and sculptors.

No prime minister, no explorer, no painter, artist, singer, soldier, no other Australian in history has ever merited such attention. Ned Kelly, the desperado, outranks all his rivals by at least four to one.

It is the variety of opinion on the merit of Kelly that is so fascinating, and it is no different now than it was in 1880. The conservative press in Melbourne was outraged by Kelly. He couldn't be hanged quickly enough. He was a "white-livered poltroon", he was a "murderer, a traitor, a hypocrite, a liar and a coward".²

Yet the country press was not so sure. There were those who pointed out that in the area where the Kelly gang roamed three out of every four in the male population was on their side.³ After the Kellys held up the towns of Euroa and Jerilderie a Beechworth editorial writer admitted he was staggered. And he went on to say these were "the most daring outrages in the history of civilisation".⁴

There is no doubt that he had some appeal. His spectacular escapades covered little more than two years, he was only twenty-four at the end and before he was hanged 4 000 people gathered at the Melbourne Hippodrome to plead for his life. There was an equally large crowd outside.⁵

And so the controversy continues today. That great historian Professor Manning Clark takes rather a romantic view of Kelly. He sees in him the "nostalgia for the life of the free, the fearless and the bold".⁶

On the other hand, Malcolm H. Ellis, another distinguished historian, has described him as "one of the most cold-blooded, egotistical, and utterly self-centred criminals who ever decorated the end of a rope in Australia".⁷ Then Dame Mabel Brookes, a lady with a feeling for history, wrote: "If a cog had slipped in time the Kelly boys would have been on Gallipoli, one probably a V.C. winner."⁸

I grew up in Melbourne, the city that hanged Ned Kelly. In recent years he has proved to be a very useful legend. Sometimes I have wondered whether he is heading towards a posthumous knighthood from a grateful government or even sanctification.

Perhaps the artists and writers have done most in heading him towards a heavenly course. Back in the early nineteen fifties the artist Arthur Boyd came out clearly on the side of Ned Kelly. He did an extraordinary sculpture; he depicted the Kelly helmet sitting on a donkey. Simple enough but the donkey had the bewigged head of the law, suggesting as clearly as you like that the law is an ass.

Then Sidney Nolan did the first of his Ned Kelly series in 1946–47, a series so great they have become classics of Australian art. The entire series of twenty-seven paintings is now in the possession of Melbourne's National Gallery and they are valued at over half a million dollars.⁹

Right through the series there is the awesome, almost God-like symbol of the Ned Kelly helmet. On the other hand the policemen are painted as absurd creatures. Robert Melville in his book on Sidney Nolan describes the police as sinister figures of fun, reminiscent of the Keystone Cops, their heads far too large for their bodies.¹⁰

His defenders came from all sides. One of the last pieces Norman Lindsay wrote before his death was a passionate defence of Kelly. Sidney J. Baker, an expert on the Australian language, told the world there was little doubt Ned Kelly was a homosexual. He pointed out that Ned was fond of perfume, that he and other members of his gang sometimes dressed as women, that Steve Hart and Dan Kelly were said to have died in each other's arms at Glenrowan, and that young Dan Kelly had asked the schoolmaster of Glenrowan, Tom Curnow, to dance with him.¹¹ And indeed he had. In the Royal Commission of 1881 Curnow said that he had objected when asked, but Dan Kelly had said: "Come on, never mind your boots", so he went into the hotel at Glenrowan and danced with Dan Kelly.

Norman Lindsay, furious, aged eighty-eight, wrote to the Bulletin. He said he was old enough to go right back to the Kelly era. The small boys of his generation were obsessed by the exploits of the Kelly gang. They played police and bushrangers, and being a member of a large family, he was forced into the miserable role of being policeman when he desperately wanted the noble role of being a member of the Kelly gang.

As for Kelly being a homosexual, he wouldn't sustain that rubbish for an instant. His father, a very dominant male, always used perfume, like most other people. Sewerage was so bad and there were so many open drains, surely it was normal for men to wear eau de cologne to drown the stench. Men danced at get-togethers for the simple reason women were not to be had in these country areas; they were practically all male communities. As for Dan and Hart dying in each other's arms, what did they expect? A building was burning down over their heads. Probably they were terrified.¹²

Norman Lindsay, artist, sculptor, etcher, novelist, said that no trial of law before or since has taken possession of the Australian people with such a passionate intensity as that of Ned Kelly. It touched on a national ego which hasn't changed, and that is of a reckless man and dangerous man who becomes heroic when threatened by war or conflict.¹³

This is coming close to the reasons for the Kelly adoration. Some believe artists and writers have impinged on him, because there are so few Australian heroes, but there is more to it than that.

Australians for nearly 200 years have been a migrant people. Many of them migrated to escape old world oppressions, vindictive laws, sectarianism and the like. The Australian soldier is notorious for being disrespectful to his superiors, easy-going and independent.

Donald Horne, that perpetual examiner of the Australian character, has written how Australians distrust authority and that goes for the police whom they often hate.¹⁴

It is no accident the nation's beloved song, the alternative national anthem, has the most extraordinary theme of any patriotic hymn. Australians when travelling overseas will chant it at the slightest encouragement. It concerns a swagman who steals a sheep and finally commits suicide rather than be captured by the police.

So Ned Kelly, although a murderer, fulfilled something of a national dream. For two years he and his three young comrades outwitted an entire police force, made it look ridiculous, then died with courage. Of such stuff legends are made.
