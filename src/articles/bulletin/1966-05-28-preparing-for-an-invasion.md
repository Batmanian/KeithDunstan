For over four years your correspondent, in a very well-behaved fashion, has been quietly writing this page. The nom de plume 'Batman' was chosen because here was a name that was dignified. John Batman was one of the founders of our city and the name would give the page a certain authority.

But these have been difficult weeks for Batman. First there was the account of John Batman in the new 'Australian Dictionary of Biography — Volume I.' The biographer, P. L. Brown, gave him much praise for his 'exceptional talents,' his courage, his humane consideration for the aborigines, but the description of his death makes sad reading: 'Batman gave his libido to many an outback maid; to his lawful wife, Eliza, he doled out his time. There can be little doubt that the natural leader of 1835, to whom she was closely linked, lost her affection as a thwarted cripple. She traced his downfall to excessive exposure; others to grog and venery. Simpson himself feared Batman in his cups. But there was no break with Eliza until the physical failure, when suggestions of nasal syphilis developed, and Batman's Sydney natives became his only attendants.'

Eliza Batman married again, became Sarah Willoughby of somewhat abandoned character, and was murdered. Such things are not included in our gentle history books.

The Batman name received its second blow just this week and this your correspondent had been hearing about for some time. Batman the old comic strip is a 1966 TV sensation, a muscle-bound supermanic, hooded, masked, ineptly drawn, all-American hero. Already the local cinema circuit has dug up a 20-year-old 15-episode movie serial and it is away. We went to see a man from New York, Alex Tolmer.

Alex Tolmer is one of those remarkable fellows who promoted the hula hoop. He is the man who brought Super Ball to Australia. (Remember the Boeing 707 that arrived last September with a vital shipment?) Now he is here to discuss the Batman craze.

He called at the Licensing Corporation of America, which has a whole floor in New York on this sort of thing. The Licensing Corporation of America, which owns Batman, LCA's chief is Jay Emmett and he told Alex Tolmer that Batman was the biggest thing they had ever handled. He was five times bigger than Davy Crockett and twice as big as James Bond. Already they had collected $US5 million this year in royalties and they expected to collect $50 million by the year's end. While Tolmer was there, Jay Emmett did a deal with the Borden Milk Co. for $400,000, just so they could use the name for Batman ice-cream.

Altogether there have been over 500 Batman lines. You name it, said Alex Tolmer, they've got it. 'There's even Batman shaving cream and a Batman under-arm deodorant. It's just unbelievable to me. Anything with a Batman name on it they'll buy. The only thing they've missed is little girls' lines. With Davy Crockett there used to be a Davy Crockett teaset. When you said "how come?" they replied, "it belonged to Davy's mother." Trouble is Batman's parents were murdered by thugs when he was a kid. No mother, no teaset. But you know, when I got to New York, I found it impossible to get a taxi on Wednesday and Thursday nights between 7.30 and 8 p.m. All the cab drivers were in bars looking at Batman.

'So I said to a cabbie "Who is this Batman guy? Is he some kind of a crook or something?" They looked at me as if I was from another planet. "Batman's a good guy, see," said this cabbie. "He looks after people."'

I asked Mr. Tolmer if there had been any change from the original strip. He said all sorts of gimmicks had been introduced. 'There was the Batmobile which looked like an A.D. 2000 experimental dream from General Motors. Also Batman now had a girlfriend.

'You might remember,' he said, 'in the original strip Batman had a ward, Robin the Boy Wonder, and they were always seen together. The decided the town was talking. They decided they'd better give Batman a girlfriend. But, of course, they couldn't get rid of Robin. Now they travel together as a threesome: Batman, his girlfriend, and Robin.'

Mr. Tolmer explained there were all sorts of gags: a Batman table lamp, Batman punching bag, very good for the release of Batmaniacal feelings, and a whole series of Batman cards. One says 'Batman is a fink' and another one which could be a solution to a most difficult situation, 'Batman for Vietnam.'

Tolmer really needs Batman on Australian TV. 'So far, no Australian station has definitely taken it. I believe it will be a stiff price, something like 4000 Australian dollars for each half-hour episode. They'll pay it eventually. British and Japanese TV held back for a while, but apparently they're paying now.'

So Tolmer is very happy with Batman. He says there is nothing new in entertainment. This has been going on now for 1000 years. Right at the moment big money is in private-eye gear: Bond and the Man from UNCLE are big, and so are sales in private-eye gear, cipher kits, guns equipped with silencers, etc.

Meanwhile, Tolmer has a 1965 cream Rolls-Royce. This he calls his Tolmobile.

So now do you fully appreciate the problem of writing this particular page? Why aren't you wearing a black hood and a mask? And don't you feel just a little bit battered, Batman?
