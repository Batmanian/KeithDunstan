For those rare creatures who live in New Guinea, in the arts and the church, at school at Geelong Grammar, the cup was almost running over. At one stage rumour flew around town that Old Geelong Grammar gear, including the school's famous disposal store, would be available at Sammy Bear's disposal store in Russell Street. Your correspondent went round at the speed of light to pick up half a dozen. But no, it was untrue. There were those who said if trade would be available the trade would be reserved for the Old Country.

Actually Geelong Grammar began to suffer with all the publicity, and was smart enough to change their letterheads smartly with a Prince Charles issue. There was a piece in the British press which corrected 'fallacies shown in the Australian Press'. It said (1) there has never been any fagging and never will there be any; (2) only five boys, the house captains, are allowed the privilege of caning incessant troublemakers; (3) Prince Charles will not be treated as the typical boy at Timbertop; he will be subordinate to the masters only and will be supervising the boys; (4) he only has to get up for breakfast at 7.30; (5) he doesn't have to perform any menial tasks, not a procedure that has never existed; (6) there is not a mile marathon at the end of the year; (7) the meals are above the standard of incessant mincemeat and eggs.

Any old boy would raise an eyebrow at the statement that there never has been fagging. Perhaps they mean at Timbertop only. But what really hurt came from an unnamed Old Geelongonian on the Diary page of the 'Evening Standard': 'Within two seconds of a Geelong boy coming into a room at Geelong you can tell that he went to Geelong. In the best Geelong tradition he will be very arrogant, very distinctive, and often not much liked. All of the parents have to be loathsomely rich to send the boys there. Except mine, of course.'

There's a choice of interpretation in the Blue Pouch Tea advertisement. It said boys have done. There are very few 'rich' boys here, some families are well off, others are sacrificing a lot to send the boys to that place that has Timbertop.

The other place that suffered, even though it was terribly good for the area, was Haverbrack Avenue, Malvern. Haverbrack Avenue is just a few streets away from Toorak, S.E.2. For those on the wrong side of Malvern Road there has always been a tragic sense of being so near, yet so far. It is well down to the area now that shops in the neighbourhood have gone as far as to change their letterheads.

One time Haverbrack was graced by the presence of Robert Singleton, Master in Equitation, who in 1868 had a riding school in the area. He also had a property in the Dandenongs and he used to keep in touch between his two properties by heliograph. But more important than the local history, February 1941, No. 2 Haverbrack Avenue made the 'nice homes' department of 'The Argus Beautiful'.

News of the purchase of the house by Sir Robert made the headlines on a Saturday and all Melbourne wanted to inspect it. The streams of cars began to build up on the Saturday. They came in solid cohorts on Sunday morning. Invariably, there was a queue of up to eight cars waiting for the look. Some people walked over with dogs from S.E.2 and S.E.3 and took up viewing positions outside the gate.

Mr Graham Davey, former owner and still in residence, maintained a nice amiability through it all. He found that he had to keep his gates locked and only use the back entrance. He had one regret through it all. He could have made a fortune charging for the window to chalet, the harbour and the mountain views, making a charge of a Woburn Abbey job, at 2s. 6d. a head.
