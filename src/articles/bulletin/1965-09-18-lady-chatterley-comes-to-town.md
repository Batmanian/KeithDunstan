I have been protected all my life from salacious and dangerous influences. All salacious books have been kept out of my hands.

Well, on September 3, 37 years after 'Lady Chatterley's Lover' was first published, I finally laid hands on it. Being accustomed to this kind of literature it was fascinating to note how well Lawrence wrote about loins, bellies and buttocks. Indeed it would be safe to say he is the best buttocks writer in the language. Could one give some examples? Page 84: '... little jazz girls with small-boy buttocks like two collar studs'. P. 116: 'The most secret part of her was the long sloping fall of the haunches from the socket of the back and the slumberous round stillness of the buttocks like hillocks of sand.' And there is more good buttock-writing on pages 62, 111, 163, 223, 264, 273, 274, 276, 278, 283, 285, 290, 311 and 324.

Actually, getting a copy is downright difficult. I had to confess I was well beyond the age of puberty and I would in no way reveal the name of the bookseller. I took it home carefully wrapped in brown paper and then I sat right through the night, reading every line.

It was fun — I mean, no one had ever explained before what sort of people Lady Chatterley and Mellors were, and that often the book was good fun. For example, did you know that Lady Chatterley was 'a ruddy, country-looking girl with soft brown hair, big blue eyes and curling brown hair, with a soft voice and rather strong, female loins. She was inclined to freckles with big blue eyes'. And Mellors: had 'pure, white, delicate-looking hands, brown moustache and distant eyes'.

The first surprise came when I discovered that this really wasn't a society-working class love affair. Connie's father was only an artist and her mother a Fabian, intellectuals, and not true Tatler Society at all! Then we discover that Mellors one time had a commission in the army and he was a scholarship scholar. 'The game-keeper Mellors is a curious kind of person. He might almost be a gentleman.' But then he would slip into a Whitby more entertaining vernacular, far better than anything ever done by Ena Sharples ... 'I niver meant nuthink' ... or ... 'appen ye'd better 'ave this key, an' mind fend t'bods some other road.' 'Bods' is Derbyshire for 'binds'.

Quite the most marvellously amusing scene in the book comes on page 283. They weave forget-me-knots together, he in her maiden-hair and she in his man-hair. In some more advanced era it will make a fortune for Hollywood in Cinerama.

Yet one learns so many things from Lawrence, particularly the theme that without sex a woman is unfulfilled, she becomes thin, and wastes so regrettably away. With it she ripens like a beautiful plum. In her teens Connie used to set out for the Continent, tramp through the forests with sturdy, lusty, German youths. She knew she was all right. But now, 'she wastes away and the longish slopes of her haunches and her buttocks had lost its gleam and its sense of richness'. Her father then tells the crippled Sir Clifford that it doesn't suit Connie to be a demi-vierge. 'She's getting thin ... angular. It's not her style. She's not the pilchard sort of little slip of a girl, she's a bonny Scotch girl.'

Eventually, a child. The end is all rather messy and terribly unfinished. Mellors gets sacked from his job. He is still married to his slatternly Bertha and God help him if he isn't. Connie goes to Venice. She is in London waiting for the arrival of her baby and she writes to Mellors. Sir Clifford refuses her a divorce. So it is all terribly unfinished. We don't know whether the love affair is continued, whether Mellors divorces his wife, whether Connie, with the £20,000 left by her father, will eventually be able to set up Mellors.

Best of all, could we have an episode with Lady Chatterley and Mellors calling on Mr Rylah, pleading for better treatment for them? Can you imagine the scene! Lady Chatterley now would be a nice old thing of 71 and Mellors would be a slim-loined old boy of about 85.
