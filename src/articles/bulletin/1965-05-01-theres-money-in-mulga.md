Once upon a time the windows of Melbourne's tobacco shops were altogether irresistible. There were racks of Dunhill, Loewe, Barling and other pipes, great bins full of the finest shag, urns of snuff and cedar boxes carefully packed with coronas and other delights.

Now the tobacconist windows are full of koala bears, boomerang money boxes, kangaroo fur bookmarks and shiny mulga maps of Australia with Aboriginal art, briar and box and the odd packet of cigarettes and cigars.

Overseas tourists have spent £20 million. Some of it had to go on Australian souvenirs. Your correspondent managed to find Australia's leading souvenir man, John Frost, just before he left for Manila, Hong Kong and Japan on Sunday. The Frosts have had a family tobacco business since 1898, but now souvenirs are looking even better. Over the back of a chair Mr Frost had a pile of glistening gold and silver hides like something out of the very latest from Hong Kong. In Asia I'm planning to show all sorts of Australian merchandise: genuine boomerangs from the Queensland Native Affairs Department, toy koalas, kangaroo-hide wallets, hand-painted mulga, moccasins, decent boots, cushions, car mats, belts, rugs, passport covers, and there is even a very nice line in kangaroo fur hot-water bottle covers. What's more you can buy him cut into little furry squares — the dinkum Aussie postcard. In the window of the Southern Cross Hotel there is the ultimate kangaroo souvenir. It is the actual paw of a kangaroo, maybe 18 inches long with fur still on the upper surfaces opened up as an ashtray.

Some of the other popular items include kangaroo pouches. There is a nice selection of the best koalas. The latest are extremely attractive. You can find them with a pyjama bag inside and with your koala serving as a pyjama case. There are wind-up koalas which give forth 'Waltzing Matilda' from the depths of their stomachs. Size is no limit: John Frost recalls that the biggest koala order he ever received was from Mike Todd. He wanted a toy koala and a toy kangaroo each nearly five feet high. The koala cost £58 and the kangaroo £68. They were so large he bought another plane ticket so that they could ride back with him and Liz in the aircraft cabin to the US. Yes, the koala and kangaroo had their very own seat.

Mulga, of course, is in the timber line. Think of any object and it is available in mulga. There are mulga coum leaf ash trays, mulga book-ends, mulga crib boards, clocks, calendars, inkstands, nut crackers, boomerangs, paper knives. There is an intriguing example of the mulga art in a set of coasters embellished with the map of Australia. There are shell or opal cufflinks, sheepskin jackets and dinner plates decorated with Aboriginal art. With each comes a pamphlet in several languages.

Can Australia create the right export pace? The Qantas image has not been without concern. Could a boomerang advertising campaign be fruitful? These are the questions exercising Mr F. G. Langer, managing director of Portex Trading Co., the man who is metalising those skins for export. He said: 'I'm not worried. There are 300 million kangaroos in Australia. In Queensland and West Australia they're a pest. We get most of ours from Queensland and they reproduce so fast you need a force so strong that you need to reduce the numbers. Moreover, male kangaroos have such tremendous muscular force around the pouch that a baby joey could have no contact whatever with the male.'

This was something to think about for the male kangaroo.
