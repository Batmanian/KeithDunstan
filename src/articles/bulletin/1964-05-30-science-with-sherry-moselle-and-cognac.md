Unquestionably the most exciting serial we have ever offered on this page has been the saga of Victoria's liquor. In our last episodes our protagonist, Mr P. D. Phillips, QC, Royal Commissioner, was deeply involved in his liquor inquiry. He had done battle with the breweries, he had brilliantly persuaded some of our leading clergy to his own way of thinking, and he had been on a personal tour of Melbourne pubs at closing time. NOW READ ON.

Some months back Mr Phillips intimated that he would hold a test dinner, not so much a temperance affair, but an occasion where they could really find out, scientifically, what happens at a dinner party. Dear reader, this party has taken place and it was a beauty. The venue was University House.

Now, you must understand that University House, in effect, is the professors' club at Melbourne University. The lovely old awning from Ogg's Pharmacy is outside and the whole place has a reputation. An association of ease and grace and, my dears, it has to be savoured, for nobody can do it in a hurry.

Among those invited were Mr Kevin Coleman, assisting the Commission, and Mrs Coleman, Mr R. F. G. Fogarty, chairman of all our breweries, Mr S. W. Johnson, Senior Lecturer in Criminology, Mr E. Sharp, of the Wine and Brandy Producers' Association, the Rev. D. Hurse of the Presbyterian Church, and Dr J. E. Sewell.

Before dinner there was a choice of martinis or Rhine Castle's Ramada sherry. At dinner the first course was a choice of consommé or spaghetti marinara. The next course was fresh-water trout, which was served with Rhine Castle Victorian bin 92 moselle. This done, there was filet mignon and what was described as a rather lovely Cowalta Hunter River hermitage, vintage 1958. Then, if one didn't want the cassata, the mellow dry hermitage could carry one through the cheeses. Finally, with the coffee there was Cockburn's port from Portugal, Remy Martin cognac VSOP, Drambuie or Benedictine; as Mr Phillips put it: 'A very enjoyable dinner party.'

Everything was scientifically recorded under the sharp eyes of the pathology expert, Dr Norman McCallum, and the police surgeon, Dr John Birrell. The star performer of the evening, and the deliberate test case, had two martinis, one dry sherry, two white wines, one red wine, a port and one liqueur. His breathalyser reading was .053. The police are inclined to look upon .050 as the plimsoll mark. They like to natter head-to-head.

Mr Phillips obviously was pleased with the whole affair. Everyone drank varying amounts of alcohol, but no one more than the equivalent of 9½ 7oz beers. So, if liquor was consumed slowly in relaxed conditions in not unreasonable quantities, one wouldn't come to any harm. As he put it: 'It seems that people who show a reading of more than one per cent in police tests must be having one hell of a time.'

Well, the last guest finally departed from University House at 11 p.m. Naturally, we were agog to know who paid for the feast. It was a little disappointing to discover later that it was what we used to call a 'Yankee shout': everybody chipped in.

Of course, we can hardly wait now to see what is coming next. Mr Phillips said earlier that the dinner party was only one of several experiments he had in mind. He also wanted to see what happened at a smoke social, and one can only presume that the queue of scientific volunteers for this will be bigger than for the Beatles.

The old Royal Commission hands report that they have never seen a man so happy in his job. PD has style and aplomb. He always looks nicely turned out, with white cuffs and gold links. The Herald reported one day that he was a very modern character in a synthetic suit. PD corrected this at once. It was not a synthetic suit. It was pure Veronese silk.

As he warms to his subject he is an interesting man. He feels that our drinking habits are most revealing. He says that Australians have great difficulty in communicating; there is a strange sort of restraint or reserve which prevents them from putting over their true feelings. They only really become alive and actually communicate in the warm atmosphere of a bar. That is why you don't see many chairs in Melbourne pubs. People don't really want them. They like to natter head-to-head.

And in the Commission itself he likes to wander down side alleys. One day he got on to this most delicate point: how does one behave when one is a club guest. Said PD: 'I'm never quite sure when I'm expected to buy a drink. The more exalted the club, the less inclined I am to offer to buy a drink.'

PD's instinct was right. We checked that out with several utterly exalted clubs, and they were affronted at the very idea of a guest paying. However, on lesser levels of exaltation, guests seemed to do a lot of paying.

Some feedback: Nobody seems to know whether this Royal Commission will run as long as 'Blue Hills'. The first draft of the transcripts has run to 308,000 words. The end is not in sight yet. PD has said that he may not file his report until the New Year. He did make one revealing comment at one day's hearing: 'I was invited to report to the Government on various aspects of the liquor industry. I don't know whether it was a joke or whether some useful purpose will be served.'

Will PD produce the goods? Will he recommend longer hours? What will a potentially hostile Labor Government do? To get the answer to these questions don't miss next week's thrilling episode of BATMAN.
