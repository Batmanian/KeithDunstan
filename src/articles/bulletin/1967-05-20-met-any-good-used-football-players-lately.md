IT HAS BEEN an awfully good start to the footy season. Mr. Gough Whitlam came down here and had himself fitted out in a Collingwood football guernsey. What's more he had himself presented with a Collingwood football badge which he wore in his lapel. How do you do this? Most politicians understand the oath of office and that's about all. Collingwood is the biggest club, and, as was said at the time, he's getting with the strength.

What else has happened this year? You know the old practice in America of having baby's first pair of booties 'permanised'. You don't? Well, in New York it is quite the thing. For $10 or so you can have baby's booties permanised in bronze, 'every lovable wrinkle preserved forever.' Then the proud parents, of course, hang them from the rear-vision mirror, or use them as book-ends.

Now this can be done in Melbourne, but nobody is interested in baby's booties. We have made a natural cultural progression befitting our city. The news is that you can buy footy boots permanised in a coating of copper. So if you have in the family a great football hero who is scouts-rest hanging up his boots, you can have them permanised in copper, preserving every lovable wrinkle. The great boots of Ray Gabelich, former captain of Collingwood, have already been done.

The most important development that has happened this season is this: we now have an open transfer market. Only three years ago the very mention of the words 'transfer fee' would have shocked League officials to the backs of their guernseys. It's like incest, everyone knows it goes on, but it is never mentioned in polite society.

The Coulter Law, which forbids the buying and selling of footballers, came into force in 1930. It never quite stopped trade, but one had to purchase one's footballer with the utmost discretion. You formed your select group of rich patrons, or 'the coterie' as one club calls it. Then to get your man you offered him a house, a Holden, a seat on the board of directors, a job in a nice company — anything, as unseemly as making it a straight cash transaction.

The Coulter Law is still in force, but this year, apparently by some sort of mute agreement, everybody has decided it doesn't matter any more. Indeed, rather than keep the matter a secret, it is considered pretty good public relations to leak out the transfer fee. It is like being the top boy at the leading Merseyside group, or the president of General Motors, and it is very much the symbol of success.

Would you like a little bit of a price list? The price for the Latrobe Valley League Bairnsdale player, Michael Redenback, was $500. Fitzroy had to pay $800 to get Alec Ruscuklic from Morwell. Fitzroy had to hand over $2000 to get back their former captain, Kevin Murray, from Western Australia. The Victorian Football Association has laid down a minimum price of $3000 for a transfer to the Victorian Football League.

There has been a total breakdown in relations between the VFL and the VFA. There is a move to make $400 the standard payment for a transfer from the country leagues, but the real transfer of the year is Peter Hudson, the new Hawthorn full-forward.

Peter Hudson was a sort of dream full-forward who had kicked hundreds of goals for New Norfolk, Tasmania. New Norfolk laid down a flat price to Hawthorn of $10,000. Much bargaining went on and the general opinion is that Hudson exchanged hands for $8000. Some say he was bought on a fine old time-payment system — $2000 down and $100 weekly. If this is true, Hawthorn could be in trouble. If they fall behind in payments, or suddenly at 2.15 p.m. one Saturday afternoon hear a knock at the door, bailiffs will appear outside the dressing-room and Peter Hudson will be repossessed.

Regrettably, Mr. Hudson is not kicking too many goals at the moment and he is costing about $1000 a goal, but that is beside the point. It is a very good thing that all this has come into the open. The Coulter Law was designed to protect the poorer clubs, but curiously enough it is the poorer clubs which have been most aggressive in trying to buy new players. Perhaps we may soon switch to the American system where millionaires or well-heeled actors like Gene Autry buy and finance baseball teams. Instead of having a Dame Pattie syndicate we might have a Fitzroy syndicate. Instead of Mr. Aubrey Gibson pouring his money into the National Gallery he might pour his money into North Melbourne or Footscray.

There was a suggestion in the 'Herald,' even, of applying the logic of advertisers to the sale of used cars. This would be a very good idea. Out on the Nepean Highway we could set up a Used Footballer Lot. The footballers could be labelled 'one owner,' 'slightly used,' 'A1 warranty,' 'only suffered minor damage.' However, one would expect used footballers to be in clean condition, cartilages in good shape, and possessing the right number of ears.

There would be a fixed scale for depreciation. A footballer who had been out on the road for 30 years could not expect to fetch as much as a smart, new, polished footballer of only 20. We would keep a doctor on hand to give a sort of RACV inspection service for buyers, and Custom Credit would be on hand to provide easy terms.

Any teams that were over strong in one department or weak in another could do good business through the Used Footballer Lot. You may smile at all this, but Victorian Football could very well come to it.

There's one aspect that does not get so much publicity. Despite all the television coverage on football, and even on the 'Herald,' attendance does not seem to be thriving. Here are the total attendance figures for the first four rounds for the past five years: 1963, 701,666; 1964, 660,069; 1965, 671,517; 1966, 658,217; 1967, 590,396.

Some of these figures were boosted by split rounds but it is plain that despite rising new grounds, 1963 was the best year and 1967 is the second worst.
