BASED on July 14, the date of our 44th derailment for 1964, Mr Bolte said that he had every confidence in the Victorian railways. 'Indeed,' said he, 'they are the best in the world!' At the time there was considerable doubt as to whether we would score a century of derailments for the year, we were making barely six derailments a month. Since then we have picked up magnificently and now, to coin a phrase, we should bolt it in. Just as we were going to press there were three in one day, at 8 a.m., 9 a.m. and 10 a.m., to bring the year's total to 85.

That night the Labor and Country Party Opposition launched a censure motion, a dull and unrewarding affair. Mr Meagher, the Railways Minister, pointed out that we had only one derailment for every nine million miles travelled, then he came out with one beautiful line which should always be preserved in the cutting books: 'In 1962-63 the miles covered by the railways increased in "leaps and bounds" to over 207 million truck miles.' The Country Party called for an open inquiry into the railways but they have no chance of getting it.

What's really wrong with the Victorian railways? Back in 1949 the Hollway Government brought out Mr John Elliot, now Sir John Elliot, the British transport expert. He said the main thing wrong with the Victorian Railways was politics. In his report he wrote that the VR suffered from 'all the frustrating defects of a machine bound hand and foot by parliament'. And he said it was fantastic that a railway should have to provide cheap rates to the various sections of the community as an obvious subsidy. If the Government wanted to subsidise the wheat grower, then it should pay the cash direct to the farmer. The result was, he said, 'muddled economy and a semi-bankrupt transport system'. In his opinion the VR should be free of politics, like the BBC.

Now 15 years later the situation is beautifully unchanged. According to the last annual report the railways were carrying superphosphate at an average of 2.14d. a ton mile, barley at 2.86d., wheat at 3.34d., and oats at 3.43d. These are fantastically cheap prices, rates which would barely pay for the axle grease. Mr Bolte, though, wouldn't dare to put the goods traffic on true paying prices for fear of upsetting the country vote, and in particular the Country Party, which has now won back the balance of power in the Upper House.

Meanwhile, the bankrupt railways go short of £96 million, to pay for the type of modern railway trucks needed to stop 100 derailments a year.

Any private organisation can pay above the award rates. Not so the railways. As soon as a young man graduates from the Railways Technical College at Newport, said to be one of the finest technical colleges in the Southern Hemisphere, he's likely to be offered an engineering job at a great deal more than the railways can pay. The Victorian Railways at present have a staff shortage of more than 2000. Good maintenance and provision of the best service are hardly possible with such a staff shortage.

Who wants them, price and all? Who knows, one day we might get a decent price for the whole outfit.
