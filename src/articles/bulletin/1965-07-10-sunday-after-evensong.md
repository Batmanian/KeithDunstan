Word has reached us that the NSW Government is looking at the problem of the six-day week, and the time may even come when NSW will have Sunday picture shows.

Sunday picture shows are legal in Queensland, and Tasmania and Western Australia has had them for 30 years. Queensland treats it as a dangerous subject like liquor and other part of their own Sunday options. The first Sunday movies came to us on December 27, 1964. Mr Graham said it was a mistake for picture shows to open. Despite our reputation for respectability, we are actually well behind the NSW. We were ahead with TV, TAB, we were ahead with a great many pictures. Why, we even had afternoon racing on Sundays in 1963.

After all, the screening of Blue Murder was watched by a committee in a law based on an English statute introduced in 1780 during the reign of George III — the Cinematograph Act for preventing certain misuse and uses before the Lord's Day came.

The Chief Secretary, Mr Rylah, was terribly careful. According to the Bill, film shows must not begin before 4.30 p.m. This meant that all cinemas must end the Evensong before the movies started. Indeed by forcing them at last at 4.30 he was even interfering with Sunday TV. Mr Rylah said he was determined. He said he was not encouraging Sunday shows. He said the exhibitors provide public-interest performances to be judged. He would not hesitate to repeal this legislation if the public wanted 'morally unsuitable films', meaning no horror films on Sunday and nothing you shouldn't see.

Young people should be taught how to enjoy their own minds. And apart from the art galleries, the library, the variety of things to see like the bush, and films in place, they should think what they can give. Why not try visiting people, in hospitals, to people who never get a visitor?

Sir Irving seemed to miss the point. After 4.30 p.m. Melbourne is not much good for looking at the best Sunday reviews.

Oddly enough, the real opposition came from the Theatrical Employers' Association. It demanded that their workers on any Sunday show should receive one third of a week's pay with a quarter holiday pay. Lovely money if the union could get it. For musicians, the rate was £8 10s. for suburban workers. Of city workers, all but about four hours' work.

It would be poor provider for either one-third of a week's pay with a quarter of a week's pay with a day off for a week's pay with a day off. I. R. Longbeam of Retail said: 'The Theatrical Employers' Association is

to be congratulated on its stand against Sunday films. It is to be hoped that these conditions will be strong to persuade them to forego their Sundays.'

The union put its 'black ban' on Sunday and it is still on. Also, we can inform you that the theatre owners, any of the theatres operated by the Hoyts chain, the Village chain and Metro. However, according to Mr Graham, the chairman of the Cinematograph Exhibitors Association, the drive-ins are showing Sunday films. Of the 36 metropolitan theatres, 26 drive-ins are showing Sunday films, of which 12 belong to Hoyts. They get better than regular union wages. No theatre in the city or suburbs, no theatre members have bowed to the monetary sop.
