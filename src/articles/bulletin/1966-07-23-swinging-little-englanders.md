It's interesting how different governments go about spreading goodwill. Last week we had Mr Charlton Heston spreading sweetness and Mark Twain on behalf of the U.S. State Department. This week we had Keith Ovenden and Jeremy Burford giving us swinging England on behalf of the Commonwealth Relations Office. Actually they are on a universities debating tour and already they have been to Perth, ANU, Melbourne, Monash, and now on to Adelaide, Brisbane, Sydney, Newcastle, and Armidale. The idea is for the brilliant young to meet the young. The U.K. Government pays their fares, our universities feed and pillow them. A man from the High Commission was there to sort of keep an eye. 'We'd rather, of course, that they didn't slate the Old Country too much, but nor do they have to agree with the Government.'

The two were cleverly chosen. Keith Ovenden is 6ft 4½in., Beatle-haired, and, until he talks, rather like the ruckman recruit from Manangataing. He's a double honours in Institutions and English Literature from Keele University in Staffordshire and a former president of the Keele Union. Lovely contrast to Jeremy Burford, 6ft 5½in., who is smooth, silky Cambridge. He is an honours law man and a former president of the Cambridge Union. He is going on to a Fulbright at Oxford. Ovenden is going on to a Fulbright to Michigan to read politics.

In Perth the debate was 'The Westminster System is the worst form of Government apart from all the others.' At Monash it was 'Columbus went too far.' And at Melbourne that 'Culturally this century regrets having been born in the 20th Century.'

Your correspondent, who still retains an affection for this century, went to hear this last debate. In the negative corner we had Keith Ovenden, Miss Morag Fraser, an ex-editor of Farrago, and Rachael Walsh, president of Sydney SRC. Batting in the affirmative corner we had Jeremy Burford, Gareth Evans, president of Melbourne SRC, and Patrick McCaughey, an M.A. student and 'Age' Art critic.

It would be a mistake to bore you on all of it, as it was very long and some of it was very bad, but for many of the others the prospect of the debate ahead was awful. All speakers felt that all could be saved somehow at the Melbourne University Buffet.

A question from the floor asked Rachael Walsh: 'Are your attempts at wit a further demonstration of life without culture?' 'Yes,' said she sadly, 'I am exhibiting something the 20th century truly has been good at producing.'

But it was the visitors who frequently kept things moving. Miss Fraser quoted from various volumes at great length until a student two rows back stood up and said: 'From where I am, I can't hear a word you are saying.' Jeremy Burford said: 'I CAN hear and I'm willing to swop places.'

As I say, the visitors were skilled. Ovenden pulled his weight when he opened with: 'I'm the short one.' Then said: 'It is my duty to warn you against Mr Burford. He is a liar from Cambridge. He has devoted most of his study to criminology and when he comes before you remember that anything he says is the product of an unscrupulous legal mind. The last time he was at the cinema was when he was four. That's when he went out bawling after three minutes. Cambridge is known for its lovely dreaming spires. Not so, anymore. It was renowned for its screaming tyres.' Then he proceeded to demolish the 20th century. 'Mr Burford is a product of this. I come from London, "the swinging city." This is designated by Time and Life magazines to inform the world that London is the most exciting place on earth. And they do this just before the Americans move in with their own mass culture, the Bunny Club, Batman and Pop Art. All this serves just greatly to excite the tourist to visit the swinging city so that they absorb this mass culture themselves.'

And then Keith Ovenden proceeded to cover the ugly breakdown in modern culture. He described the Beatles as just an example of brilliant selling on the consumer market. They were, he could play, couldn't sing, and hadn't got real talent. Then came up a man who had gained his culture at a Staffordshire university. He said quite frightful things about the twentieth century. Only 'Z Cars' was any good and I'm glad he didn't bring up 'Coronation Street.'

Then, the tall one, Jeremy Burford. He said: 'I'm not the Keith Ovenden,' then went on. 'The new British is a changed man.' And he was, too, a debater with respect only for his creator. He was a good debater, but the audience didn't like him all that much.

He has written off Cambridge, but, you know, the old place has discovered some pretty permanent things. I think it was in 1934 that they discovered that grasshoppers could hear with their legs. It was successfully proved by the scientists. When they tapped a tuning fork the grasshoppers reacted by jumping very rapidly. The scientists then cut off the grasshoppers' legs, and tapped the tuning fork, and they didn't jump at all. The case was proved.

Mr Burford didn't say a word to this. He said, 'I pass,' but he did point out that the mass pop, while not being culture, had been enormously successful in the pursuit of the man in the street. He liked the hand of the common man clasping it.

Patrick McCaughey had a small start with Henry Moore and Picasso ... Admittedly, the Warden Sinclair Wilson did interrupt with: 'There's one creative force which seems to be disappearing with the pill,' and the young Mr McCaughey replied: 'Oh, I admit, there's a lot of new media about.'

It was an efficient, well-conducted debate. The motion was lost 200 to 71 with 27 abstentions, and so we went home somewhat comforted that our century was not lost after all.
