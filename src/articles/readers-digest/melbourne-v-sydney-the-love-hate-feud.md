As a Melbourne man, I was taught early in life that a Sydney type of person was to be found in Melbourne for a very natural reason — it was only natural, when the Royal Family decided to visit Australia, that they should be received in Melbourne. It was only natural when an important trade delegation arrived that they would do business in Melbourne. The banks had their headquarters in Melbourne. One just preferred to do business in Melbourne.

There was alarm in Melbourne, therefore, when Sydney firms began relocating their headquarters northward, and considerable satisfaction whenever a Sydney concern moved its head office south. This was not viewed as commercial fact but as a matter of civic honour.

I should confess that I belong to a generation of Melbourne men who were happy at Geelong Grammar, contemptuous of Sydney Grammar, and who regarded New South Wales as a separate and slightly inferior country beginning somewhere north of Albury.

**Head-On Collision**

There was one constitutional flaw in Melbourne's assumption of pre-eminence. Until Parliament decided on the precise location of the new Australian capital, the Federal members would meet in Melbourne. Melbourne assumed, naturally, that this pleasant arrangement would continue indefinitely.

It did not. Canberra was built, Parliament moved, and Melbourne never quite forgave the Commonwealth.

The rivalry sharpened. Melbourne's admittedly more moderate attitude in civic matters did not prevent its boosters from publicising everything Victoria had that New South Wales had not. Sydney people returned the compliment with vigour. There was genuine bitterness — expressed most freely through newspapers, radio, football and the determination of each city's residents to visit the other as infrequently as possible.

One of the running grievances concerned the arts. Sydney's Opera House was initiated and managed almost entirely by lotteries — a bold, if financially precarious, method of civic improvement. Melbourne's Arts Centre plans were slower, less dramatic, more orthodox in funding. This gave Sydney the early advantage of a visible, arguable, internationally famous building site, and Sydney was not modest about pressing it.

The Melbourne press was not above pointing out, when the opportunity arose, that the Opera House had cost rather more than its original estimate, and was taking rather longer to build than promised.

In the 1960s, Premier Henry Bolte and his New South Wales counterpart Robert Heffron maintained a cheerful, pointed public antagonism that Keith's Bulletin column turned into a running entertainment: jetport routes, industrial investment, where interstate visitors should properly alight. Bolte once proposed, not entirely in jest, that the two premiers settle the old Albury railway-gauge question in person, in a railway carriage stranded precisely on the border.

**Comparing the Airports**

The rivalry found perhaps its most concrete expression in airports. When both cities built new international terminals, the competition was conducted in square footage, VIP lounges and terminal capacity with the same intensity previously devoted to cricket scores and beer consumption.

The Sydney terminal at Mascot covered 490,000 square feet. Melbourne's Tullamarine managed 330,000. Sydney had three VIP rooms; Melbourne had two. The debate continued in the newspapers for months, with each city's partisans interpreting the differential according to their own civic convictions.

The debate went on all through 1973 — and, one suspects, will go on rather longer than that. What keeps it alive is not malice but a deep, shared assumption that comparison is the natural condition of Australian civic life, and that the other city is always, in some small but important way, getting away with something.

Whether that is a love-hate feud or simply patriotism by another name is perhaps, in the end, a matter of which side of Albury you were born on.
