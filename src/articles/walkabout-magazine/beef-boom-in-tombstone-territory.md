IT WAS HARD TO GET USED to the casual way they talked about "The Peninsula". I kept thinking about the other peninsula, Victoria's Mornington Peninsula, which is also "The Peninsula". People in Toorak and wool barons of the Western District, after they have made their first half-million, always buy a summer shack at Portsea.

On both peninsulas there is much tea-tree. On Cape York Peninsula there is also much tea-tree, but there the similarity ends. The big peninsula is no place for big shiny cars with nice springing. Bitumen doesn't exist, and the bumps send you sailing up through the roof of the landrover.

The country is hot, dusty, flat and unbelievably devoid of human beings. Every 50 yards there are strange nine-foot-high ant hills, flat, triangular, with the sharp edges always running magnetically north and south. They look lonely, very eerie, like tombstones of another civilisation. They look even more eerie as the sun starts to sink, and long shadows appear on the red, dry earth, Tombstone Territory.

In the heat of the day not a thing moves. It seems odd to see country where there seems nothing alive except the ants in their tombstones, nothing in the sky, a quiet almost beyond belief. Yet in the early morning there is the shattering racket of a million birds: galahs, black cockatoos, white cockatoos, jabirus, Burdekin duck, geese, pelicans, budgies and, out around the waterholes in squads of 20 or more, fat, black wild pigs.

Another difference from Mornington Peninsula is that it's dry, dry as an old tennis court for seven months a year. Then, after Christmas, all their rain comes in at once, and the whole peninsula is a quagmire. In many areas it is more than that, just islands sticking above a great lake. Old hands will tell you stories about cattle after the Wet, their legs bereft of all hair, from standing for months in water. At this time, unless you have a high level airstrip, travel ceases; road travel can be forgotten until after May. Isolation has come.

On Mornington Peninsula, the livestock is largely what you can see in bikinis. On Cape York the livestock has always been a run-of-the-mill creature, best described as a shorthorn. His quality has never been high, and the locals have facetiously dubbed him the "scrubmaster". He has wandered unhampered over vast areas of the north, and very often has exchanged ownership through the cattle duffer.

There are no fancy homesteads, no display of wealth, and the country has always been poor. So there is no Victorian Western District baronial tradition. The turn-over in properties has been high. People mostly have moved in, grabbed what they could, and moved on.

But the name, "The Peninsula", is dying, because now it is known as the Gunn country and for a good reason. Few people in Australia have not heard of Sir William Gunn. He is chairman of the Wool Board and the International Wool Secretariat, a grazier in his own right, and an extremely active and influential businessman. He went to the United States, interested Americans in Australia's north, and, using entirely American capital, he bought up property after property. It would be untrue to say that he controls all Cape York Peninsula, but there are only two or three quality properties left out of the arrangement. Under his company, Gunn Rural Management, he has Laura, Lakefield, Silver Plains, Bizant, Kalpowar and Merluna which includes Batavia Downs, York Downs and Bertiehaugh, a total of more than six million acres.

He has another six million acres further afield at the huge Northern Territory development, Tipperary. This too is backed by American dollars.

So far, on the peninsula he has spent more than $5 million. When you think of all the brave schemes that have taken place in the north and how few of them have succeeded, it all seems a fantastic gamble. I toured these properties with young managers, Greg Webster at Kalpowar who is 25, Don Stewart at Lakefield, 26, Rob Gifford at Bizant, 27, and Noel Reid at Silver Plains, who is about 40. They are young men to be dealing in millions of dollars, but this was arranged with a special purpose. It takes the young to be sufficiently enthusiastic to deal in new methods.

The Gunn people are gambling entirely on three things: improving the quality of the stock with selective breeds, fences and a supplementary diet of molasses and legume crops.

Many of the old hands up there think they are out of their minds. Take fences, for example. Except for the bull paddock and the home paddock, fences used to be unheard of on the peninsula. Many said that those scrubmasters were so used to roaming free, that, once behind a fence, they'd fret and die.

Greg Webster took me on a long drive around Kalpowar. During 1966 he put in 120 miles of fences. This has enabled him to undertake a careful breeding program with eight pure-bred Brahman bulls, steadily to raise the quality of the stock.

It is an experience seeing Brahmans for the first time. They recall the old joke about the camel, the creature designed by a committee. The Brahman is a sickly grey, has a great humped back, a knobbly head and ears like drain pipes. He is drought resistant, tick resistant and a splendid beef producer, which is all that really matters. Greg says you get used to them; they seem to fit into the landscape. In fact you get so indoctrinated that when you return south the British breeds look peculiar.

Anyway Greg Webster loves them. On his four-wheel-drive he has a special cattle-caller. He works it with a lever under his steering wheel. He can make it give a beautifully passionate moo.

We came to a paddock where a tractor was in action with a 22-run plough. Greg said: "We are putting in 650 acres of legumes here, mainly Townsville lucerne. We're doing a little farming." His grin was large when he said "farming", because this is fantastic. People just don't farm on the peninsula. It is something odd, like growing bananas in Martin Place.

But behind this lies the whole theory of future success. The grasses in the dry season provide little nutrition, so that when the time comes for turning off the cattle they are in wretched condition. If some way can be found to build them up between June and November then real money can be made.

They must have protein, so all the Gunn properties are adopting the same program. Firstly they are feeding them molasses mixed with urea and phosphoric acid. At Kalpowar, for 5,000 cattle they use 400 gallons a day. They pour it into troughs, each with a 40-gallon drum in the centre, on an axle. It is most fascinating to see the cattle in action. They lick the drum, the suction of their big, long tongues on the metal is sufficient to turn over the drum and scoop up the molasses.

The next item on the diet is the high-protein grass, the legumes, which when produced in quantity will make for a high density of cattle to the acre. The feed doesn't only mean better beef; it means better production all round. Noel Reid at Silver Plains says that, before, they were lucky to get a 30 per cent calving rate, but with legume supplementary diet they are boosting it to 80 or 90 per cent.

Very similar principles are being applied at the King Ranch property at Tully, south of Cairns and in the right-hand corner of the peninsula's base. Here, American-Australian capital is under the chairmanship of a different knight, Sir Rupert Clarke. The manager is a big, amiable Texan, Neil Alderman.

King Ranch has been going just over four years, and they have an astonishing 11,000 head of cattle on 5,000 acres. They plan to boost the herd to 20,000 with a Santa Gertrudis stud.

Tully has a rainfall of 157 inches a year. On March 13, 1967, they had 18½ inches in one day and 50 inches in three days. The grass really is as high as an elephant's eye, and you can almost see it growing. Neil Alderman says the place is really a fattening property, and many of the cattle come in from another King Ranch station, Brunette Downs, out west on the Northern Territory border. There the country is like the back of the moon. The Brunette cattle get such a terrible shock when they see all that green grass that for a time they won't touch it.

Tully country was once swamp, open forest and almost impenetrable rain forest. It seemed a very cockeyed thought that it could ever be cattle country. Even if they cleared it, the re-growth would ruin everything and there was no nourishment in the grass. Well, Neil Alderman has been using five big bulldozers to smash down the rain forest. He has ploughed and planted legumes. Just like the properties on Cape York Peninsula, planting legumes has been the answer.

Far north country, including the peninsula, is some of the most exciting in Australia, but what do they do for recreation? Several properties are among the most remote on earth. Silver Plains, for example, is 390 miles by appalling road from Cairns. Noel Reid and his wife Helen, live there, virtually alone. I asked Noel if they went swimming, but in the rivers there are crocodiles, and in the sea there is the choice of sharks, stingrays, stone-fish, sea wasps and gropers. "You name it, we have everything," he said.

That seemed to settle fishing, too. Greg Webster, of Kalpowar, goes oyster hunting, and around Princess Charlotte Bay there are thousands. They are a huge variety, as big as cricket balls, and one virtually needs to eat them with a knife and fork.

Then there's cricket. I attended the annual cricket match between Coen and Laura which took place at Musgrave station. It was said that half the entire Cape York Peninsula was there; that is nigh on 70 people, and they came from 300 miles around. It was a three-day affair.

On the first night there was a great barbecue and everybody sat outside, in front of the old Musgrave homestead. Like all homesteads in these parts it is made entirely of galvanised iron, walls, ceilings, the lot; first, because galvanised iron is cheap and easy to transport, second because white ants don't like it.

They had two truckloads loaded with stubbies of beer, and this was available night and day, including the tea break during the cricket, which was better known as the stubbie break.

And I have never seen so much steak. Most of the cattlemen had at least three at a time. It was like eating sugar in sugar country; one had to be patriotic and keep on eating in order to support a great industry. At night a full moon came up. The cattlemen brought out their guitars and everybody sang. This, so far from that other peninsula down south, was an unforgettable experience.

Next day cricket began at 9.30 a.m. before heat made play difficult. The stockmen played in their big hats and some of them in their elastic-sided, high-heeled boots. Others played in bare feet, although the ground temperature must have been well over 180 degrees. It is a new experience seeing a batsman all padded up and in bare feet. Shins may be tender but northern toes can resist anything. Coen had a very fine batting victory, dismissing Laura for 68 runs, which Coen made easily, with only two wickets down.

It was a pretty scene. The grandstand on the edge of the ground was far more attractive than the Melbourne Cricket Ground's. The uprights were pieces of tea-tree, and across the top there was a thatch of fresh gum leaves.

But we have wandered from the serious subject in hand. What is the general reaction to Sir William Gunn and this vast use of American capital? It is not all friendly. There is plenty of talk about selling the country to the Yanks, making it the 51st State, and so on. The Gunn people reply that nothing has happened in the far north for 100 years, and if they do not develop it nobody else will. And they ask whether American capital in beef is any different from American capital in iron ore or in the manufacture of Fords and Holdens.

In Cape York the story, right now, is straight beef production. And at Tipperary, by Katherine, N.T., the Gunn people have teams of great tractors, and they are planting thousands of acres of sorghum. There is no hope of any profit in the next five years, and for many years they will be ploughing back the profits they do make into further expansion.

There is also the point that these ventures have yet to come off, and the Americans are making a big gamble that Australians are not prepared to make. It could fail like some of the other northern ventures. But if it does come good others will want to settle, and settlers will be rushing up there by the busload.

Meanwhile I am wondering if Sir William Gunn will eventually find a holiday house on the other peninsula.
