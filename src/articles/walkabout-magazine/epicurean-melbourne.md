Just lately the Royal Commonwealth Society in Melbourne has been running a real he-man's lunch . . . naturally for men only. They start with half a pint of beer. Then they have thick pea soup, then steak and kidney pudding, then steamed roly-poly with jam and cream, then blue cheese and biscuits. When I was there the honorary treasurer finished it all with a little ceremony. He took a little box from his pocket, and dropped a pellet of saccharine into his coffee. It was a gesture. After all, a man has to watch his weight.

These gentlemen of the Royal Commonwealth Society are fighting a fine rearguard action. In Melbourne now there are Polish, French, Greek, Hungarian, Spanish, Russian, Turkish, Jewish, German, Swiss, Japanese, Polynesian, Indian, Italian, Chinese and, it is said, even an Australian restaurant or two. It has become so cosmopolitan that, for the connoisseur, steamed jam roly-poly is hard to come by; and it is certainly easier to come by shish kebab or lasagne verdi than steak and kidney pudding.

Back in the immediate post-war years, admittedly, there were a few excellent restaurants like the Florentino, Mario's, the Society, the Ritz and Molina's. Elsewhere there was a pattern. One could go into almost any café in town, and order steak and eggs for 2/6; or, if he wanted to be a little devilish, steak for 3/6. This normally consisted of steak and eggs AND onions, plus a slice or two of tomato and a piece of lettuce incredibly cut into streamers. The climax to the meal was white tea or white coffee. These, no doubt for efficiency in handling, were designed so that both tasted exactly the same. As for the interior décor, sometimes there was a picture of Carbine on the wall, but more often a dear old, framed picture of a mid-Victorian maiden in her nightie, sitting on the grass looking soulfully at a waterfall.

The change has been spectacular. In the place of the mid-Victorian maiden, there is a piece of driftwood, a monstera deliciosa plant and, in extreme cases, a waterfall. You eat, in the dark, exotic foods with names you can't spell and accept prices which sometimes look better on an expense account. The Florentino, in this regard, handles things very tactfully. The menu for the host carries a blue ribbon, for the guest a yellow ribbon. But only the blue ribbon menu carries the prices.

It is hard to put a finger on the exact reason for this change in eating habits. Some say it is due to the new enlightened system of giving more restaurants wine licences. Some believe that Melbourne, which has absorbed more migrants than any other city, has found from them this new way to live. But there is more to it than that. Australians are a very footloose people. They have learned through travel, and, since 1946, there's the simple fact that there has been more money about.

We have had a few oddities like a Chinese smorgasbord and even a café that served continental Worcestershire Sauce, but I firmly believe there are few cities where restaurant eating is so interesting and varied. Let us look at some of the newer eating houses.

The Capers in Collins Street has a glassed-over courtyard, ivy on the walls and, much to the joy of the Gas and Fuel Corporation, real gas lamps. The proprietor and his staff like to keep things moving. They have French weeks, Spanish weeks, Italian weeks. The night I was there it was Scottish week. All the walls were draped in tartan, there were pots of Scotch thistle, and heather had been flown from Scotland. The haggis was piped in by a highlander in the kilt and Robert Burns's 'Ode to the Haggis' was read by Jock Dyall, secretary of the Royal Caledonian Society.

*Fair fa' your honest sonsie face,*
*Great chieftain o' the pudding-race!*

At the ode's most stirring moment, Jock Dyall waved a great carving knife in the air and drove it deep into the haggis. The Sassenachs among us were prepared to swear, at that moment, that from inside the haggis came a little anguished cry.

Across town, close to the financial centre in Bank Place, is Sukiyaki House. In the first week that it opened last September, it was reported that a gentleman came out, looked down in astonishment, and hurried back inside. He had on the wrong shoes. Clearly this was a new approach. All our Greek, Russian, Hungarian, French and other restaurants may have been able to mix up hats, coats, scarves and umbrellas, but this, a restaurant that could mix up shoes, was different.

For Sukiyaki House is the real thing. The guests do take off their shoes. There are Japanese cooks, and the girls are in their kimonos and are the Japanese wives of former Australian servicemen. You sit on the floor on tatami mats, but if your Western bones start to creak, you can lower your undercarriage into a well under the table. The main dish, of course, is sukiyaki and there are other delicacies such as raw snapper and raw squid.

At the opening there were some surprises. Staid Melburnians weren't used to the service. They were surprised at the way the Japanese girls plied them with hot towels and bowls of green tea. They could understand the purpose of the hot towels but one customer thought the bowl of green tea was a finger bowl, and washed his hands in it.

La Popotte is in Latrobe Street and the setting is almost perfect. The building is a very old, Melbourne residence, once said to have a most shady reputation. Now the exterior is painted a blushing pink. Ian Fairley is the proprietor. He has had a good deal of experience on the stage, as an actor-dancer. He had many years in France and he did a season at the Lido in Paris, where the door show is superb, the food exciting and the nudes even more so. Mr. Fairley also managed a restaurant on the Côte d'Azur and he wanted a restaurant that was really provincial French. He has it in La Popotte.

The menu is as provincial French as you can get. The tables are slabs of wood nearly two inches thick. There is a patio out the back and herbs grow between the stones. As for the name, Mr. Fairley says 'La Popotte' means a delicious meal that you whip up in a fifth-floor garret out of absolutely nothing.

The South Seas in Collins Street is another new one. It opened with a flourish. On the first day it had a new style of doorman, a gentleman dressed as a cannibal with a tusk through his nose. It has a chief's hut, complete with throne, coconut palms, jungle waterfall, tropical fish and crayfish pots. The cashier's desk is designed to look like a cannibal's burning cauldron. But as a symbol, I understand, it has nothing to do with the prices.

If you were one of these fortunate Automobile Club men who do nothing else but give star ratings to eating houses, you'd have to give four stars to the Balzac. It is in Wellington Parade, and the proprietor, Georges Mora, is French and he insists that this is a REAL French restaurant. It is not over-expensive. The vin ordinaire comes in lovely pottery carafes. There are ceramics by John Perceval on the walls, and it does have something of the easy-going atmosphere of a restaurant of the Left Bank. And the snails! They are eaten there by the thousands of dozens. I still find it hard to believe that Melburnians can do this, a people that was always peculiarly devoted to the national diet of pie'n'peas.

The Sideway Inn is in George Parade, just off Collins Street. Here they take their steak terribly, terribly seriously. The steaks are all tagged, say, 8/- for a six-ounce steak, or 14/- for a 12-ounce steak. The manager suggests that you might like a porterhouse, always more tasty than a fillet. And they have a notice pointing out that they will accept no responsibility should the customer want his steak well-done. Over-cooking causes loss of the vital juices and shrinkage. So you are a little nervous about suggesting it. That would be as bad as asking for steamed jam roly-poly.

For high-speed living in the silver age there is McClure's, the new £150,000 restaurant at 454 St. Kilda Road. Everything about this restaurant is slick and American. It opens at 6.30 a.m. and goes through to 12.30 a.m. Eventually it will run 24 hours a day. Keith Dixon, the manager, says that 54 per cent of American business men like to eat out for breakfast, and he feels that Australians will develop the same habit.

And was there ever a restaurant like this for saving time? At each position at the central bar there is a toaster. According to the proprietor, Les McClure, the only real way to get hot toast is to make it yourself.

At each table there is a telephone. Under each telephone there is a button, so as soon as you sit down you press the button. The cook rings back and you give your order to him direct. He writes it on a docket, which he puts under a time clock. This stamps it with the time, then he puts the docket on a great wheel which moves in and out of the kitchen, where it can be seen by the manager. The idea is that no order must ever take longer than 10 minutes. So far there has been only one complaint about the telephone system. A gentleman complained that he couldn't get through to get a beer at Werribee.

Yes, everything is geared for speed. There's a remarkable coffee machine which pants nervously in front of the kitchen. It looks like a chrome-plated Puffing Billy and it produces an incredible 16 pints of filtered, percolated coffee every 10 minutes. Turn over the menu and there's a slogan on the back, 'Eat and beat it . . . an old Paris street cry.' And I think they almost mean it.

Before we go any further, we should mention the Walnut Tree. It is in William Street, at the back of the Victoria Market, and it could rate as the most expensive place in town. A lunch there would cost you at least £3, and it is a favourite for businessmen who are trying to impress. It is actually the home of the proprietor, Mr. G. N. Sommers, an architect. The furniture, the tables, the chairs are late Georgian mahogany. There is thick, red carpet on the floor and the silver is nothing but the best sterling. The patio has a heated marble floor, and the ladies in winter just love to kick off their stiletto heels and warm their feet. There is, too, a real walnut tree, and it grows up through the roof.

But, if it is expensive, there's no question that the food is superb. You eat what Mr. Sommers likes to eat, and that means there is no menu. There are nine courses, and you go through them all, hoping bravely you will be back at the office by three . . . or four. The courses can be simple enough: oysters czarina and spiced sausages, sweetbreads in Madeira sauce with sippet, roast stuffed turkey, capon, duck, hare, fresh lobster au gratin.

From the most expensive let's go to the warmest place in town. In the Carlton area there are a number of excellent bistros like Ferdi's. The most famous of them is Jimmy Watson's. How can you describe it? It's in a more than a century old Carlton house. It is invariably bursting with businessmen, university students, plain people. The walls are covered with dreadful pictures, usually bequeathed by very bad artists.

There is a cartoon which depicts a barman saying: 'I think, old chap, in the interests of trade you should endeavour to classify the wine in rather broader categories than 'Red Ned', 'Bombo' and 'Extra Grouse Plonk'.'

In the midst of it all was Jimmy, one of the town's greatest extroverts. I say 'was' because the much beloved Jimmy sadly died last February. Always he wore a bow tie, police and fireman braces and a great brewery wagon driver's leather apron. He dispensed wine at a furious rate, sometimes at sixpence a glass, but more often in his enthusiasm to get you to try a different vintage, he would forget to charge anything.

'The fifty-eight, Sir; try the fifty-eight! A shilling? Lord, no, sixpence. What do you think this is, the saveloy piazza? Come on, come on! A bottle to drink now, a bottle to pour over your steak. A-ha! That's the way to make money, I say. It's not what they charge, what they pour over it.'

But nobody was deceived by that exterior. Jimmy had been in business a long time, and he was one of the finest judges of wine in Australia. At Jimmy's, also, you eat well. Just like Mr. Sommers at the Walnut Tree, he didn't give you a menu. You select your own steak, your own mushrooms, go outside and cook them over the hot plate. And you are darned lucky here, too, if you get back to the office by three . . . or four. The spirit of the place is now ably carried on by his son Alan.

Fanny's by Gaslight is in Lonsdale Street. This bistro satisfies the world-wide nostalgia for all that is Edwardian. Fanny's belongs to an era before the megaton bomb was thought of, and with its gaslight it is highly thought of by the Gas and Fuel Corporation. The menu is effulgently Edwardian. There are cast iron panels and columns taken from an old Carlton home. You eat virtually in the dark, but the food is good.

But how can you mention them all? There's the Caprice, the Domino, Le Jardin, Rick's, the Nashery and the Beefeater, all good for a relatively inexpensive meal. Then, farther out in Toorak and South Yarra, there's a whole galaxy of eating houses. This is what we call the S.E. 1, S.E. 2 area, like a refined Kings Cross, or Kings Cross in a mink coat. Antonio's and Maxim's are in world class.

Here too there is the Two Faces, a Swiss restaurant with a great poster of the Matterhorn, and, on some nights, you can even get Swiss yodelling. By contrast, in Toorak, there is the Stromboli restaurant, the Atelier for Spanish-South American food, and the White Elephant for genuine Indian meals. Louis's in Toorak is quiet, refined, with silver candlesticks, a good place for Toorak husbands on those nights when wives need to be placated.

Around the bayside at Beaumaris there is the Didjeridoo. This is, perhaps, the only first class restaurant that tries to be aggressively Australian. There are aboriginal bark paintings, murals, spears, and, of course, a didjeridoo. The food covers the whole range, but as for anything Australian, you are more likely to get shish kebab than witchetty grub.

For all those restaurants that have been left out one can only apologise. It is difficult for even the hungriest of correspondents to get around to them all, to say nothing of the expense. Furthermore, the whole range of hotels and night clubs haven't been mentioned, but one day they may make another story. But the situation is such in Melbourne that now you can barely walk 200 yards in the city without finding some place where you will get good food. Why, you might even wander past the Cockerels. That is in Swanston Street. There they do something which I think is never quite fair. They roast their chickens on a turning spit in the window and they seem to organise things so that the smell wafts across the street. To make matters worse they amplify a tape recording of cocks crowing. So, in effect, you can listen to the poor creatures as you actually eat them.
