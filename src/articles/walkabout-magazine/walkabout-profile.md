DR. JEAN BATTERSBY is the recently appointed Executive Director for the Australian Council for the Arts.

It is a formidable title which makes her virtually the supreme cultural egghead, the Australian Andre Malraux. The Council has an initial budget of $1½ million and its main job is to subsidise the performing arts, drama, ballet, opera, film production and orchestras.

It needs to build standards of professional competence, twist money out of willing sponsors, promote cultural exchanges with other countries and as for Jean herself, she needs to know all the important people in the performing arts on a continent of three million square miles.

Jean Battersby should be able to cope. As an arguer she has a gift for expressing her thoughts quickly, with a splendid telling clarity, an ability born perhaps through years of appearing on TV discussion panels. She looks young, attractive and knows how to dress. She has other qualities extremely necessary in the job. She has a magnificent self assurance, great confidence in her intellectual capacities.

Jean Battersby completed her secondary education at Geelong High School and her Bachelor of Arts and Master of Arts at Janet Clarke Hall, Melbourne University. In 1956 she received an invitation from the French Government to spend a year studying in Paris. Her doctorate thesis at Melbourne University was on the poet Baudelaire and this was her chance to take her studies further.

"The French are marvellous," she said. "They had created this International University Centre for the purpose of bringing people together and exchanging ideas. For example, at the centre, on scholarships, there was a South African economist, an Argentinian civil engineer and a top American nuclear physicist."

She returned to Australia to a variety of jobs, public relations for a wine firm, compere for a TV station, a performer on the ABC show, "The Critics", literary critic, film critic and a variety of jobs in television and radio. "I didn't find this completely satisfying," she said. "In that sort of work you don't see anything concrete for what you have done. It has all disappeared into the end of the day. I like to be completely involved and absorbed in what I am doing."

One of her examples of this involvement was three years as Projects Officer for the third Duke of Edinburgh conference. There is an instant criticism of this affair; one can mention the hundreds of thousands of dollars that were spent on the grand talk fest and then ask for precise evidence as to what it all has achieved.

Do this and you will have a formidable opponent in Dr Battersby. "It wasn't a case of achieving something in bricks and concrete," she said. "This was never intended. The object was to bring together a number of vital people from different class levels, from different jobs, countries and environment. By getting together and talking, people were able to learn that their points of view were not necessarily the right ones. So people who would go back eventually to very high positions in their own countries had corners rubbed off. I have no doubts that the conference was worthwhile."

She sees her new job as an advocate to make the public more aware, more appreciative of the arts. She has spoken in the past of the old suspicion of the intellectual and even the slight contempt for the artist. "I can remember the time when painters here couldn't make a living. Boyds and Nolans were selling for about £5, and that's not long ago. But over the past 10 years there has been a tremendous change, a new awareness."

The main job of the council at the moment, she said, was to make surveys, to discover what had to be done. For example, they wanted to discover what happened to artistic expression in schools. "You take little children," she said. "You see them dancing, painting with gay abandon, singing their heads off and generally carrying on in what is very primitive artistic expression. But by the time they are 14 something goes radically wrong. If they go to the theatre at all they do nothing but drop Jaffas on the floor and laugh at the wrong jokes. The boys are too inhibited to dance. I don't know what it is. It could be something wrong with the education system."

Jean Battersby says her staff is very small but they are setting up committees for all the performing arts, and they are calling in the most expert opinion in the country.

Before leaving Jean Battersby, it was important to discuss one aspect. She had always been strong in her opinions on treatment of the artist, the role of intellectual; she has been a forthright member of the Freedom to Read Association and she has always said if you have certain beliefs then you should be prepared to do something about them. For some odd reason one also suspected that she would be an ardent feminist.

"No," she said. "There is no legal barrier now for women in occupying most jobs. I can only remember once being knocked back for a job because I was a woman, and I'm not even sure that this was the reason. It could have been simply because they didn't like me. No, I believe it is better to forget this and just get on with what you are doing. And that is what I am trying to do."
