One expects a top man in advertising to have charm and style. Dick Cudlipp, who has died from cancer after a short illness at the age of 78, had these qualities in abundance. He rose swiftly to become, in 1965, the managing director of George Patterson Bates in Victoria.

Bates had just become part-owner and everything had gone a little flat. A number of bright new agencies were claiming they had all the flair, all the originality. But it was Cudlipp who had the flair and secured the big accounts. One was Carlton and United Breweries, which incredibly Patterson's still has, 32 years later.

The first big battle was against the Courage Brewery. A market survey recommended that the way to fight Courage was to establish one big brand and meet the opposition head-on.

Cudlipp said: "No, we'll do the opposite. The future is in packaged beer. We will establish a team of brands and crowd them off the shelf."

So CUB did exactly that and within a few years Courage was on its knees.

Advertising was always a tricky trade, but Cudlipp had a reputation for integrity. He believed the only way to keep a client was to inspire confidence, and you couldn't do that without complete honesty.

During his reign, the firm coined famous slogans: Holden as "Australia's own car"; "A glass-and-half of fresh cream milk in every half-pound of Cadbury's Dairy Milk"; "A hard-earned thirst needs a big cold beer and the best cold beer is Vic - Victoria Bitter".

Cudlipp's parents migrated from England and settled in Perth. Joe Cudlipp started Cudlipp Motors, dealing in Oldsmobiles. But these were Depression times and they moved from Perth to Melbourne to Sydney.

Young Cudlipp went to school at Grimwade in Melbourne and later won a scholarship to Sydney Grammar. Meanwhile, his father became advertising manager of General Motors Holden.

Cudlipp finished school just in time to go into the Australian Imperial Force and serve in World War II with the 4th Battery 2/2 Heavy A.A. Regiment. He fought in the Middle East and New Guinea, finishing the war as a sergeant.

Joe had been friends with George Patterson, the founder of the firm, and Cudlipp followed his father into advertising and joined the firm. His brother, Bill, also went into advertising, but he joined a rival firm, USP Needham. When they had lunch together, there was a rule: no business.

Cudlipp had many interests. He was on the board of the Adult Deaf and Dumb Society for 26 years and was president from 1970-79. He also had a passion for the land.

In 1967, he went into partnership in 1,400 hectares near Maryborough, in Queensland. He pioneered the development of cattle in an area that before had been coastal bush. His partner, Royce Sommerfeld, wanted brahmans; Cudlipp wanted herefords. They settled for brafords, the brahman-hereford cross.

In 1973, Cudlipp bought 200 hectares at Kyneton in Victoria, and here he did raise herefords.

He always believed advertising was a stressful occupation and land was the perfect antidote. He also loved golf and was a member at Kingston Heath in Melbourne. There is a theory that had he been a better golfer his passion for the land might have been slightly less.

In 1950, he married Maureen Carswell Matchett, a Bendigo girl. They had three sons, twins Richard and Robert, then Peter, who followed his father into advertising.

The Matchett family owned a liquor retail business in Bendigo. When Bob Matchett died, Cudlipp bought the business, had it redesigned, brought in fine food and became interested in promoting the wines of central Victoria.

When he retired from George Patterson Bates in 1977, he was the vice-chairman.

He is survived by his wife, three sons and nine grandchildren.
