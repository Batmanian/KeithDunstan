CRICKETERS threatening strike! Would someone pass the smelling salts? Could anything be more shocking? Let everyone else go on strike - footballers, jockeys, baseball players (they did that for a whole year in the United States). But cricketers? Oh dear, no.

Cricket is not really a sport; it is a drama, a rite, or a dance. A Test match is akin to a symphony by Beethoven or Brahms. It has its movements. The first movement tends to be allegro, sheer power as the fast bowlers pound down the wicket. Then we have, the slow movement, the batsmen are settling in; andante, largo, lentamente, even gravemente. The third movement, the slow bowlers are being hit around, it could be allegro non troppo.

For the final movement, there is a rhythm, the mood intensifies and, if the play is good, it works relentlessly towards a climax. Yes, on the fifth day the mood should be vivace presto.

Cricket can be violent. When Dennis Lillee was bowling and Bay 13 used to bay "LILLEE! LILLEE! LILLEE!" they were baying for blood. But the truth is, I have always found cricket particularly acceptable when it is boring. In this era of violence, beautifully organised boredom is difficult to find. One of the great pleasures in life is to go the MCG. As John Arlott used to say: "A perfectly poised contest between bat and ball is taking place." That means absolutely nothing is happening, so one can quietly doze in the sun and awake with a start when a wicket falls.

Cricket commentaries are the same. I can listen to them by the hour, even up to four hours at a time. This can send some members of our family crazy, but they don't understand. The steady droning on 3LO, without anyone really saying anything, is perfection in boredom. It is really extraordinary therapy.

The cricketers have learned from the trade union movement. Did you notice they have presented a complete log of claims with more than 60 items? I worked nigh on 40 years for a newspaper organisation and never once in that time did an editor find it in his heart to produce a leading article that condoned a strike. Yet there were occasions when the tram drivers, the railwaymen, the teachers and the nurses seemed to have a cause.

But wait: the Oxford Dictionary describes sport as an amusement, diversion, fun. How could you go on strike over a fun thing? The Oxford has lost the plot. Sport is business and a sportsman doesn't play for enjoyment any more. One of the last of the great amateurs was Dr Donald Cordner, captain of Melbourne and a Brownlow Medal winner. I said to him once: "If they didn't pay you, I bet you received a free feed at the end of the game." "Never," he said, "we bought our own." I suspect he even paid for his own oranges at three-quarter time.

There are no more Don Cordners. Now a footballer can earn $300,000 in season. We have cricketers who earn more than $400,000 and Shane Warne, if he counts his sponsorships, moves close to the million. Tennis players can make better than half a million when rarely getting past the second round in a tournament.

We have made a habit of treating our sports people as the most important in the community. There is talk of a national trade union strike in Victoria this week over Workcare. It is unlikely that the Premier will take much notice. Imagine what would happen if we had a national sports strike. Imagine a weekend with no cricket, no racing, no basketball, no tennis, no golf. That would bring down the Government.

But could I leave you with one last thought? An American doctor was here earlier this year. When asked on radio what he thought of the high fees paid to sporting people, he replied: "When I hear of a teacher, a social worker or a nurse being paid as much as a golfer, a tennis player or a footballer, then I might be interested."

But that's our trouble: we are much more interested in sports people than teachers, social workers or nurses.
