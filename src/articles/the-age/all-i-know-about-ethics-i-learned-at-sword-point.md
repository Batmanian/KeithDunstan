ONE can only weep over the dreadful events that have caused the demise of the News of the World. The standards of English journalism have sunk to their lowest ebb.

But wait, there is a line in the Bible which says: "Why beholdest thou the mote that is in thy brother's eye, but considerest not the beam that is in thine own eye?"

Stand by for a few beams and motes. Only those who have been there can fully understand. Imagine the terror. You have a rampaging editor. It's a dull paper. The deadline is only 45 minutes away and you are staring at a blank sheet in your typewriter or empty computer screen.

Back in the 1950s, The Sun News-Pictorial with its circulation of 650,000 was one of the wonders of the world. The 50-50 letters column was the most popular column in the paper by a country mile. How to keep the standard? Often the letters were lacking in sufficient anger or spice.

We had a chief of staff who had been an army colonel. He would come around at 10pm and say: "I want a letter from YOU and YOU and YOU. NOW!"

I was moving rapidly into the role of top letter writer. I used to thunder about the potholes in Exhibition Street, about the broken bottles left by the milk man and the droppings left by their horses and I became well known as Fed Up, East Malvern.

My greatest triumph came when I walked down Bourke Street and noted that Myer had some unclad dummy models in their window. How appalling was this? So I wrote, "imagine what impressions, what lust would be aroused in young men when they saw the figures of naked women in our most important store". To make matters worse, these naked models were black. I signed the letter, Shocked, East Malvern.

It was marvellous, letters for and against poured in for over a week and 50-50 had never been happier.

From this I learned it was a good idea to keep the reading public irritated, hence such organisations as Anti Football, National Distrust and Pro Mini Skirt. However being a royal tour reporter was a problem. Real stories were difficult to find. When Prince Charles came out to be a student at Timbertop, Geelong Grammar, he flew Qantas. He had so many minders no one could get near him. My editor had the idea of buying a first class seat behind HRH, so that I could spy on his every move. All I learned was that for dinner he had filet de boeuf Crecy, supreme de poulet princess and for breakfast thick charcoal broiled Virginia ham steak and scrambled egg washed down by grapefruit juice.

Perhaps we did better with Prince Philip. The story began at the Chelsea Flower Show in 1959. The Prince was being dogged by photographers and his short temper was evident. Suddenly off went the sprinklers and the cameramen were drenched. The Prince denied he pushed the button that started the sprinklers, but the photographers didn't forget.

Some time later we were at the Commonwealth Games in Perth and the photographers wanted revenge. They worked out the path the Prince would take and planted a sprinkler. The Duke shouted: "Don't you dare turn that bloody thing on." At last a royal tour came alive and it made a splendid story.

I have not been guilty of phone tapping, but almost. Police reporting was deadly competitive. The police had their own radio station, D24, which sent messages to all patrol cars. The top cops knew we listened in to every word, so they invented a code, 45 was armed robbery, 25 murder, 13 sexual assault and so on. To confuse us they changed the numbers every month.

I had a mate in the Wireless Patrol. I didn't bribe him with money, but I fed him a lot of beer at the John Curtin Hotel. He kept me up to date with the code.

It was just after 11pm when I heard the message: "Forty-five, 13 Tobruk Crescent, Preston."

I grabbed a taxi and said: "Drive as quickly as you can to Tobruk Crescent." When I knocked on the door there was a woman in her nightie. She screamed: "Thank God you're here. My husband is trying to kill me with a Japanese sword."

I tried to tell her I wasn't the police, just a reporter, but she wasn't listening. Her husband was rampaging in the next room. We rushed into the bedroom and hid in the wardrobe cupboard. If the lady was frightened, I was terrified. Five minutes later the police actually arrived and we were saved.

I learned a great deal about newspaper ethics that night.
