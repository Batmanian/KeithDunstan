IS THERE a fair in the country not being run by the extraordinary Wittingslows?

Not many. They go right round the nation; Moomba, Sydney Show, Brisbane Show, right up the coast to Cairns, back for the Melbourne Show. There was a time when they took a nice three-month holiday after the Sydney Show. Not any more. They are too busy with regional shows, festivals and entertainment in shopping centres.

Now, the Wittingslows. There's Tom, the founder. Gregarious, charming, a wonderful storyteller. He's 84 and living on the Gold Coast. But don't for a moment think he's not here. He's on the blower all the time.

There's Des, Tom's son. A much quieter character, utterly unflappable. He was a boxer in his youth, now he is managing director of Melbourne-based Wittingslow Enterprises. The quiet Des controls everything. There's Des junior, very like his father. He runs all the rides. Then there are the two other grandchildren, Lisa and Michael.

Lisa looks after the side shows, like the shooting galleries and laughing clowns. Michael is chairman of Moomba.

The truth is this. Without the Wittingslow family, Moomba, long ago, would have collapsed, gone, finished. Six years ago they took control of the entertainment area, then three years later they went a step further and now they actually run the festival.

This past week they have been moving their fun machines into the Alexandra Gardens. They are unbelievable. Michael says the cost of a major ride these times can run close to $3 million. Take the Rainbow, that's worth $2 million.

You may be acquainted with this machine. The guests sit in a long carriage, looking like the choir in St Paul's. At either end of the carriage are two prim-looking angels. The choir is then swung up to an extraordinary height while heavenly music blares.

Most of the rides are designed to create the maximum discomfort. For example, there's Chaos, which looks like a mentally disturbed octopus and sets out to shake your stomach as much as conceivably possible.

Perhaps most remarkable, Des junior has installed a full scale Scenic Railway in the gardens. There is one just like it in Luna Park. Michael says it takes 12 40-foot trailers to shift it. Their record time for putting it up is four days.

Old Tom likes to tell the story of how it all began. In 1932, in the days of the deepest Depression, he had failed with a fruit stall in the Victoria Market, so he won a job in an arcade called Penny Land in Bourke Street, supervising the penny slot machines.

He did this for three months. Then he went out on his own. He guessed weights. If he couldn't guess within five pounds, he gave away a box of chocolates. "It was the women who were tricky," he says. "Men, you could feel their ribs, but the women would wear these whalebone stays. You would think they were slim, but when they sat on the scales you'd see their bottoms spread out. You knew you were gone."

"But I was right two times out of three. I charged sixpence to guess the weight and I bought the chocolates for sixpence, so it was good business."

Tom bought a T-model Ford, then a 1924 Dodge and toured all the country shows with various shooting games, giving away china dogs. In the shows, there was always the pin-headed Chinaman, the tallest man and the fattest lady. There was a naughty show, with the spruiker announcing that Rosie would show her pussy. The tent would be packed, according to Tom. The grand climax came when she lifted the side of her knickers and showed a pussy tattooed on her behind.

Tom went into the Army in 1940, was captured by the Japanese and worked on the infamous Burma railroad. After the war he used his deferred pay to buy an old-time merry-go-round, which he insists to this day is the most dangerous of rides; kids tend to fall off those horses.

For the 1957 Moomba, he had his first thrill ride, the Cha-Cha. It cost him 12,000. It had seats which swung up and down, then with a sickening lurch appeared to hurtle towards the fence. Tom loved that Cha-Cha and it was the basis for their future fortunes.

It is very different now. Wittingslows have 25 rides which run into the millions each and 20 kiddy rides. Michael believes it will be an outstanding Moomba with countless attractions. He says they are planning to take up an option to do it for another three years.

"The city has something special going for it and we plan to be part of it," he says.

He believes 2000 will be particularly wonderful. Federation Square will be in action then. They will be able to shift all the entertainment to the other side of the river. No longer will the Yarra provide a break. Batman Avenue will be closed. It will be the theatres, Flinders Street, then fun and entertainment right down to the water's edge.

He is looking forward to it.

Moomba runs from 5 to 15 March. It opens tonight with fireworks on the Yarra from 9.30pm. Tomorrow night, the Australian Ballet presents Swan Lake in a free, open-air concert at the Sidney Myer Music Bowl, starting at 7.30pm.
