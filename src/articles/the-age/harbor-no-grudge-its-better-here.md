JEALOUS of Sydney Harbor? Of course I am. On Boxing Day there is a rush of the green-eyed monster. We get these television images of all the Sydney-Hobart yachts scrambling to get through the heads.

For nigh on 200 years we have had this harbor thing pushed at us, but here in Melbourne we have been blushingly modest, when we should have been strutting and boasting about the wonders of Port Phillip Bay.

Back in the dreamtime, when we were busy building great dams all over the place, the engineers would boast of their works. They would look for comparisons and they would pick on Sydney Harbor. For example, the Hume Dam might be six times the size of Sydney Harbor, or Eildon four times the size of Sydney Harbor.

It became so common we invented a new scientific term of measurement, the "Sydharb." Port Phillip Bay is 1942 square kilometres and 264km around the skyline. By comparison, Sydney Harbor is a puny thing. Our bay is at least 20 Sydharbs.

What a place P.P. is for yachting. On a nice day with a gentle south-westerly breeze, the forest of white sails goes on forever from St Kilda, to Brighton, to Sandy, to Rosebud, to Balnarring, to Portsea. If we had to put our P.P. armada against the Sydharb armada, we'd outgun them five to one.

But I want to talk about the beauty of Port Phillip. Back in Edwardian times there was a great painter named Clarice Beckett. She could capture those lovely early-morning mists that you get across the water around Beaumaris, Seaford and beyond. Every time I wander along there I think of Clarice.

I have a number of favorite spots. Favorite is Sea Winds, a gem of a park just before you reach the top of Arthurs Seat in Purves Road. Here you can see almost the entire bay, including the You Yangs way across the other side.

Spot number two is Point Nepean, a long arthritic finger. Mercifully, nobody mucked it up, because the army and the quarantine station occupied it for so long. Pick what people would describe as a frightful day, go to the very tip and look at the rip when the winds are thrashing, the sea is pounding on the rocks and that narrow space we call the Heads is boiling. Imagine the 156 sailing ships that never made it through. That is something to see.

The ferry trip is a joy, too. Don't go on the huge thing that looks like the Love Boat; take the little one that operates in the summer, the old Sorrento ferry. First it takes you along the coast to Portsea and you get a perfect view of where the rich live. Then it goes right up the arthritic finger. It's chatty, it's friendly and they always make a point of trying to find the dolphins. And so it continues past Mud Island across to wonderful Queenscliff.

It is a great place for fishing. My mate Doug Crittenden, who fishes out of Mount Martha, claims he has never come back without a feed of flathead. Of course, you need the right conditions. Never venture out there with white caps and a little experience will tell you the good spots. Just drift, not too fast and not too slow, and the fish will start biting. But I tell you, that feed of flatties just out of the water, cooked in a beer batter, is one of the world's greatest culinary delights.

What else is good about the bay? Not many people realise there is a bike trail that goes 32km from Williamstown to Carrum. It starts with the little punt that links Spotswood to Westgate Park. You don't have to go on a bike - use inline skates if you like. This is pure glory. On the way, stop at St Kilda Pier.
