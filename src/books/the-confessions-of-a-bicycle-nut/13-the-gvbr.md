By 1984 the bicycle was fashionable and accepted. We did not see the Governor and his Lady out on a tandem, nor did we see the Prime Minister on his way to Parliament House aboard a safety, but at last bicycles were accepted by the city authorities. The Bicycle Institute now had its own quarters, complete with permanent secretariat and Government backing.

One way to catch the imagination and spread the word was to stage a big bicycle ride. The idea for the mass bicycle ride, with a great many people taking part, really began in Iowa. In 1972 Don Kaul was a columnist and John Karras was a feature writer on the *Des Moines Register*. Both were keen cyclists. Karras suggested to Kaul that he ought to ride his bike across Iowa. Kaul said he would do it as long as Karras came with him. Kaul and Karras fixed the date for August 26 and invited 'a few friends', that is the public, to go along with them. The *Register* public relations department took it up and they called it 'The Great Six Day Bicycle Ride'. Three hundred people took part and 114 actually made it right across Iowa.

The ride became the biggest annual biking affair in the United States, if not the world. It became a tradition for riders at the beginning to dip their front wheels in the Missouri River and the grand finale would be the wheel dipping in the waters of the Mississippi. In the second year they had 2700 riders and it just grew and grew until eventually they had a great mass like 16,000. The name became RAGBRAI, an acronym for Register's Annual Grand Bike Ride Across Iowa. The route varied but most rides were around 460 miles (736 km), so riders have always averaged more than 120 km a day.

On the first ride the columnists focused their attention on Clarence Pickard, who was 83. Temperatures reached over 100 degrees, but old Clarence did not give up and made it through to the finish. The same Clarence was the oldest rider in the 1976 Bikecentennial ride across the United States. In the winter of 1982, Clarence was walking across the street in his home town in Indianola. A car hit him and he was killed. So RAGBRAI XI was named the 'Clarence Pickard Memorial Ride' in his memory. The commemorative patch worn by all riders was similar in shape to Clarence's helmet. (1)

There were a number of people who had a vision for a big ride in Australia. There was Anna Lanigan, later to become Anna Erben. She was running a small touring club called Crankset. Anna thought there should be a ride, a grand tour around Victoria, based on the American Bikecentennial. She suggested it to Ron Shepherd, who thought it was a wonderful idea. The timing was perfect. This was just before Victoria's 150 birthday, called, somewhat awkwardly, the Sesqui Centenary Celebrations. The Sesqui committee was looking for ideas and this was a good one.

Ron Shepherd realised an application for funds would have to come through the Bicycle Institute of Victoria. Committee members agreed it should be something like RAGBRAI, it should be a ride across the State, it should end in Melbourne and it should have the title the Great Victorian Bike Ride. Ron Shepherd devised the plan, and after countless phone calls and many pleas, he received a grant of $10,000. The only person who had ever organised a big ride was Graham Rebbeck, a radio announcer with the station 3MP. In 1982, 3MP staged a bicycle ride from the Melbourne Hilton Hotel to Ringwood. Graham told Ron, if they could get 2000 for a ride like that, they could get 2000 for a ride across Victoria.

As he put it, Ron cornered Graham Rebbeck and said to him: "Graham, I've got a proposal for you. I'd like you to work for Bicycle Victoria for three months for $10,000." Rebbeck replied: "Are you fair dinkum?"

"Yes, I am," Ron replied. "I've received a grant of $10,000. It's nothing like enough to run big bike ride, but we need to get a major sponsor. We'll give you the $10,000 as a salary for three months, and if at the end of the time you've got a sponsor, you can run the ride. If at the end of the time you haven't got a sponsor, we wrap up."

Graham Rebbeck said: "It'll be bloody difficult, so that's why I'll do it."

Graham kept reporting back, no success. At the end of the three months Ron Shepherd sighed: "Oh well, that's the end of it. They were about to have a final meeting of the committee to wrap up the proposal, when Graham arrived. He gave a slight smile and said, 'I've got Caltex. They have promised us $25,000.' (2)

Ron Shepherd said from then on Rebbeck "organised like mad". He was brilliant. Nobody had ever planned a ride like this before. How do you work out the needs of 2000 people on the move? He went to the Army, he went to all the people who had run big events in the State. He found out how many mobile lavatories, how many showers, how much food.

So that was the start of the Great Victorian Bike Ride. The GVBR became an institution, an idea that was copied in New South Wales, South Australia and all over. It grew year by year, reaching a peak of over 6000 cyclists. It was described as the greatest mass migration across Victoria since the Gold Rush, and the organisers even believed that it was greatest all-catered-for, all-luggage-carried bike ride in the world.

The difference from Bikecentennial was this: on the American ride the bikers went off progressively on their own, in groups of a dozen, day by day for three months. Again it was different from RAGBRAI, which did not actually mother the cyclists. The GVBR did everything, provided the meals, carried the luggage, found the camping sites, the sag wagons, the mobile repair teams. The GVBR was more like

Mr and Mrs Paul Whitington of East Kew took along their children, Sarah five, and Tom, three. Paul had a tandem organised so that Sarah could pedal. It had an extra chain wheel and chain, raised to the height of her small feet. Paul said it didn't add a great deal of additional power, but Sarah was splendid going down hill. Tom rode on his mother's bike, but the weight became so great, at one stage Paul had the two children on the tandem.

There was a sag wagon to pick up the wounded and the fallen. This became a great institution and it was always there following behind the last rider. There were three or four semi trailers, which carried the several thousand kitbags, sleeping bags and tents. When we arrived at the camp, the bags were spread out in a vast sea across the ground, 99 per cent looking exactly like the other. Finding one's own bag was like trying to find a particular friend among the crowd at the Melbourne Cricket Ground at the Grand Final.

Things became more refined and better organised at later Great Victorian Bike Rides, bags were tagged different colours, trailers were labelled and bag search cut down from from 50 minutes to 10. At the first ride, the queues could have been mistaken for rationing in war time London. There were the queues for the food, queues for the showers and some quite desperate queues for the dunnies. It became known as the Great Victorian Bike Queue.

Yet there was a feeling of adventure, fun and cameraderie that overcame everything. The bicycle was the key which brought everyone together, age did not make a difference. There was instant communication between the grey hairs and the pony tails, the knobbly knees and the very young.

There was some ingenious and original behavior. The organisers kept it a secret but they engaged a drama group, Fiona Syme, Gilly Farrelly and Mike Bishop. They called themselves the Mixed Company, and they specialised in street theatre, little cameo scenes, designed to startle. Outside Benalla we saw a sign, 'Goanna Xing'. Fifty metres further on there was Mike Bishop galloping across the road in a goanna suit. Close to Shepparton there was a sign Nun Xcrossing. Next we saw Mr Bishop, dressed as a nun, crossing himself. A sweet little cameo was one of the girls up a tree playing a violin. Another was Gilly Farrelly dressed in gear, circa 1910, pedalling an exercise bike. In one hand she was holding a parasol and with the other she had a crocodile on a string.

Unquestionably the best stunt took place outside Maldon when Mike Bishop and Fiona Syme did the wedding scene. There they were, the full bit, bride and bridegroom, out in a bare, empty paddock, sitting at a table, drinking a bottle of champagne.

One cyclist shouted: "Wouldn't you know? The bloody Housing Commission hasn't delivered your house."

The Victorian countryside rarely looked more beautiful. It seemed as if there were a million acres of pale gold wheat ripening in the sun. The farmers everywhere were getting in the hay. In the old days they piled it into little stooks, now they had machines to do the job and the hay, never before looked as pretty as this, like great slices carved from a Swiss roll.

Maybe the best part of that ride was the finish. The organisers gained special permission to use the Tullarmarine Freeway. This was the freeway that carried the two-metre-high signs advising it was banned to horses, pedestrians, cyclists and dogs. So this was a triumph. The numbers at the finish swelled from 2000 to 5000 and as the city skyline came into view we felt proud, like Caesar's Legions returning from Gaul.

There were some fascinating bicycles on the ride. Harry Lamb of Camperdown brought out a Hobby Horse, which dated back to the early 19th century. The Hobby Horse was the original bicycle which came into vogue in the 1830s and Harry's was classic. It had no pedals and no brakes. The rider pushed it with his feet on the ground. No tyres, the wheels could have been used for the Duke of Wellington's cannon, and the whole bike had an immense weight. Harry did not cover the entire course on his Hobby Horse, it would have been too heavy for that, but he did look the part. He looked like Mr Pickwick out of Charles Dickens in a top hat and a red jacket with tails.

There was a fleet of penny farthings. Donald Lemin of Ferntree Gully and Paul Farren of South Yarra went all the way and became regular riders of the high bikes on the GVBR. Another was Phil McDonald, a Lilydale brick and stone merchant. He was the ultimate high bike nut. Phil owned a 54-inch Singer Coventry, which be bought in 1981. In April 1984 he rode it to Adelaide. Then in February 1985 he attempted to break a record that George P. Mills set back 1886, 506 kilometres on an ordinary in 24 hours. Phil made a valiant attempt, the temperature was 40 degrees and he covered only 320 kilometres. He tried again on 18 October 1986. Now the weather was miserable, cold and wet. He had trained long and hard, pedalling up and down the hills around Lilydale. This time he did 813 laps of the Knox Shopping Centre and covered 513 kilometres (318.4 miles) in the 24 hours. He achieved a world record and won himself a place in the Guinness Book of Records. (5) Then in 1988 he rode 15,500 kilometres right around Australia at an average of 120 km a day. He started on April 11 1988 and finished on August 14.

He said it was an uneventful ride. There were some dangers. The biggest danger was from characters in outback pubs who wanted to fill him with beer. There was the weather, the headwinds, it was just wonderful how you could get the full benefit of a headwind when you were on a bicycle with a 54-inch wheel. Then there was the traffic in the big cities. Stopping at the traffic lights, he said, was a pain in the neck. On a high bike it is a serious business, descending to the earth and getting up again. Phil McDonald found it wasn't always necessary. He could put a hand out and lean against any obliging truck, or even put your put a foot on the roof of a small sports car.

The first Great Victorian Bicycle Ride was such a success it became an annual event, increasing in popularity every year. Every year there was a different route, so the real enthusiast became an expert on every part of the State and, particularly, the wonders and beauties of a richly varied country. The bike ride discovered the Gippsland lakes, the splendid, sharp uprises and downs, of the Grampians, the gold fields of central Victoria, the great plains of the Mallee, and oh yes, the twists and turns, coupled with the amazing sights of the Great Ocean Road.

The Americans had a Bikecentennial across the nation to celebrate their 200th birthday, we could not do anything as grand as that, but Graham Rebbeck did organise a Bicentennial ride from Melbourne to Sydney for Australia's 200th birthday in 1988. There were 2200 riders and 550 of them came from the United States, bikers from Indiana, Florida, Illinois and Canada. The ride took two weeks with two rest days.

Don Lemin was at it again. He had just ridden to Sydney via the Blue Mountains with a team of vintage bicycle lovers. No sooner had he returned than he did it again. He owned three penny farthings. On this ride he had a new machine, made by a penny farthing manufacturer in Dayton, Ohio. It cost him $2200. Climbing the Australian Alps, his rear wheel collapsed with 11 broken spokes. It was not a good area for finding spares for an 1880s type machine, but Don, who was a maintenance worker for Nissan, created new spokes out of normal bicycle spokes, bending, binding, and making little hooks.

Marie and I had our own tandem troubles, a rear cable snapped, a derailleur exploded, a chain came apart and a rear tyre wanted no more of it, but this was nothing. We were in awe of some of the Americans. There was Donna Clark of Worthington, Ohio. She was little, she weighed only 48 kilograms. She towed Donna, her five-year-old daughter, the whole 1100 kilometres, over the Australian Alps, in a trailer. These trailers are known in America as 'buggers', presumably because they are a bugger to pull. Donna sat there in the trailer, very happy, with a pile of toys and spent her time waving to the other cyclists.

There was Glen Visser of Harvey, Iowa. He was an antique dealer and his wife Jeanie, a nurse. Glen had no arms. He scooted along manipulating his bike and his gears with a pair of calipers. The only serious problem was braking but he had an ingenious system of foot brakes attached to the bar just above the chain wheel.

Another was Ross Hendrix, an optometrist in Phoenix, Arizona. Ross had never ridden a bike until he was 67. Now he was 79, but five years earlier he had a colostomy. "So now I have my bottom sewn up," he said. "And you are still going on bike rides?" one gasped. "Yes," said Mr Hendrik, "it doesn't make any difference."

Red Davis, 74, of Columbus, Ohio, was almost as tough as Ross Hendrix. He had his first heart attack in 1985, and another in 1987. Before the Melbourne to Sydney ride his heart specialist told him: "Yes, why not? Go on the ride. If it's what you like doing, it's the best way to go."

He had a computer on his bike, which not only told him distance, speed, etc, but his pulse rate. We found him on the Razorback, a hill out of Sydney so steep you almost need oxygen. Mr Davis said: "I watch my pulse rate and my limit is 110. Climbing up here I've gone beyond that five times. Whenever it happens I get off and walk."

Red Davis played a dulcimer, which he made himself. When we reached Moss Vale he came third in the talent quest. He sang a ditty about the monotony of egg sandwiches which the caterers had provided every day for the past 15. As a prize they gave him an egg sandwich.

At Yerrinbool on the way to Picton we met Tibor Hajda, a railway ganger, born in Hungary. Tibor boiled a billy by the side of the road and called to the cyclists "Come over and have a mug of dinkum billy tea." He did it in the classic style, put in the eucalyptus leaves, threw in a handful of tea as soon as the water boiled, then swung the billy stiff-armed around his head. The Americans were fascinated. It was the greatest thing since Paul Hogan put another prawn on the barbie. (6)

There are other people to remember. Saxophone Jack was on all the early rides. Jack became an institution. He did almost everything a bike rider should not do. He carried no water bidons. Instead he strapped four cans of Foster's Lager to the bike tubing. This, he claimed, could sustain between pubs. He reckoned a 'coldie' would remain cold for 20 minutes. His real name was Jack Krane, 51 of Surrey Hills, formerly of Dimboola. He was a diesel fitter, but his forte was his dubious skill on the saxophone.

He bought this saxophone, an E-flat alto, in a pawn shop for $19. He played it in the camp every morning at 6 a.m. as a reveille to get the riders on their way. His playing was plaintive and mournful. He was very fond of *The Road to Gundagai*. (7)

Riding a bicycle one meets so many remarkable people, but perhaps the most amazing of all were Paul and Charlie Farren. We first met Charlie when she was in charge of a vintage 1930s three-seater Malvern Star. She was taking part in the 1986 Great Victorian Bike Ride. She had two other girls on the bike and sitting on the luggage rack was a large red doll called Freda-the-Free-Loader.

Charlie and her husband Paul did more for the bicycle movement than anyone I could think of. Paul was an orthodontist. He lived in South Yarra, had a practice in Cheltenham and held sessions twice a week at Frankston. He did not speed down to Frankston by car, he went on his bicycle. He covered and still does, around 500 kilometres a week. It had been rumored that the Farrens actually owned a petrol-powered vehicle, and they did, a van. They called it a 'bicycle accessory', useful for carrying spare bikes like tandems.

Charle, slim, small, blonde and dynamic, was the extrovert; Paul was the quiet one, very thoughtful. Some time around 1980 they started collecting bicycles, a couple here, a beautiful old thing there and it became like a drug. They couldn't stop. They had them all over the house, in the garage, in the hall, in the laundry, until finally they needed another house just for all the wheels. It was never easy getting Paul to talk about these bikes. How many did he have? He would look wistfully out the window. "I don't know. I've lost count. But I think there must be over 100."

The collection was always a wonder to behold. The best perhaps was the Humber Tandem. This dated back to the 1880s. It was tricycle with two large wheels side by side and a small wheel behind. The man sat behind and the lady sat in front. It was not proper for a lady to have to look ahead at a male bottom.

The Farrens also owned a companion or sociable. This was a tandem in which the lady and the gentleman pedalled side by side. The wheels were as large as those on a penny farthing. They made them that size, according to Paul, because they provided a beautiful, comfortable ride. They would bounce easily over ruts, gutters and terrible roads. However, the companion was enormous. No door was wide enough to take it and probably even Hubert Opperman would not have had the power to push it uphill.

Another wonderful item was the Pedersen. Mikael Pedersen was a Dane who made a fortune by inventing a milk separator. Very sensibly he came to the conclusion that the worst thing about cycling was the torture inflicted on the behind. He believed the most comfortable bed for humans was the hammock. So circa 1900 he had this idea. He took a tiny hammock and built a bicycle around it. The Pedersen had the hammock for a seat and it was connected to a frame which looked like a tripod. So it became one of the most comfortable bikes ever invented. The Pedersen had a revival during the born-again bike boom and it is possible even now to buy them in Copenhagen.

The Farrens also owned a Quadrant, built in 1889, one of the first of the safeties, and the forerunner of the diamond-frame chain bike we ride today. The Quadrant had solid tyres, Mr Dunlop had yet to invent his pneumatics. But they were thinking of tortured sit-upons even then. The Quadrant had a saddle which was mounted on a massive elliptical spring.

There should be an extra word to define Charlie, but it was not there in the dictionary. Charlie was heavily involved with the Great Victorian Bike Ride right from the beginning in 1984 and she took over as manager in 1990. She ruled with great efficiency until 1995, with females in charge in most of the key positions. She had a female caterer, a female planner, and a female transport chief. When asked about this she gave her usual cheeky grin. "It's not true. We do have males helping us. You must have some decoration."

Perhaps the best year was 1991 when the GVBR took over the Great Ocean Road. This was the biggest ride ever, with 6000 bikes. As Charlie says: "It was absolutely stunning." But she says it was bit like the story of the duck...the bird looked very smooth on the surface but the feet were going like hell underneath. For example, there was the Port Campbell saga. There was only one possible place to camp at Port Campbell and this was in a farmer's open paddock. A week before the cyclists were due to arrive, the farmer had not cut his hay. Three days before they were due to arrive, he hadn't cut his hay. On the very eve of their arrival it was still full of hay. Charlie thought: "What will I do? Here I have 6000 people on bikes. They will arrive in Port Campbell and I won't know what to do with them?"

They were at Port Fairy on their way to the Great Ocean Road. She managed to find a local who had a small but worrying two-seater aeroplane. The father briefed his son before the flight. "Here's your lunch. Now be careful. It's not much of a strip at Port Campbell and look out for the power lines at the end."

If she wasn't frightened before, she was terrified now. But they made the flight. Charlie almost got down on her knees and pleaded with the farmer. The hay was cut and 6000 people had a happy time at Port Campbell.

Paul Farren also owned a Quod, a four-seater, in effect, a double tandem. It is not easy to find such a machine that is both stable and safe. Paul's dental technician, Malcolm Martin, built it for him. Several times he has lent it for weddings. Paul has dressed in chauffeur's uniform with bride in full regalia and bridegroom down the back. We rode it in the 1994 GVBR from Swan Hill to Melbourne. Paul was number one, Glen Lawless of the Multiple Sclerosis Society was number two, I was number three and Tom Dunstan, my grandson, aged 13, was number four.

Taking off was a delicate matter, like a Boeing 747, a quod was somewhat unstable at low speeds. Paul laid down the rules. One and two would have one foot on the ground, three and four would have both feet on the pedals, no waving to Aunty, no looking across at girls. "At the word GO! ... PUSH!"

It was like a great railway train pulling out of the station, very slow at first, but then we gained momentum and soon we were moving at a splendid speed, passing every bicycle on the road. Riding towards Melbourne from Swan Hill, the temperatures were in the 40s with a strong north wind. The Quod was a little slow on the hills, but on the flat it was amazing, and downhill it was awesome. We cruised at nigh on 45 kmh.

Of course, there were the usual cries. Tom had a bad time. At least 2400 people called out: "Hey, the little bloke on the back is not pedalling." Actually Tom thought he was doing all the work, and the three characters up front were taking it easy.

The camps were interesting, becoming more and more refined. Graham Rebbeck said originally the plan was to have the camp laid out perfectly, everyone in their right spot with proper aisles. Quickly they found this impossible. Everybody liked to huddle in, tents close to each other. Always it was the most amazing sight, 2000 or 3000 tents all different colours placed on football grounds, parks or show grounds. The Army of the Duke of Wellington on the eve of Waterloo could not have been more impressive than this.

One of the discomforts of growing older is that need, around 3 a.m., to go out for a nocturnal pee. It is something that calls for great resolve, the unzipping of the sleeping bag, the finding of the torch, the unzipping of the tent, and then the obstacle race through a thousand guy ropes to find a suitable open area.

On two occasions I encountered the same nightmare. It is all very well to leave one's tent, but to return is another matter. At 3 a.m. in the dim light every tent looks the same colour and the same shape. One night at Cohuna I must have wandered, stumbled and searched for over an hour. Many times I thought I had found my tent and attempted to climb in, only to hear shouts of surprise and even rage. Remarkable things often were taking place in those tents. I was almost ready to give up and wait for daylight, until at last I found my little green haven. At breakfast I related my adventures to Jeff Hook. He looked a little red-eyed. He had been through exactly the same experience. The only answer is to have a tent that looks like the Taj Mahal or pitch it in an extremely identifiable spot, like close to a gum tree.

There were some refinements introduced for the old hands. All the school riders were segregated into one area, so that they could make their own noise and Charlie Farren put in the Café de Canvas. This was a special area with tables, chairs, and glory be, a bar. There was also the Massage Room, with a team of masseurs who could rub away the pain.

Some riders go on for ever. The GVBR had its own Clarence Pickard. Alan Hartup was there in 1998 for the GVBR 15, the ride from Wahgunya on the Murray via Alexandra and Benalla to Melbourne. Alan Hartup was 83. We had seen him on at least five previous rides. Alan was small, bow-legged and they called him 'Curly', because he had little hair. He was slow, invariably he arrived after everybody else, but always he got there. In 1988 we suggested to him that the 100 kilometre ride from Myrtleford to Benalla in all the heat, must have been hard. "Not a bit," he said. He was always doing 100 km rides around Newstead, Maldon and Castlemaine to keep fit. Curly came from Newstead and had worked in the motor repair business.

He liked the ride, because everyone was so nice to him. Particularly the girls, always they waved. He had one big worry in 1988. He had heard the insurance companies would not insure any bike rider over the age of 84. They were concerned that people of this age could drop dead on the road. That was extremely unlikely, according to Curly. He was as fit as could be. But he was terrified that the GVBR would not have him next year.

This was also very unlikely. The GVBR could hardly get along without him.
