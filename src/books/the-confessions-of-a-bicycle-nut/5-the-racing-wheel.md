title: The Racing Wheel
date: 1999-11-11
summary: A history of professional cycling's boom years at the Austral Wheel Race on the Melbourne Cricket Ground, dominated by the brilliant, scandal-prone American 'Plugger' Bill Martin and later by Major Taylor, before motoring killed the sport's popularity after 1906.

All this passion for cycling naturally produced some extraordinary competition. For nearly 30 years Australia was the home of professional cycling. Here the crowds were the biggest, the prizes most inviting and some of the world's best cyclists were competing.

The classic event was the Austral Wheel Race. It took place at the Melbourne Cricket Ground and it became the Melbourne Cup of cycling. It even had the same social prestige. The Governor and his lady would be there, society would be there, and just like the Melbourne Cup, ladies would have to put on their finest; the social papers listed their names and precisely what they wore. You had to look the part for the Austral.

The first Austral Wheel Race was on 30 January 1886 at the M.C.G. The first prize was a grand piano worth 200 pounds and the race was a handicap over three miles. There were few racing bicycles available in 1886, so that anyone who rode a machine over 46 pounds in weight received a handicap of 30 yards in the mile extra. There were 144 entries for the meeting. In those days the low-to-the-ground safety bikes were unpopular, chiefly because of their manoeuvrability. They would zip in and out of their towering competitors who would crash to the ground like tall timber. The New South Wales Union even passed a resolution forbidding the safeties to compete against the high bikes in open races.

For the first four years, the Austral was an amateur event, but from 1890 onwards the trophies were in cash, and to the horror of many members of the Melbourne Cricket Club, the bookmakers moved in and the stately ground had all the atmosphere of the Caulfield Race Track. So with big cash prizes to be won, the world champions began to arrive. Arthur Zimmerman, 'Zimmie' of New Jersey, undefeated from one mile up to six miles in America, England and France, arrived in 1895. So tremendous was his reception that 65,000 people paid 3301 pounds to see him at a two-day meeting in Sydney that November.

Yet the most extraordinary of the world champions to race in Australia was 'Plugger' Bill Martin. Plugger was born in Dublin, went to Detroit when he was three, had red hair and an old-fashioned Irish temperament. He raced everywhere; in the United States, West Indies, South America, Honolulu, South Africa, West Africa, Spain, Portugal, Italy, Germany, England and New Zealand, but best of all he liked Australia. Here he thought the money was good.

'Plugger' was his best-known nickname, but he had others - 'The Iron Man', 'The King of the Handicap Kids', 'The Cast-Iron Boy from Detroit', 'The World Marvel'. He was the number one personality, the big drawcard at all the races. Plugger gave the crowd its money's worth because he was never out of trouble.

The events, which the *Adelaide Advertiser* called the "disgraceful proceedings", all began in Adelaide during the carnival of October 1896. Alec McDonnell, another American cyclist, was chasing Martin hard around the edge of the track when he swerved into him and crashed against the fence. Martin charged that McDonnell deliberately "bored" his bicycle into him. McDonnell charged that Martin assaulted him right there on the track. The inquiry sided with McDonnell and suspended Martin for two months. (1)

Plugger denied that he ever hit his colleague, but seeing that it was not unusual for him to hit people, his reputation was further enhanced. It was enhanced yet again the following year in Sydney. At a professional meeting a spectator shouted a foul word at him, so Plugger, wearing his brilliant orange racing costume, jumped off his bike, chased the man through the crowd and thrashed him. The New South Wales League of Wheelmen thought he had been "extremely provoked" and exonerated him. (2)

The following month he was back in Adelaide for the 1897 carnival, still very angry about the McDonnell affair. On the opening day, 9 October, he was leading well in the mile scratch race when he appeared to have a spasm. He waved to the Australian champion, Joe Megson, "Go on, Joe, I'm done". Megson, who had been disqualified the previous March through Plugger, said sourly, "Go on yourself", but nevertheless Joe did go on and he won by six lengths. Martin then rode slowly round the ground amidst a furious demonstration from the punters. Some shouted: "Fall down Bill". (3)

The following Wednesday the League of South Australian Wheelmen held an inquiry. Plugger opened up well. One of the referees was Mr W.F. Ward, and turning in his direction, Plugger said:

"I want to ask this party here something that occurred a year ago. I want to ask him when I was disqualified for two months, was he given 10 pounds by Alec McDonnell and what was it for?"

Mr Ward: It is an absolute lie.

Martin: Am I to be judged by him?

The Chairman: Do you object to Ward sitting at this meeting?

Martin: I do, very much.

The Chairman, however, ruled that Ward had to stay. Mr R.Cruikshank, one of the referees at Saturday's racing, was the first witness. He said no-one alive had ever insulted him the way Martin had insulted him on Saturday. Martin had come over and this conversation followed.

Martin: You are nothing but a scoundrel.

Cruikshank: Thank-you.

Martin: I intend to insult you. You insulted me on the track and I intend to make it a personal matter to insult you and all the rest of them.

Another witness said that he had called them all low scoundrels and that they always treated him like a dog in Adelaide.

The League fined Martin a total of four pounds, one pound each for a series of charges. The judges for the carnival felt insulted by the verdict. They thought it inadequate and refused to act for the next day of the meeting on Saturday. Meanwhile, Plugger Bill counter-attacked with a letter to the League:

Mr J. Gazard, secretary, L.S.A.W.

Sir, I respectfully ask the League to donate me 25 pounds to defray expenses caused by decisions. If my wish is not fulfilled you may scratch me for every event.

Yours faithfully,
W. MARTIN, American Cyclist. (4)

This was a magnificent piece of effrontery, a calculated threat, like Phar Lap scratching himself from the 1930 Melbourne Cup. The secretary replied that if Martin did not race, the L.S.A.W. would take legal action against him. Plugger's next move was to distribute handbills around the streets of Adelaide on the Friday evening:

ATTENTION TO PUBLIC OF SOUTH AUSTRALIA

*This is to certify that I will not be competing in the cycle races on the Exhibition Oval on Saturday, October 16, as advertised by others.*
W.MARTIN, American Cyclist.

All this had a fine effect in bringing a record crowd to the ground. 'Plugger' consulted a lawyer and engaged for four races. He came out after his heat in the first class mile and rode the distance alone on the asphalt. He mounted a family style roadster with a tool bag, pump and bell, which he rode up the straight ringing the bell merrily as he went. The crowd received him with mixed boos and cheers. In the next race, the League Handicap, he appeared without colours or numbers, paced hard for a lap, then retired. He scratched from the other events, claiming that he was injured. (5)

The extraordinary story continued in the Adelaide Police Court on the Monday morning. Plugger was on a charge for having assaulted Mr Frank P. Ridley, who had been a member of the South Australian League's Record and Racing committee during the McDonnell affair of 1896. Witness after witness told the full tale. On the previous Wednesday morning, Ridley and Martin had been on the track training. Martin came in first to the changing room, 50 or 60 other people followed him. Then, as Ridley entered, he said:

"Gentleman, I want to ask this man a question and I want you to hear what he says. You remember McDonnell being here last year?"

"Yes," Ridley replied.

"If he gave you 10 pounds, what did he give it to you for?"

Martin did not wait for an answer. He dragged Ridley off a form and proceeded to hit him. He cracked him 20 times with an open hand, very hard. Ridley did not put up any resistance. Plugger then challenged anyone in the room, asked them if they would like similar treatment, or whether they had anything to say. No one did. (6)

Ridley appeared in the court with two black eyes and he complained he could still hear ringing in his ears. Mr Paris Nesbit Q.C., explained that the blows were merely with an open hand. Counsel for Ridley, Mr Symon said: "Yes, with a cyclist's thumb. Let him exhibit his thumb. It's like a kick from a young horse, I believe."

Ridley denied vigorously that he had been bribed and the court was on his side. Mr James Gordon sentenced Plugger Bill to 14 days imprisonment with hard labour.

Had Martin committed murder, or even appeared before a Victorian Football Tribunal, he could not have received more space in the newspapers. Every small detail was covered. A *Register* reporter interviewed him after the sentence. He found Plugger in the court yard, standing there with the diamond stud missing from his shirt front. "You've lost your stud, Martin," said the reporter. "Oh, they've relieved me of those things," Plugger replied sententiously. "I'll never race again. I'm going to quit. I intended to do so before. Can't do anything straight on the track now; they seem to think I've made too much money. That's the size of it." (7)

Martin served 13 of his 14 days. He came out on a Saturday because of an Adelaide Jail rule that no prisoner is discharged on the Sabbath. Once again, reporters were waiting for his comments:

"Well, I enjoyed myself down there. It is the most complete rest I have had in years. I was treated the same as the others, but I can't recommend the tucker you have served out to you. You had to imagine that you had enough. Of course I wore a belt and kept pulling it up a notch as the day wore on." (8)

Plugger admitted he had made 1000 pounds since he had been in Australia, and he was worth more money than all the Australian cycling leagues put together. The *Register* commented:

The sentence passed (on Martin) was richly deserved. This man has been taught that bullying and bounce - even from conceited professionals - will not be tolerated by the conductors of South Australian sports. (9)

The South Australian League disqualified him for six months, and various other disqualifications put him out for two years. One journal said: "Exit William Martin, the best cyclist ever seen in Australia."

Plugger Bill swore he was finished with Australia for good, but he really returned in 1899. He suffered for a time from injuries, but he came good the following year. At the 1900 Austral on the M.C.G. he won eight of nine events on the first day. On the second day he won the 10-mile scratch race and on the last he took the mile handicap and the five-mile scratch race. His total winnings were 278 pounds.

Everyone was ecstatic. Plugger was again the favourite, just like old times, and the *Sportsman* printed his glory in verse:

When we went to see the cycling at the Melbourne Cricket Ground,

How we hollered and shouted as The Plugger whistled around,

With his 'trilbies' both a-movin' as quickly as you like,

And his 'youthful' hands a-grippin' both the handles of his bike.

With his auburn hair a-floatin' like a banner on the breeze

With his quarters up to heaven, and his head between his knees;

With his body all a-wobblin' and his features stern and set

Well, the Plugger made a picture I never will forget. (10)

The *Sportsman* said:

Martin must be given the bread, cake, pastry - ay, the whole baker's shop - for being the best all round man in Australia. Champions come and they go again. A new set appears. But Bill Martin is always in the lead. (11)

But Plugger was soon back at his old tricks. He was fined. Five pounds for suspicious riding at Charters Towers in Queensland and the North Queensland League disqualified him for three months. When the Queensland executive ratified the decision, it said that if the offences were as reported, then the suspension should have been for a year. (12)

Then came the famous Austral Wheel Race of 1901. The *Sportsman* wrote of it glowingly:

There was never such a burst of excitement on the Melbourne Cricket Ground, there was never such a brilliant crowd on the ground, there was never a more perfect day, there was never a better Austral wheel race, there was never so much money taken at the turnstiles, there was never so much money at stake in a cycling race anywhere in the world. (13)

Plugger arrived in town aboard a steamer called the *Austral* and he stayed at the Austral Hotel, so all the omens were beautifully set for the Austral Wheel Race. But he was getting on now in years and it hardly seemed possible for him to triumph again. How old was he really? Plugger would never say, but this year he gave a hint:

How old am I? Waaal, I was born in Dublin, went to New York as a boy, an' my father's a U.S. pensioner for fightin' in the Civil War. It's 20 years since I started ridin' in Nebraska. Then I was foot runnin'. I went to the Cape foot runnin' in 1884.

The shrewd ones estimated from all this that he was really 42.

The crowd on the final day was 32,000 and the takings 1307 pounds. Plugger rode a remarkable race. He won by 15 yards and he was only the third rider in the history of the race to win from scratch. The *Argus* reported there was a scene of great excitement as Martin crossed the line.

Tremendous cheers were raised, and there was no question about the popularity of the win. Martin toppled from his machine utterly exhausted and had to be supported by his trainers as he was led up to be introduced to the Lieutenant Governor, Sir John Madden, and the first Prime Minister of the new Commonwealth, Mr Edmund Barton.

The *Argus* said unquestionably the best man won, (14) but there was much trouble over this race. The pay-out in the ring was estimated at between 7000 and 8000 pounds and the amount of money wagered was a record for a cycling race anywhere. Rumour was that the famous gambler, John Wren, of the Collingwood Tote, had plunged on Martin.

The stewards questioned the riding of F.S. Beauchamp on the grounds that he had acted as pacemaker for Martin. The League suspended him immediately with the recommendation that he be disqualified for life.

The following Thursday, seven riders had to appear before an inquiry at the Port Phillip Club Hotel, next door to Young & Jackson's in Flinders Street. They came in before the officials one at a time and the tribunal asked each man, did he receive any money in action with the race; did he try to win and was he concerned in a conspiracy? Every man swore his innocence and the inquiry was inconclusive. Meanwhile, Plugger's purse had been withheld and always he was in the background at the Port Phillip. Whenever any of the officials appeared he asked: "When as I going to get my 400 pounds?" Eventually Plugger did get his money, according to the *Argus*, just as he was about to serve a writ. (15)

Later, the *Lone Hand*, a journal which provided much of the material for Frank Hardy's novel *Power Without Glory*, came out bluntly and said the whole race was fixed by John Wren. It gave the version of the settlement as told by Plugger Bill's trainer, 'General' Gordon.

The bribes were laid out in piles of notes and sovereigns upon Martin's bed at the hotel. One by one the cyclists were admitted to the bedroom, and Martin, who had a loaded revolver beside him, addressed each successive visitor by name in a loud voice, and stated the terms of the contract. The visitor replied "Quite right, Bill" or words to that effect. Then Bill paid over the money, and solemnly requested the recipient to sign for it. Everybody was enriched with a heavy burden of notes and gold, save poor General Gordon, who ultimately sued his employer for 60 pounds.

According to *Lone Hand*, Plugger Bill had 19 opponents in that race and all ran dead except Mick Lewis and one or two others. (16)

'Plugger' never revealed how much money he actually made that day, but obviously the amount was considerable, for he did say that now he had enough to retire. Yet the lure was too strong. He went to New Zealand, won some races there, and with his Austral friend, Frank Beauchamp, toured America once more. He returned to Australia again in 1902 and actually retired in 1903.

But the reaction to the 1901 Austral was widespread and although the classic is still run today, no one would ever suggest again that here was a rival to the Melbourne Cup. The *Argus* was filled with angry letters. This was one:

Sir.- I was in the grandstand on Saturday, unfortunately accompanied, as it turned out, by a lady. I have never enjoyed myself less. The deafening clamour of the barrackers, the degrading antics of the 'ticktackers', the absence of true sport, thoroughly disgusted me. Sport! More sport could be found in watching a mosquito and far more excitement. (17)

The *Argus* commented:

We have seen sports rise to extraordinary popularity and fade and die within a few seasons, and the cause has always been the same. Already there has been too much talk about crooked running in connection with these cycling contests that, providing there is no drastic interference to minimise the evil, the sport must certainly perish. (18)

The greatest years for visitors were 1902 to 1904, and all the top Americans came south looking for the Australian riches - Major Taylor, Floyd McFarland, N.C. Cooper, Ivor Lawson, Hardy Downing and Orlando Stevens.

The most famous of the group was Marshall Walter 'Major' Taylor. He was not big, only five feet seven inches, but beautifully built, and a remarkable sprinter. In 1899 at Philadelphia the Major rode a mile behind a steam car in one minute 19 seconds. He came from Worcester, Massachusetts and he was a minister of the local Methodist church. He was such a devoted churchman that when he was offered $30,000 to compete in a series of races on the Sabbath, he refused.

Why he was called 'Major' is a mystery. When asked in Sydney, he said: "It was Major to 30,000 people when I defeated their favourite Jacquelin in Paris and it is Major to the lift boy in my Sydney hotel."

In 1903, the spectacular Hugh D. McIntosh, pieman, super promoter, inaugurated the Sydney Thousand at the Sydney Cricket Ground. The first prize was 750 pounds, 350 pounds more than the Austral, and now the world's richest race. *The N.S.W. Cyclist* spoke of the affair with wonder, how the Sydney Cricket Ground looked "splendid", illuminated with hundreds of glittering electric lights. Floyd McFarland and Major Taylor were there, but the Major had a fall and N.C. Hopper won off 75 yards handicap. (19)

The following year a protest by O'Brien, who finished second, ended with the disqualification of several riders, including L. Corbett, the winner. Corbett took legal action to get his prize money, but O.Brien, the defendant, won the case. (21) This was virtually the end of the Sydney Thousand.

Major Taylor had two tours of Australia, 1902-3 and 1903-4, and he won a great many races, but his parting words were far from happy:

Haven't you people got eyes? I'll tell you that if racing, as I have seen it, is allowed to go a couple of years will see the end. Teaming or combining, call it what you like, is rampant here. I wonder how many of your riders go out to win. What do they do? Systematically to pull one man and share the prize money. What chance has an honest rider against such odds? Take the Sydney Thousand, it was the worst race I have ever competed in and, owing to the teaming, I never had a chance. When I mentioned this point to a racing official he remarked, 'Oh we could never tolerate the same man winning all the time.'

Curly Grivell in his cycling history wrote that nobody heeded the Major's warning. The public became fed up, turned down cycle-racing and rival sports took over. Racing tracks closed down in every State and by 1906 it was virtually over. (22)

There were valiant attempts to hold the spectators, particularly at the Austral. For the Austral of 1908 there was a balloon ascent on the Melbourne Cricket Ground. Messrs John Rinaldo and Christopher Sebphe went aloft and reached a height of 5000 feet. Rinaldo came down by parachute and landed in a tree in the Fitzroy Gardens. Mr Sebphe came down with the balloon and landed on the roof of a house in Queen's Parade, North Fitzroy.

For the Austral of 1909 the public was told that Miss Mollie Mostyn, the American lady astronaut, and Captain Penfold, the Australian balloonist, would go aloft by balloon, and descend to the ground by parachute. Indeed, Captain Penfold would perform his sensational act of parachuting down riding a bicycle.. Regrettably the balloon did not attain sufficient height and ended on top of a fence in Vere Street, Collingwood. The sensational act, thus, was of a different variety. Captain Penfold used his bicycle to ride back to the Melbourne Cricket Ground.

What else was there to do? In 1910, the Austral committee hired Mr G. Cugnet to take off from the Melbourne Cricket Ground in his Bleriot aeroplane; an extraordinarily hazardous idea. On the day the weather was so bad, Mr Cugnet was fearful of making his ascent until the wind dropped. That situation did not arrive until 7 p.m. when almost all the Austral crowd had gone home.

Mr Cugnet did get off the ground. He had to bank violently to miss the scoreboard, he dived into the tennis court and the Bleriot was hideously wrecked. That was the end of the Austral on the Melbourne Cricket Ground. (23)

Never again was cycling capable of being a greater drawcard than football or cricket. It had lost its chance. But corruption was not the only factor, the gloss had gone, the bicycle craze was over. After 1900 the citizens did not see the Governor and his Lady out on bicycles. The ladies of Toorak and Vaucluse did not hold bicycle parties. No longer was it the thing to join a club and go touring on Saturdays and Sundays. It was all over. Now the motor was the thing.

The peak was 1897. There were 1200 bicycle manufacturers in New York City and 83 bike shops within a mile of Lower Broadway. There were complaints that people were spending so much on bicycles that the sale of pianos was down 50 per cent and jewelry stores were empty. The shoe market was stagnant on the theory that people were riding bikes instead of walking. (24) Suddenly there was a glut of bicycles, they were not selling.

In October 1898 *Australian Cyclist* quoted a "pessimist" with the words: "I know half a dozen homes where the bicycle is now a thing of the past. Two years ago the wheel was quite fashionable, but I am glad to note the delirium has now subsided. The boom in cycles has burst, and the days are numbered." The Journal denied this with fervour. It even cited the fact that clergymen and registrars were claiming that marriage was on the decline. Why? The bicycle had given people too much independence. They preferred to pedal instead. (25)

Bike shops, and department stores, however, were noticing a change. Their establishments were filled with unsold bicycles. Already the new enemy was in evidence. Anthony S. New of Messrs New and Mayne, London discussed how motor cars would affect the cycling world: "We do not expect it to revolutionise the cycle trade, but it will undoubtedly have its effect. We have now orders for a hundred oil cycles. There is one that has been running over 600 miles in England. The motive power is petrol spirit, the ignition being effected by electricity. Of course, these machines are heavier than the ordinary cycles; but they get over the ground quickly, this machine easily doing 80 or 90 miles on a Saturday afternoon." (26)

In September 1897, Mr Harry Sutton of Sutton Brothers in Bourke Street, Melbourne, drove a motor tricycle to Ballarat. The engine was a single cylinder, air-cooled and it operated at 1400 revolutions a minute and developed one horse power. It could cruise at 20 miles an hour and cover 45 to 60 miles on one change of oil. Harry Sutton left the G.P.O. at 8 a.m., reached Bacchus Marsh at 11.30 a.m., then after lunch took the back road to Ballan. This was a mistake. The road was a "terrible strip of alternate mud, rock and crabhole, not negotiable even for a bicycle. Although the motor worked splendidly without a hitch, the wheels would not grip the slippery surface." They did not reach Ballan until 6.30 pm.

At 9.30 p.m. Sutton's machine reached the outskirts of Ballarat. The Mayor and almost everybody else had been waiting five hours for the arrival. But, according to *Australian Cyclist*, there was a "scene the like of which has never been seen in Ballarat...From one end of Sturt Street to the Town Hall was one dense mass of eager, anxious men and women, who, as the car advanced, pushed and fought each other for positions of vantage, and when the motor and cyclists (500 of them) glided by, stampeded after them in one mad rush, each endeavoring to elbow back his neighbor."

Harry Sutton took his machine to his Ballarat offices and escaped inside. Thousands of people stood outside, cheered and yelled for it to come out again. Finally the police asked Sutton to bring his motor out and let the people see it or they would, despite a strong guard, wreck the premises. So Harry had to start it up. His motor 'puffed merrily through the crowd' and everybody was happy again. (27)

One by one the cycling journals closed down. The *Austral Wheel* expired in April 1900. On 9 August 1900, the *N.S.W. Cyclist* announced that it was its last appearance as a weekly journal. Henceforth it would publish fortnightly. It said: "We have for some time contemplated this step. Most cyclists must know that the cycle trade is not what it was a few years ago, and that it cannot support a weekly journal now. This fact will become at once apparent on scanning the advertisements in this issue."

*The N.S.W. Cyclist* lasted only a short time after this and the *Australian Cyclist,* which had attempted to be a really national magazine, folded two years later. It was a world-wide phenomenon. In the United States 131 bicycle firms closed in 1897 alone. In 1899 the American Bicycle Company, owned by such magnates as A.G. Spaulding and John D. Rockefeller, won control of most of the remaining American bicycle manufacturers. It went out of business in 1903.

However, the bicycle had done its bit in preparing for the automobile. It inspired the first mass production. It was through the bicycle that spokes, the first gears, the first satisfactory brakes, the first lightweight steel tubing, the development of ball bearings and even the dynamo for electric lighting was invented. It played its part also in liberating women and providing sensible fashions.

But after the turn of the century the bicycle was no longer fashionable. The fun aspect was not there. It remained as a utility machine for postmen, policemen, for grocers to deliver their orders, and for the great mass of people, laborers, people who could not get to work any other way and for the delivery of newspapers.

Now the motor had arrived. Nobody sang any more about *Daisy* and *the bicycle built for two*.

The new song concerned *Lucile* and *the merry Oldsmobile*.
