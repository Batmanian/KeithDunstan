There are all kinds of nuts. There are the nuts who collect snuff boxes, nuts who adore snakes, and nuts who like to make houses out of bottles. There are fly fishing nuts. Their minds constantly wander to some mountain stream and you can see them at home or in the office making strange movements with the wrist, involuntarily casting for a brown. Skiing nuts are the same, they tend to lock their knees and make swift movements in the living room.

There are football nuts who ask to have their ashes sprinkled between the goal posts at the Melbourne Cricket Ground and there are racing nuts who can remember the name of every Melbourne Cup winner. If you ask the date they were married you might get the reply: "Ah yes, it was in Gala Supreme's year, 1973."

There are nuts who believe that the automobile never progressed once they were mass produced post-1930; there are nuts who are reduced to tears by the sight of a Purdy gun; then there are the steam nuts and the tram nuts. These I call terminal nuts. There is no known cure. I have known tram nuts who have gone into raptures over the terrible screech as a W class swings round a corner on its almost square wheels. They are deeply moved too by the strange asthmatic noises made by ancient Ws as they build up compression or seem to gather energy for the next foray. Tram nuts love tram noises so much they keep recordings so that they can play back for special pleasure their screeches, howls and tramic gasps.

Yes, indeed, bicycle fanatics are prime terminal nuts. Bike-itis is a deadly virus and like the tram disease there is no known cure. Bike-itis hits perfectly normal people; it is no respecter of age. I have seen it strike a Melburnian judge whom one would have thought was old enough to know better. I have seen it hit a professional pilot, who spent his days flying a Beechcraft Baron. But the truth was this: he preferred to be on the ground riding his bicycle. Finally his wife left him, he was away every week-end pedalling.

There was the sad case of a middle-aged stock broker. He owned a Ferrari, a Lotus and a Jaguar. He had half a million dollars worth of machinery in his capacious garage, but all was being neglected. He was too busy riding his bicycle. I have seen it hit a famous TV and radio presenter. The poor man worked for many years as an early morning announcer, which meant that he was bicycling to his microphone, often in frightful weather, daily before 5 a.m.

I was in my mid-forties when I caught the virus. The urge for exercise had been creeping up for some time. The craze for physical fitness houses had just started. Bonnie Muir, had just opened an establishment just off Flinders Lane, not far from the Herald office. Bonnie had been senior prefect at Melbourne Grammar, but he had chosen to become a professional wrestler. I tried to plead that a journalist needed only sufficient strength to carry a portable typewriter. Bonnie insisted that superior strength was good for the brain, so he had me lifting weights, working the rowing machine, improving the biceps by hauling on pulleys. This became not only time consuming but hard to endure.

Then came a new craze, 5BX. It was a system of exercises invented for the Royal Canadian Air Force. In the physical fitness field it was described as the greatest thing since the invention of yoghurt. In 1962 it swept the world and the Canadian Government Printer sold copies of the course by the hundred thousand. It sounded like a new virus or an antibiotic, but 5BX meant five basic exercises which each airman was supposed to complete each day in exactly 11 minutes. For females there was 10BX. There were five extra exercises, apparently because, getting the weight off female hips was rather more complicated.

The five exercises for the male included sit-ups, push-ups, bunny hops, toe-touching, and running on the spot. But here was the rub. One started on scale D, with just a few gentle push-ups, sit-ups etc. to be completed in 11 minutes. Then one had to follow the chart, and gain promotion by moving up the scale. By the time the budding athlete reached A grade the torture required 45 push-ups and running on the spot 205 times. The 11 minutes became a sweating frenzy.

The 5BX craze passed, then came jogging. This was a New Zealand invention, which spread to the United States and then to Australia. It was the new way to fight off heart attacks and defeat death. Every morning at 6 o'clock, I went for a run around Central Park, East Malvern. There was a chorus of barks and howls from every dog in the neighborhood. Quite rightly they were suspicious of a middle-aged character in a track suit running down the street before it was even light. One gentleman in Beaver Street called up Marie and said: "I wish you would stop your husband jogging. His dreadful breathing and groaning keeps me awake." Ernest Neimann, a famous accountant, who had a house on the corner of Kingston Street used to holler out his window, as I went past: "God, you're slow. Can't you do better than that?" Then in the pre-dawn gloom of the Park, there was a shadowy figure who kept lapping me. Neimann was right. I was far too slow at this caper. He could do precisely 2.5 laps to my one. I discovered later that his name was John Landy. His parents lived in Central Park Road, beside the park.

All this became too much. There was the daily stiffness in the legs, and much worse, I seemed to be developing a pain in the hip, so always I had to lie on my left side in bed. That was when the virus took over and I began riding a bicycle to work. Why jog? Bicycling was smoother, easier on the body. I gave up jogging for ever, the pain in the hip never returned.

Marie Rose thought the bicycling was just another strange phase, like the Bonnie Muir Fitness Centre, like the 5BX and the jogging: "I mustn't worry about it," she thought. "It's the sort of thing that sensible people get over." I never did. As pointed out earlier there is no known cure. Of course, everyone in the family caught the virus. Marie rode with me across the United States from Oregon to Virginia. Our eldest daughter Jane had a beautiful 15-speed bicycle made for her by Arthur Sutherland in Armadale. She still has it and rides the countryside around Castlemaine with her children. Our son David bought the top of the line Malvern Star, a Sovereign Star. Kate, our second daughter, wanted a Sovereign Star, but it is a sexist male world. Sovereign Stars were not designed for five foot tall females. She discovered that the 'Malvern' in the Star didn't mean much any more. Osaka Star might have been more relevant. These bicycles were made entirely in Japan, so she went to the Japanese producers and had a Sovereign Star specially built to her size. Our youngest, Sarah, had a Raleigh. She went with us to the United States in 1980 and rode the length of the Mississippi.

So we found there were no generation gaps in bicycling. This was something whole families could do together. We rode with our children and grandchildren. But how fascinating it is to go on any organised ride and note the families that ride together, the parents who rig ingenious tandems which can even cater for five-year-olds or the daring young mothers who pedal bicycles complete with trailer that carry three or even two-year-olds

How often can you identify the exact moment when you are happy? The mind is for ever projecting itself forward to something else, to what's on for dinner, for the lights to turn green, for the dog to stop barking, to holidays far from here, or for the vicar's sermon to come to an end. One sits at the cricket, watching, looking forward to that moment when something might happen, hoping desperately that Shane Warne soon will take a wicket. That done, one is looking forward again, hoping for another.

How often is everything so perfect, that you want this moment to be prolonged. It is possible to think of some. There's the delectable moment when the electric blanket is on and I have my arm around my wife; when the first spring roses bloom and the fragrance is so strong it even comes inside; when I am tasting a first quality Pinot Noir; when listening to the explosion of sound during, say, a Bach Brandenburg Concerto or how about going to sleep in front of the TV after a long day?

Yet, I can count many hundreds of occasions riding a bicycle when I wanted the moment to go on for ever. There was that afternoon in Burgundy. The sun was shining out of a stainless blue sky, there was no wind, the hills were gentle and it was late spring. Vines, which for at least two hundred years had produced great chardonnays, were thrusting out new leaves. Surely this was a 10. There were so many moments, riding through the hills from Moe to Walhalla, or a thousand times riding on the bicycle path by the Yarra to Melbourne.

I have tried to analyse the reasons for such pleasure. Maybe it is the cadence, the gentle rhythm, the easy conversion of muscle power into movement, no jerking, no anger, it's the smooth flow like a river. I have heard of athletes who at times of extreme exertion have claimed of being on a 'high', a state of actual euphoria. I was never an athlete, but one can understand that the release of energy does produce a high and it comes with bicycling.

Then there is the silence. We have produced a world of clamor, vibration and noise. It seems that silence is something dangerous, not to be tolerated. We have music in lifts, we have music and commercials to take up any dead, waiting space on telephones; the proprietors of supermarkets believe their customers will buy better if their ears are belted with rock, and the quality of a motor bike is judged by the quantity of its roar. Ah, but a good bicycle makes no noise at all. Indeed the faintest squeak, or clunk in the chain is as maddening as a cute message on an answer phone. Bicycling is akin to skiing. The beauty of skiing is the peace it provides, one shoots down hill to the accompaniment of only a gentle hiss. Bicycling is the same. Noise comes from cruder machinery all around.

Next there is the feeling of liberation. The early explorers all spoke of it, the daring cyclists who crossed the continent where roads barely existed. Here was a machine which did not have to be fed. It required no oats, no petrol, no steam, just the energy of the rider. It needed no harness, no saddle, no warming up, it was always there, ready for instant action. It could go where other vehicles could not, through narrow gaps, through parks, and if there were a traffic jam, what pleasure to pedal past all the stalled motorists. This gives one an immediate feeling of triumph. The triumph is accentuated once in the city. There is no more useless object in the city than a high polluting tonne of metal with four wheels attached. It has to be placed somewhere and placing in a city car park can be very costly indeed.

There is a yin and a yang to everything. If there are moments of euphoria and bliss, it must be conceded there is also the reverse. The perfect, all-round, well-tuned bicycle nut must also be a masochist. The bicycle is no comfort machine. It does not have a seat that adjusts to your shape upon the touch of a button, it does not have a switch labelled 'climate control', so that one can choose the correct temperature to suit one's sensibilities, or a CD player hi-fi to sooth one's nerves. It has no roof, no windscreen and a seat that looks like a blacksmith's anvil.

When it rains you get wet, when it's hot you perspire and get sunburnt, and when the wind blows it can be like pushing against a truly primeval force. It's a very curious thing, wind. Ask any biker how often did he or she come across a truly splendid tail wind? Wind is tremendously clever, ingenious at devising tricks. According to Murphy's Law it is always blowing in your face. Indeed, it is possible to tell of countless occasions when one set off in the morning with a headwind. The thought comes: 'Ah, this is good, I will have a tailwind on the way home.' Not so, the wind swings around in the afternoon to provide another headwind, just to give the full benefit of its majesty.

But here's the point, the genuine biker likes suffering. This goes right back to the days when the chain-driven bike and even the penny farthing were first invented. The bikers sought out long rides under impossible conditions. They outdid the likes of Burke and Wills, crossing the continent. The clubs loved to offer trips of a 100 miles in a day or contests to see who could ride the furthest in 24 hours.

I was a member of the Ventura Club in California. One had to earn badges. There was the 100-mile badge awarded to club members when they rode 100 miles in a day, but it was not just a simple 100. It involved climbing 3000 feet into the mountains outside Los Angeles. Then there was the 200-mile badge. This had to be done in 24 hours, and for most, unless they were hot shot track cyclists, this meant being in the saddle for nigh on 23 hours, trying to stay awake, enduring all sorts of pain in the legs, arms, back, neck and far more intimate parts.

The body is not designed to sit on such a narrow space for such a long time. The whole crotch and behind goes numb, like your jaw after it has been injected by the dentist. On such rides it is interesting to watch fellow masochists. They rise up out of the saddle and pedal standing for several kilometres hoping some feeling will return to nether regions. Now 200 miles is about 320 kilometres. There was one marvellous female in the Ventura Club. She covered her bicycling shirt with badges so that she looked like General George Patton in the German offensive in World War 2. She did not carry two pearl-handled revolvers on her belt, but they would have suited her. I never managed to score a 200 but this lady had at least six of them on her shirt. She had superbly developed thigh muscles which look like port barrels, and she made it clear badgeless people like me were weakies.

The masochism is not endured for nothing. In its curious way it is the truest pleasure of all. There is a feeling of triumph, achievement and extraordinary happiness when it all stops. You got there by your own muscles. It's nice.

There is however the cyclist who handles masochism with great care and is careful never to go outside. When we were working in California from 1979 to 1982, exercise bikes were actually outselling 10 speed and 15 speeds. There was an extraordinary range but one that almost made the heart miss a beat was called 'The Heart Mate'. It sold for $4000. It had all the regular features, it noted the cadence of your pedalling, the speed, and the distance you allegedly covered. There were sensors in the handlebar grips that gave a digital read out of your heart beat, and they also gave a report on your mounting blood pressure should a heat attack be at hand. There was a fan which provided an artificial breeze which increased as you pedalled faster; there was an ergometer which gave a reading on the energy output and how many calories you were consuming. There was a built in TV set and stereo, which provided the morning news and stock reports. But the video was the particular asset, you could make up a ride simulating hills and flat country. Indeed you could even plan a simulated course right across the country and never go outside.

As for the true outdoors people, please do not get the impression that bicyclists are angels. Many of them are deeply irresponsible. Perhaps three out of four feel there is no necessity to obey road rules. They ride through red lights exactly as it suits. They ride the wrong way against the traffic, they make hair-raising short cuts across intersections. They weave in out and around lines of heavy traffic with a derring-do that would be the envy even of motor cyclists. Yet most remarkable of all are cyclists who ride at night. Some ride only with tail lights, but more often they carry no lights at all, not even reflective clothing. I have tried to keep a personal score. The average is this; three out of four cyclists carry no lights and depend only on reflectors. This seems madness beyond belief.

This irresponsibility comes perhaps from the very freedom of bicycling. Freedom brings with it a carefree madness. Here I am on my bicycle, I can do anything. The motorist on the other hand resents the bicyclist because he doesn't pay. The bike rider pays no registration fee, no third party insurance. Sometimes there is personal insurance cover, but this is rare. The carefree existence goes even further; the bike rider parks his or her machine for nothing.

It is time for a change in Australia. Bicycles that use the open road should be registered. They should have a number-plate as they do in China. This would make cyclists identifiable and it would give them a legitimacy. They would feel they are there by right. Oh yes, they would be more easily identified by police, but then their bicycles, when stolen, could be found more easily. If all cyclists were carrying a number plate, who knows, perhaps it would give them a new responsibility.

This book is in two sections. There are four chapters which tell of a golden, euphoric age in cycling, which ran from the 1870s until the turn of the century. Then there are another nine chapters which tell the story of the bicycle, re-born 100 years later. This tale is largely autobiographical, part of a trilogy which began with *No Brains at All*, followed by *My Life with the Demon* and now *The Confessions of a Bicycle Nut*.

Now in my mid-'70s I am still riding the bicycle, using it as a means of transport, for all errands and for trips to the city. I average around 100 kilometres a week. The bicycle craze as a social phenomenon in its first great phase faded at the turn into the 20th century. One wonders whether we will have an exact repeat, a sad decline that will begin the 21st century.

I prefer to think that the reverse will happen, that the God automobile will wither and start to disappear, that the pollution will go, that the awful statistics of death by car will become a long ago memory like the bubonic plague, and the happy scene will be a sea of bicycles with everyone pedalling in safety.
