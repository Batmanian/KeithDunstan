title: The Love of the Wheel
date: 1999-11-11
summary: Traces the bicycle's first golden age in Melbourne from the 1868 arrival of the velocipede through the reign of the high-wheeled 'ordinary' to the safety bicycle and pneumatic tyre, including the sad case of Bendigo rider W.J. Crowle, killed testing his 'Invincible' ordinary in 1884.

It is fascinating that there have been two great periods in the social use of the bicycle, almost 100 years apart. The first lasted two decades from 1880 to 1900. The second was like a re-birth of belief in Jesus and began circa 1970, when it was right and marvellous to ride the bike again. The born-again bicycle continued to blossom right through into the millennium.

It is nigh impossible to underestimate the joy, the passion aroused by the advent of the bicycle. It was the new type of transport, a liberating force. D.J. Quinn in the journal, *Review of Reviews*, was typical in his eloquence. "The bike is an addition to human methods of locomotion as definite and as epoch-making as the steamship and the railway...Mounted on the flying wheel a youth seems to have become suddenly independent of feet, and if he has not acquired wings he has got something very nearly approaching them. Our wide open space and level roads are exactly fitted to the realm of the bike. No long winter, with roads snow-covered, or slippery with a mail of ice, makes the roads dangerous for months. Australian blood, too, is thirsty for swift motion, and the joy of flying, self-impelled, through leagues of blowing air." (1)

Mr Quinn in his joy could see no fault with the bicycle: "It over-runs frontiers, it obliterates race antipathies, includes a sense of cameraderie amongst foreigners, breaks down social barriers and brings 'the nicest people' into line with the common mass....Of exercise it is the very champagne, it takes us out of ourselves, out of the fever and stress, the smoke and dirt, the noise and contamination of city life into the pure atmosphere and open face of heaven."

Many thought bicycling a sin, a dangerous pastime discovered by the young to irritate their elders. Parsons railed against the bicycle from the pulpit. Cyclists sinned against God by bicycling on Sundays; even worse, they sinned by bicycling when they should have been at church. Then men and women went off into the country on bicycles and there were fears about this. Heaven knows what took place behind hedges and there were worries that the bicycle could cause a collapse in the nation's morals.

This new bicycle was so marvellous, so convenient. How could one get around a city? Horse-drawn trams arrived in the 1860s, suburban trains developed in the 1880s and Melbourne's cable tram system began in 1885. But really, one was dependent on the slow, polluting, fly-attracting, inconvenient horse. Horses had to be watered, harnessed, groomed, housed and fed, and carriages alone took up an immense amount of space. The hire of hansom cabs was costly. The bike took up so little space. Some kept their precious velocipedes in their living room and those wide Victorian hallways were wonderful for showing off one's most precious possession, 'a beautiful glittering wheel'. Yes, here was a machine that could go anywhere.

In the mid-1890s it was absolutely the thing to ride a bicycle. Arthur Balfour, leader of the House of Commons, rode to Parliament on his bicycle. Field Marshal Lord Roberts, V.C., commander in chief in Ireland, rode a bicycle. The King of Greece shocked his subjects by dodging cabs in the streets of Athens pedalling on pneumatic tyres. Indeed, royalty all over Europe was enthused. Queen Amelie of Portugal was crazy for her bike. It was even suggested in newspaper advertisements that Britain's Royal Family had taken to the bike, including His Royal Highness, Edward, Prince of Wales. When one considers the majesty of his girth in 1895 one can only be skeptical of how many hours he spent on his machine. However his mistress, Lillie Langtry, the beautiful actress, was certainly a bike rider. George Bernard Shaw, too, claimed that a bicycle was just the thing for a literary man and he rode his bike around Hyde Park.

It would be a mistake to say they were cheap. A bicycle cost more than a horse. A Rover diamond machine, a bicycle which, interestingly enough, did not look much different from a bike today, cost 28 pounds 10 shillings; a Humber was 30 pounds, a Rudge-Whitworth 27 pounds, a Raglan 26 pounds and a Rapid was 25 pounds or the cheaper model, 21 pounds. The expensive Humber cost six to seven times the average man's weekly wage. So in today's terms it was worth at least $3000. Prices did come down in the late 1890s but in the beginning bicycling was for the socially inclined and well-to-do.

Originally bicycles could be purchased at only two or three specialists stores, but in the 1890s they were everywhere. In Melbourne, the big stores set up whole departments to cater for the bicycle. Foy & Gibson sold almost every make on the market and Georges in Collins Street had an indoor *Cyclotorium* where ladies could learn to handle their machines under expert guidance. *The Review of Reviews* reported in 1896: 'Bikes may be purchased from one's draper, one's tailor, one's bootmaker, one's piano dealer, one's music seller, just like any other every day commodity. In the country it is even retailed in druggists' establishments.' (2)

The bicycle did not have a long history. The original was the 'hobby horse' which gained a vogue in England and France in the 1830s. The rider literally sat on two wheels and was propelled by pushing with feet on the ground. Then in 1861 Pierre Lallamont and Pierre Michaux of Paris fitted pedals to the front wheel of a hobbyhorse. They had seen the possibilities of the cranked axle, like the crank of a grindstone. (3)

This was a dramatic step forward; the rider of a bicycle could stay erect in beautiful balance without having a foot touching the earth. The velocipedes smartly won the name 'boneshakers'; they had wooden wheels, iron bearings, solid tyres and no spokes. The front wheel was slightly larger than the rear, and the outfit was guaranteed to almost shake the teeth out of a man's head. Yet they were strong, often beautifully made, and could be bounced safely over a kerb. This was hardly surprising, for a boneshaker could weigh up to 45 kilograms

Velocipedes became the rage of Europe and America, the sensation of the Paris exhibition of 1867. They first appeared in Melbourne in 1868, ridden by William Charles Kernot, professor of Engineering at Melbourne University. He was fascinated by every kind of new invention and he took a hand in the design of the first steerable torpedoes. He built his own velocipede and claimed the record from Melbourne to Geelong.(4) Later, when he became vice-president of the Victorian League of Wheelmen, he told how he rode his boneshaker, his 'wary wobbler', about the streets of East Melbourne. One day he passed John Pascoe Fawkner, Melbourne's founder, who was riding in a carriage. Fawkner looked 'in astonishment at the demonstration of hostility by this novel vehicle'. The professor said Fawkner died soon afterwards, but whether the novel sight was too much for him and caused his demise he was not prepared to say.

Melbourne was the first Australian city to find the bicycle irresistible. There was actually a bicycle race on the Melbourne Cricket Ground on 10 June 1869 with a crowd of 'curious spectators', which various journals estimated at 10-12,000. J. Finlay of Fitzroy and R. Warnock of Emerald Hill covered the last two miles of the race in 11 minutes 19 seconds to win by a lap. A century later, any top athlete could do the distance faster on foot. (7)

Certainly the *Australasian* was unimpressed: "Velocipedes have had their trial and the verdict is that as a means of creating sport they are a failure. The universal opinion is that they won't do." The *Australasian* was convinced that the single-wheel machines were the real answer.

"Unicycles, when their management is understood, will soon take the shine out of bicycles, as I am told that in competent hands they can be worked up to the rate of 40 m.p.h. I suppose that the next thing we shall hear of some mountebank running one across the falls of Niagara on a single rope." (8)

The mountebanks of Melbourne were a far greater problem because the massive over-weight boneshakers had no brakes and once they started to hurtle down hills, the result at the bottom frequently must have been awe-inspiring.

The boneshaker reached its peak of popularity in 1872-73. Then in 1875 the revolutionary 'spider wheel' came to Melbourne. It was an English invention. It had an all metal wheel with an enormous number of spokes of single pieces of wire, one end clamped to the rim, the other to the hub.

It was logical progression. It was a fixed wheel device. The only way to provide higher gearing was to make a bigger wheel. The bigger wheel made travel over rough roads much more comfortable. As for the rear wheel, it was only there for balance, and it was small to reduce the weight. So they were called 'spiders', 'high bikes' or 'ordinaries'. It was after 1900 before anyone used the title 'penny farthing', and by that time they were long out of fashion.

Messrs Mason and Clarke of South Yarra were the importers, and velocipedists from all over Melbourne gathered to inspect them. They thought spiders were flimsy beyond belief. On the first day there was great debate as to who would be brave enough to try one. Messrs Mason, Hobson and Bagot took the plunge. A great number of Melburnians gathered to inspect the machines. They thought the spiders were flimsy beyond belief. Oh dear, that incredible lofty contraption! The velocipedists made bets that the big wheel would collapse and the driver fall through it before he had gone half a mile. (9)

But the penny farthing, of course was a phenomenal success. Distances of up to 14 and-a-half miles were covered in a single hour. In 1873 a team of riders went from London to John o'Groats, 1287 km in 14 days, and newspapers commented even the strongest horse would break down under such a journey. (10)

Mr R.C. Bagot was one of the first examples of the bicycle passion. He fell in love with the bike from the *moment* he saw his first boneshaker, and after being the first to buy a spider, he admitted that by 1885 he had bought 14 bicycles. When every new improved machine arrived, he had to own one. (11)

The new spider, or ordinary as it was called, came in various sizes. The enormity of the front wheel depended on the length of one's legs. Campbell & Co. of 13 Flinders Street, Melbourne, advertised "Bicycles suitable for beginners 46 inch, 48 inch or 50 inch, to let or hire". Mr Bagot had a 54 inch which, he said, was very popular. In the Austral Wheel Race of 1890, Mr Gordon of Gippsland rode the "tallest machine in the world". It was sixty-five inches high, while Mr Gordon himself was six feet four inches.

For the beginner, this high bike was a daunting object. Mounting it was extremely tricky, and alighting even trickier. The rider had to grab the handle bars, run forward, place a foot on a tiny step just above the small wheel, and while maintaining balance, smoothly slip into the saddle, then find the pedals. Seeing it was a fixed wheel, these were flying round windmill style. So he had to get his feet on to them very quickly and start pedalling. If there was any error, it was a long way to fall. Getting off again, one had to do everything gracefully in reverse, come to a halt, step backwards on to the little step and on to the ground. The finish was even trickier than the start and there were many crashes before the rider learned to do it with style.

Ah, but when actually riding, way up high, above pedestrians, above carriages even, there was a feeling of majesty, superiority and movement. The larger the front wheel, of course, the higher the gearing. They could be very fast over flat country, terrifyingly fast down hill, but uphill, that was another matter. A good spider rider needed the muscles of a weightlifter. (12)

Unquestionably they were dangerous. Some had brakes, a primitive device called a spoon brake which levered down on the solid tyre. However, the centre of gravity for the rider was immediately over the huge front wheel. As soon as he applied pressure, it jerked to a stop and almost certainly he would be projected head first. The bike makers tried putting a brake on the rear wheel, but it carried almost zero weight. When the brake came on it the little wheel just skidded to no effect. The only real way to brake was to push back on the pedals. One can only imagine how hazardous this was on a steep hill. The rider on his ordinary had to go down as slowly as he came up, and with the high gearing, this was slow indeed. If suddenly the pedals got away from him, he was gone. The big bike with its solid hard tires would speed down like a projectile and if he hit a bump or failed to take a curve, the consequences were awful. The centre of gravity was up front and the tendency was to go straight forward and land on one's head. The smart rider had to learn to fall sideways.

The journals of the day reported many accidents, often fatal. In 1884 *Australian Cycling News* told the sad story of W.J. Crowle, a 28-year-old who had won a series of races in Castlemaine, Ballarat and Sandhurst (Bendigo). On the day of his accident he had been making repairs to his bike, a 53-inch 'Invincible', one of the first its kind to come to Sandhurst. Just to check it out he decided to ride to Lake Weeroona. On the way home he was moving to over-take another cyclist. The tyre on the big wheel came adrift, caught in the brake, and he 'precipitated head foremost to the ground with great violence.' He never regained consciousness and he died the next day at the Bendigo Hospital.

There had to be a better way. W.R. George, honorary treasurer of the Sydney Bicycle Club, had a similar experience. He was worth listening to because, just possibly, Mr George was the first man to ride a bike in Australia. In 1866 a friend in Paris sent him a sketch of a boneshaker. George, a resident of Bathurst at the time, knew "an ingenious Yankee who could make anything". He induced the Yankee, with the aid of a blacksmith, to build him a boneshaker. He said this was the first bicycle built and ridden in New South Wales, and a very creditable machine it was too. He had been in a very poor state of health, suffering from alarming heart palpitations. Yet after three months of riding his boneshaker, he was cured. "The boneshaker had shaken every symptom of heart disease out of my body," said Mr George.

Next he took on a 54-inch ordinary. But the tyre came off suddenly while he was having "a spin". "I was made to bite the dust in a way I hope never to do again. I was cut from head to foot." (13)

After this, George said, he was in a nervous state and frightened of his own shadow. He almost gave up riding altogether, but along came the 'safety' machine. For nigh on a decade, the safety looked like a penny farthing that had shrunk in the wash, only the penny was smaller and the farthing was larger. They depended on gearing on the front wheel. *Australian Cycling News* reported: "Formerly there were many men, who would have gladly become cyclists, if it were not for a certain timidity about mounting high wheels, and the consequent risk of 'croppers'. Such men can now dispel their fears for in the new safety bicycles those risks are entirely done away with. By the system of gearing the same speed can be got out of a safety bicycle with a 36-inch wheel, as from an ordinary machine with a 60-inch." (14).

There were various types of safeties. The first in 1878 was the 'Xtraordinary Challenge'. It was geared by what was indeed an extraordinary system of levers. W.R. George bought himself an Xtraordinary and he was thrilled with it. He said: "The Xtraordinary seems to me the very acme of perfection as regards comfort and safety. I ride mine with the aid of a King of the Road lamp, on the darkest nights, and over the roughest roads, and I do from six to ten miles every morning before having my bath...I believe no man who has the use of his limbs is too old to ride a safety machine." (15)

*Australian Cycling News. Courtesy: State Library of Victoria.*

The Safety kept improving. By 1885 there were other varieties, and here was the first use of the chain. However, the sprocket and the cog was all on the large front wheel. The front fork was extended so that it could carry both the sprocket and the chain wheel. First there was the Safety with the chain wheel on one side, then it came with chain wheels on both sides. Most commentators thought the double chain machine was easier to steer.

And so the Safety continued to evolve until the coming of the ultimate, the Safety with a chain which ran a sprocket on the rear wheel and both wheels the same size. The frame was called 'a diamond' and a century later bicycles still looked very much the same. In 1886 Rover had a Safety with fixed gear driving a 30-inch rear wheel and a 32-inch front wheel, complete with a diamond frame. By 1890 the Rover catalogue boasted eight different diamond Safeties for women, touring and racing. (16)

But there was a real flaw to these Safeties. They were as jaw-jarring as the Boneshaker. The solid tyres served the Ordinaries well, the front wheel with its network of 54-inch spokes was shock-absorbing. The next great bicycle boom had to depend on John Boyd Dunlop, a Scottish veterinary surgeon. In 1887 he invented the first successful pneumatic tyre. He was trying to improve one of his son's toys and he made his tyre out of rubber tubing bound with linen tape. The invention was patented in 1888. At first pneumatics were costly, absurd to look at and the subject of much amusement. The tyres and wheels received such names as 'sausage rolls', 'balloons' and 'steam rollers'. The jump from the half-inch belt of solid rubber to balloons several inches in diameter was too much. But they had a superb advantage; not only did they give an easy ride, but they did not catch in the cable tram slot. Steadily they improved. By the early 1890s, there was the wire-on tyre, there was the inner tube, and what's more, the pneumatics were proving reliable. Dunlop, Michelin, Hutchinson and U.S. Rubber were all in the market. (17)

The bicycle journals constantly carried stories and advertisements of new tyres which were 'puncture proof', nothing could penetrate them. Even a century later one might suggest science has not entirely overcome this problem, but they were confident in the 1890s. Dunlop even broke into verse.

We have taken up the whiteman's burden

And issued a tyre so strong

That in future the new Multiflex Dunlops

Will carry all cyclists along.

No need for further anxieties,

All cyclists can ride on in peace;

The new Multiflex Tyres are beauties,

Guaranteed to ensure comfort and ease.

We have taken up the white man's burden

By giving a tyre that will stand

To those cyclists who ride for pleasure-

Those of the happy band;

So that the roughest of roads have no terrors,

Nor the puncture fiend the power to detract

From the pleasures derived from wheeling

Way back on the old coach track. (18)

Towards the end of the century, a new invention was reported almost every week. There was a vogue for chainless bicycles, which had a shaft with bevilled gears which worked directly on to the back axle. However, the transference of power was never as simple and efficient as the chain. They did not last. Next came the Collier two-speed gear, a forerunner to the Sturmey Archer three-speed. The gear was in the rear hub and the *Australian Cyclist* gave a glowing report of its success. At Messrs Lincoln Stuart's Cycle Factory they built 'a little mountain' over the roof. Mr T.C. Fisher rode up a one in three grade with comparative ease. Then he tried going up the roof in high gear and it could not be done. (19)

Next came the free wheel and the back pedal hub brake. Until 1899 bicycles had fixed wheels, so the legs never stopped churning, even going down hill. Some bikes had foot rests away from the pedals, and the feet were raised while the pedals whizzed free like a food mixer. The free wheel was the greatest new invention since the pneumatic tyre. Enthusiasts claimed freewheeling made all the difference. Yes, at least a third of one's travel was free. In 1900 *Australian Cyclist* reported: "The free wheel has caught on in Victoria in a manner that defies the supply. We guarantee there were not half a dozen free wheels on sale in Victoria on the 1st inst (May 1)." (20)

The hub brake made a huge contribution to safety. Newspapers constantly were railing against cyclists for riding with no brakes. Probably better than 50 per cent of them went without. The brake most used was the spoon brake, a crude device which pressed straight down the tyre and was unpopular because it scarred the precious pneumatics. There was the rim brake, but for this to work the wheel had to be absolutely true. So many thought better to go without and push back on the fixed wheel. Come the turn of the century, it was possible to have all in one package the hub brake and the freewheel. Bicycles were safer.

Of course, safety came in many forms. In the 1880s doctors recommended that the elderly should stick to tricycles, which were very popular. However, their safety was a matter of debate. The tricycles with their complex steering had almost as many accidents as the high-wheelers. Apart from tricycles, there were quadricycles, two-wheelers-abreast, and machines with all sorts of ingenious cranking devices. Some had pedals as well as hand cranks; and then there were the sociables. These were free-wheelers in which the lady and the gentleman sat side-by-side, as if they were going to church in a gig.

Then, with all the enthusiasm, there had to be clubs. There was an early Boneshaker Club which left little in the way of records. The first important club in Australia was the Melbourne Bicycle Club, formed on 1 August 1878.

Here were the rules of the M.B.C.

Annual subscription twelve shillings and sixpence.

That the club uniform consist of myrtle green coat, knickerbockers, stockings and cap, and silver badge. Members are requested to wear the same at Club meetings and when touring. White straw hats with club ribbon (green and gold) to be worn in summer. Club costumiers, Alston & Brown, Collins Street West. That the uniform not be worn on Sundays except on runs extending over several days.

Regulations.

1. The captain, or in his absence the vice-captain, or any member of the committee shall act as leader in every excursion, and his directions shall be obeyed.

2. That the vice-captain in all runs shall keep the rear, and signal (one whistle) to the leader whenever the tail of the company is getting out-distanced. On hearing the signal the leader shall slacken till he receives a second whistle (two whistles), indicating that they have closed up.

3. That the strictest order be maintained during all runs.

4. That when riding in single file, an interval of two yards shall be kept between each rider, and when in double file, three yards. In hilly country these distances should be doubled.

5. That each member is requested to carry a bell or whistle and to use a lamp when riding at night.

BUGLE CALLS.

Attention.

The Assembly or Prepare to Mount.

Mount.

Dismount.

Form Single File.

Form Twos.

Dinner Call.

Attention should be sounded before the Assembly or Dinner Calls to indicate to riders that they are official. (22)

These rules made two interesting points. They mention 'he' and not 'she'. There were no female members in 1878. The rules also make the delicate point that members should not wear their badge or uniforms on Sundays. There was much concern amongst the clergy about this new pastime. It was immoral, bicyclists were off touring on Sundays when they ought to be in church. Even as late as 1897 the Presbyterian General Assembly in Melbourne condemned the bicycle as 'evil' for its desecration of the Sabbath. (23)

Cyclists became so sensitive to criticism that they started holding 'Church Parades'. The first was in 1895. The organisers were Messrs Horace Dean and C.J. Smith. The cyclists, all from metropolitan clubs, gathered at Princes Bridge, Melbourne, at 10 a.m. then they set off for St Pauls Anglican Church, Roxburgh Street, Ascot Vale. They left punctually at 10.15 a.m. with Mr Con Dwyer as Parade Marshal, Dean and Smith out front, five lady cyclists immediately behind them, then the main body.

The Reverend W. McKie reserved half the seats in his church for the cyclists, but it was not enough. There were 225 riders and many of them had to stand. McKie in his sermon pointed out the benefits of muscular activity. Ah, but how should it be used? It should be used "in the highest way, for the acquirement and enrichment of that other life, which is not subject to time or death, which is no less real because it is spiritual." (24)

The President of the Melbourne Bicycle Club and the Victorian Cyclists Union was a Judge of the Supreme Court, Mr Justice Williams. He was a dignified gentleman with mutton-chop whiskers, but there was no limit to his devotion to the bicycle. He would ride his machine, on circuit, all around Victoria. The Judge even gave accounts of his travels to the bicycle journals. The Judge rode 'a safety' almost certainly an 'Xtrordinary' or an early model with the chain drive on the front wheel and solid tyres. In 1883 he wrote:

Friday it was blowing a westerly gale, but not raining though the gale was in my teeth. I was determined to have a ride of some sort, so I mounted my safety at 9.30 and reached Camperdown at 1 o'clock, you can imagine the force of the wind when I tell I had to work nearly as hard downhill as I have on a fair day to work up hill. It was a real hard plug the whole way and the much talked of impetus on the Stony Rises did not fall to my experience. However I rode the distance, 29 miles without a dismount. (25)

From there he continued his tour to Warrnambool, to Hamilton, to Ararat, to Moyston, to Stawell, to Horsham, to Beaufort. He would be met by officials on the way and he would preside over cases in each town. He received guards of honour from local bicycle clubs. There was particularly good treatment at Hamilton. Mr Farrol of the Hamilton Bicycling Club found him 22 miles out at Macarthur and they rode together.

To my delight Mr Farroll met me. While at Macarthur down came the rain in such torrents it was 2.30 before we could resume our saddles. With frequent dismounts on account of the fearful state of the roads, and still against the wind, we struggled on, reaching Hamilton at 6.20. Under the circumstances you may imagine how glad I was to have Mr Farroll's company. He was riding his 60-inch British Challenge, and being 6ft. 4in. himself, I on my Safety looked like Lilliputians beside him and his steed. (26)

The newspapers in Melbourne did not approve. This was no way for a Supreme Court Judge to behave. The *Herald* wrote:

That the whole legal profession is scandalized at the burlesquing of the dignity of the judicial bench by Mr Justice Williams going circuit on a bicycle. What a contrast, they say, to the pomp and ceremony attending the entry of the judges in English assize towns, where they ride in splendid equipage and are received by a High Sheriff and other officials, and the Judges attend church in State on the Sunday.

Fancy a Judge of the Supreme Court of Victoria entering a large city like Ballarat on a velocipede and being met, not by the sheriff and other functionaries, but by half a dozen boobies on bicycles. (27)

*Bicycling News* rushed to his defence:

I desire to point out that cycling stands at the very head of athletics in England, and that its votaries are generally of good status. In Great Britain alone there are 300,000 riders, among whom may be mentioned the Prince of Wales, Prince George of Wales, Lord Sherbrooke and two of Her Majesty's judges of the Queen's Bench, as well as a host of noblemen and gentlemen. The judges mentioned frequently do their circuits on bicycles.(25)

*Bicycling News* might have added that Princess Maud of Wales received 48 bicycles among her wedding presents. Twenty-four of them came with the message from the manufacturers that should she find this type of frame unsuitable they would be happy to provide a different model. (28)
