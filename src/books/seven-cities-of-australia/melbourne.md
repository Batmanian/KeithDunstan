**Chairman Jeff** once wrote: 'Melbourne seems to have been ironed out by a celestial flatiron determined to smooth out every crease and wrinkle for the sake of making tram tracks.' It is flat — ideal for trams, bicycles, roller skates or for bowling a hoop.

I like to close my eyes and dream of the Melbourne of the nineties. Close by the Richmond Brewery was the Rosella Tomato Sauce factory, and come autumn when the tomato harvest was on, the whole suburb would be bathed in the rich, red smell. The tomato smell nearly always won.

In 1976 the CSIRO made a survey and found that less than seventy per cent of Australians living in Melbourne had a broad accent. In the inner suburbs you are most likely to find the Australian-born — Prahran, South Yarra and Richmond.

Toorak is still the biggest of all Australian snob symbols. It is an extremely small pocket, yet people who live on the border will pretend that they live in the very inside of Toorak. There are even grades of quality in Toorak. After you've made your first $200,000 I think it is better to live north of Grange Road. After you've made your second $200,000, you should have your place at Portsea.

Back in the 1890s, when the great Sarah Bernhardt visited Melbourne, she stayed at Menzies. It was a time of considerable wealth. Now St Kilda is making a comeback. The Esplanade goes for miles and is altogether wonderful. On Sunday morning there is an open-air craft market right there on the Esplanade and every leather worker and bead peddler comes to show his wares. The bay is still beautiful. The bayside suburbs — Brighton, Sandringham and Beaumaris — are altogether right.

Yet why an inferiority complex? Let's stretch the old town out on the psychiatrist's couch and see why. Melbourne was the capital of Australia until 1927. During the gold rush years of the 1850s and 1880s, Melbourne was a city of brashness, excitement and self-confidence, a gaily garish marvel. The public buildings were astonishing for a city so young, and Melbourne grew with an air of magic — 'The Star of the South.'

And then came the bank crash and the severe depression of 1892. St Paul's Cathedral announced a Day of Public Humiliation, and the clergy called on the financiers to pray for forgiveness for their mistakes. The blow to confidence was immense. We produced little, did little to compare, from then on to the prosperous building boom of the 1960s. A glumness spread over Melbourne that was not entirely lifting.

The city still has a frightful climate. As an old writer recently pointed out, Melbourne is closer to the Equator than Madrid, Athens or Rome. That's about what our latitude is, and we don't ever forget it.

Do you mind if I take over as your tour guide at this point? Naturally, one is affected by one's own enthusiasm, but for a Melburnian — and I am proud to be one — the city has many fine qualities.

I hope the day would be Wednesday and I hope it would be fine. On Wednesdays and Saturdays the bells provide the oldest peal outside the British Isles, and I like to sit outside in Young & Jackson's across the road.

Then strolling down Collins Street to the Yarra Bank. The old Yarra — that's another subject that suffers from bad public relations. I go along there in the morning; sometimes the Yarra is a dirty brown colour, but just occasionally the water sparkles in the sun. Here along the Yarra in the evening is the best show in town. At dusk, there are thousands of seagulls, swans, ducks, kayaks, surf club boats and acres of rowing eights under the coaching launches.

You should observe the faces of the Melbourne Grammar, Scotch and Wesley boys as they go to school for the Head of the River. Then go to the Melbourne Club. There you will find the patrician, aloof atmosphere and be thankful that you are not a member of the Southern Hemisphere's most exclusive club. Prime Minister Stanley Bruce entertained the winning Melbourne Grammar crew to dinner at his house in Walsh Street, South Yarra.

We could have dinner at a barbeque on the Yarra Bank, then wander over for a concert at the Melbourne Symphony. Or you can lie on a rug on the grass — much better with the stars.

There are some things that are practically compulsory for your tourist. It is compulsory to visit the National Gallery — but don't go on a Monday when it's shut. Barry Humphries, the most sensitive observer of Melbourne society, once likened Collins Street to the Melbourne end of the Champs Elysees. Collins Street is crooked — Melbourne streets are often crooked — and there is the Greek Orthodox Cathedral, the Oriental Hotel and the ANZ Bank.

There is also the Collins Place with its twin towers turned on an angle. It has dragged on for years.

I prefer the marvellous, massive bluestone Gothic pile of St Patrick's. The designer was the famous nineteenth-century English architect William Butterfield. He designed it back home in England, which is why it has such style. St Patrick's spire reaches just a little too high toward heaven. It is a superb 105 metres and just capable of looking down on its surroundings.

In the 1970s the fumes of car exhausts in Swanston Street had ruined the bluestone and the government spent a million dollars on a chemical rinse, a million-dollar restoration. The bluestone of St Patrick's looked good for another thousand years.

I remember Collins Street as not merely superior but lofty. Good manners, good restaurants, taste, elegance; it was always fashionable.

Now I would take my tourist to Parliament House. John Betjeman when he visited here described it as 'the best art room in the world.' I think it is exquisite, a work of genius. It is all neo-Corinthian. The Honourable the Speaker sits in an oyster shell. The decor of this chamber is covered with roses and cherubs plus eight ladies weeping. Often the debates were excruciatingly dull, but I love it. I think it is genuinely beautiful.

I love the old Treasury Building with its elegant classical Renaissance facade. Diagonally opposite is the Windsor Hotel. I think I would make it compulsory to stay at the Windsor Hotel. There are, of course, those glittering hotels — the Hilton, the Hotel Melbourne — but to recall the last age of elegance, most Melburnians would immediately nominate the Windsor. 'Never let it go,' as one guest put it. 'A fireplace in your room, high ceilings.'

My tourist and I would pause for a beer in the Cricketers Bar at the Windsor; I would like to explain a little more about the character of Melbourne. Melbourne is the centre of the national shrine — sport: football, tennis, boxing, racing, cricket, soccer. The sporting facilities are incomparable. I think my guests should go to Flemington Racecourse if only to see the course itself. The Melbourne Cricket Ground is equally as important.

Melbourne City Council is so arranged that never since Melbourne became a city has it had a Lord Mayor who is working class. This has made me just a little proud. We might be the massage parlour gambling capital of the world, but we do work at it.

Even when no match is taking place it is a strange experience to walk to the centre of the MCG and feel faintly overwhelmed. Yet it is fascinating and quite extraordinary to be there with a capacity crowd of 100,000 people. The noise is overwhelming — like a soup-powered Wagnerian organ drowning everything, a frightening and inspiring thing.

You hear it at a Test match, late in the afternoon when the day is going well. And then they start to roll their empty cans down the precipitous colosseum steps and the sound is unlike anything else. It is a Melburnian sound, as Melburnian as meat pies and tomato sauce.

Professor Ian Turner of Monash University used to say that Melbourne was the only city in the world where you could go into any local pub and hear the same conversation. Even Gough Whitlam, who is not a Victorian at all, became a passionate Collingwood supporter.

But I think there are three reasons for the Victorian obsession with Australian Rules football. It is genuinely a most spectacular game, beautiful to watch. The second reason is the passion you might give to the arrival of the Pope, and the third reason is historical. Back in 1866 there were no tennis matches, no cricket, no golf, no polo, no race meetings, and the rivalry between the suburbs was intense. The people of Collingwood stared at the citizens of Fitzroy. The residents of Richmond were cocky and overbearing. The suburbs of Carlton and South Melbourne eyed the citizens of Essendon. There were even cases of female spectators indulging in battles with each other.

The great English novelist Anthony Trollope found it very rugged and always worth watching in 1873. Premier Hamer wants us to be known as the Garden State.

You should go to Emil's restaurant in Lonsdale Street — very elegant, and Swiss. And if you are looking for good fun, go to North Melbourne to Smacka's Place. Smacka Fitzgibbon owns it and he's just possibly the best jazz singer of his generation. Then there's Jimmy Watson's in Lygon Street — he has a wine bar you can eat in. There's Mietta's in Collins Street. You know the old Young and Jacksons Hotel facing Melbourne's Flinders Street Station — the painting of Chloe is still there. You can eat there too.

One of the very odd notions about Melbourne is that it is featureless. It has the great open bay. Melbourne kept on motoring through the years when it was almost despised by the automobile. I was delighted to see an issue of a book published in Hong Kong with a centre-spread featuring two Melbourne tram conductors. The advertisement urged one and all to fly south for a ride on Melbourne's mahogany-lined trams.

What's more, Bruce Beresford, the film director, came here to make a documentary on the life of Henry Handel Richardson and for years told me it was the most knockabout, wonderfully nineteenth-century city in the world. After taking in that film, I promise, you will think it was crawling in the old traditions. You will have a beautiful old mansion, and lots of time.

Most of them have gone from London — those basements with big balconies and lofty ceilings. And it's all there. Melbourne. Marvellous. It was good to hear someone call it marvellous again.
