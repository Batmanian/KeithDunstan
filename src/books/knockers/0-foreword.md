DETRIMENTAL BLOKES

'It was always an open question how long Barrie Humphries would last.' Thus wrote Mr Max Harris in *Nation* early in nineteen-sixty, reviewing one of my first gramophone records.

I was living in a Notting Hill basement, at the time; working at night in Wall's Ice Cream factory in Acton, and auditioning by day before indifferent West End theatrical managements. My nostalgia for warm, kindly Australia where I was a small but coruscating starlet was at its most poignant when several friends rushed Harris's appraisal of my fugitive talent to my London letterbox. I have since reflected how odd it is that Australian chums always airmail the bad news, and forget to post the encomiums; or else dispatch them to me in leaking Great Western bottles cast into the Yarra.

I suppose it is flattering that one's career should have been 'an open question' at all! A tribute to the refinement of one's art that so many deeply concerned Australians should worry about its durability. Oddly enough, self-pity, the English weather, and the smell of Raspberry Ripple all led me to take that little press clipping rather badly at the time. How long would I last? That indeed was the open question I myself was asking as I trudged from agent to ice creamery to agent. Funny they had all been asking the same question for years back in dear old Aussie-land.

Now I see Mr Harris's unmalicious jeremiad in perspective. He didn't, after all, suggest that the burning, open question was closed. It was open wasn't it? There was hope. And how could some diligent writer of feuilletons in sunny Adelaide know what tortures of frustration and self-doubt the distant subject of his pen was undergoing? Should artists be especially protected from adverse criticism; pampered by their countrymen merely because they were Aussies and deserved 'a fair go'? Here was the crux of it. I was an Australian and Max was an Australian and Australians jolly well shouldn't say things like that about each other: or about *me* anyhow!

Yet 'knockery' is a national characteristic, and we are damned good at it, viz this volume. Mr Dunstan is a most insidious knocker himself to compile such a book which might be read abroad, and its contents—true or false—employed by some foreign churl to knock our international image of bland tolerance. Am I not a knocker myself, for knocking knockers? And are the opponents of the knocker merely whingers, fearful of the devastating Truth which only the courageous and dedicated Knocker dares to speak? Is, perchance, the Knocker-knocker some base crawler, afraid of making fearless forthright criticism of the high and the mighty?

It is interesting to note that the classic Australian Knocker, he with the most withering panache, never doubts for an instant that he may be wrong. One detects no namby-pamby equivocation in his pronouncements; no cunning escape clauses should Posterity prove him in error. For if Posterity should hold views contrary to Knocker's, Posterity is wrong. Alas, however, until the compilation of the present work he has been the Cinderella of history; nameless and neglected in the chronicles of Austral criticism.

I need hardly say that truncater of tall poppies is still amongst us; in fact he thrives. In London a couple of years ago I read two reviews of Patrick White's novels in two literary periodicals. Their common note of shrill bitchery arrested my attention and I discovered that their authors were not only failed writers; they were, moreover, Australian. Yet despite the fact that Mr White elects to live in Australia he is internationally respected, and therefore he is a tall poppy who needs must be cut down to size, and who better to wield the shears than his fellow-countrymen? Local blooms may be trimmed, *en passant*, as a sport, but our vigilance and come to rest in foreign vases beheading is too good for them.

Well-known Australians abroad fall into two distinct categories: Ambassadors and Expatriates. The former, which includes beauty queens, jockeys, opera singers, film stars and tennis players are immune from transhemispheric knockery, but the Expatriate is always fair game. Envied, but unenviable, he is certainly a traitor, and probably a writer, actor or a painter. He is unquestionably—to employ a favourite pejorative of the modern Australian knocker—'sold out'.

The explanation seems simple. The Press is a popular forum for the knocker, and journalists are more frequently failed authors than they are failed jockeys or sopranos. They are therefore more likely to excoriate against the success, or even the *promise* of fellow writers than they are to attack sportsmen and beauty queens who are, when it's all said and done, putting Australia on the map and doing a good uncontroversial patriotic job.

Our art 'critics' likewise are often disappointed painters and our drama 'critics' embittered small-town thespians. Their presence is encouraged by newspapers and their splenetic frenzies are deemed 'hard-hitting', 'controversial' and 'no-holds-barred'. Woe betide the wretched expatriate they decide to get stuck into. Those awesome clippings air-expressed by anxious friends expose a perfidy and disloyalty to his homeland directly proportionate to his international success. If he would silence the diminutive Cassandras of Witchetty Grub Street, he must needs fail, give up, or come home forever.

A separate volume might be compiled from the tirades and traducements, the backstabbing and snide knockery which pervade the Lilliputian enclaves of the Australian Film Industry. The film world, with its aura of glamour and quick riches, has always been attractive to frauds and persons with grave sexual and social inadequacies. Lured thither by a combination of cupidity and sloth, the denizens of Austral Filmdom are noted for their envy and rancour; calling down upon their competitors' heads the wrath of Trade Unions or the obloquy of partisan 'critics', throwing peevish pseudo-patriotic tantrums in the Press, and where their clerical talents outweigh their artistic gifts (which is nearly always), turning critic or administrator and sabotaging their rivals.

Comedians often let the side down by doing well overseas. Not long ago I presented in London the same stage show I had done in Australia. Someone read the reviews and cabled home a story. Back came the clipping pronto. HUMPHRIES RUBBISHES AUSTRALIA. I am still having to explain—most unconvincingly—to taxi-drivers and nice ladies in cigarette kiosks that I did not, in fact, spit and trample upon the Australian flag on stage. I had merely performed the same act they'd seen me do at the Comedy or the Royal. Yet was not this fact enough to convict me of crypto-knockery? To be funny about Australia at home and abroad were two very different things. I had been guilty of bad form and were forever after, and forgiven, the chagrined accusatory glances of cigarette ladies and taxi-drivers.

My friend, the actor and comedian Dick Bentley, copped the wrath of his homeland one day in 1956. The occasion was a BBC Australia Day broadcast in which the cream of Australian actors, singers, and composers participated. Keith Michell read 'The Play' by C. J. Dennis and Shirley Abicair sang folksongs. Bentley appeared in a two and a half minute spot in which an effete Mayfair couple dining in a restaurant were contrasted with an Australian couple in the same situation, i.e.:

MAYFAIR CHAP: Vin rouge, my sweet?<br>
AUSSIE: Plonk darl?<br>
etc.

The broadcast was well reviewed by London critics, but the well-known representative of refinement feared for our sensitive international 'image'. Anyway, front-page headlines the following day read: AUSSIE SLANG SHOCKS BRITAIN.

LONDON, Saturday.—Australians in England were shocked and English listeners bewildered by a slang-packed Australia Day broadcast over the B.B.C. last night.<br>
A team of Australian radio stars led by funny-man Dick Bentley interspersed the broadcast with jargon gleaned from Australian hotels, docks, back alleys and the out-back.<br>
The Australian High Commissioner in London, Sir Thomas White, said to-day: "I have always deplored that the B.B.C. have never asked us about the Australia Day broadcast.<br>
"I have put in more than one complaint that Australia does not get a fair show there.<br>
"There has been a lot of criticism in the past of their handling of Australian subjects."

Since 'funnyman' Dick Bentley was the best-known name in the show, all the criticism was levelled at him personally. Somebody probably reckoned: 'It's about time this mug was cut down to size!' The incident was immortalized by the late Alan Stranks, one-time Australian journalist and author.

THE DETRIMENTAL BLOKE

The *Herald's* got me snouted jest a treat—<br>
The *Sun* has rubbed me beezer in the shite—<br>
I've buggered up me only chance to meet<br>
Sir Thomas White.

I've done me bollix on the B.B.C.—<br>
I've let the Aussie side down with a thud—<br>
A shit-house rat's a better man than me<br>
Whose name is mud!

In all the Stately Homes of Surry Hills<br>
The Landed Gentry cry in shocked amaze<br>
'Let's boot the dirty bastard in the Pills!'<br>
Gawd spare me days!

They point with pride at Stiffy and at Mo<br>
The wattle wilts—even the Abos grunt:<br>
'Mine tinkit Bentley gibbit lousy show—<br>
The Dirty . . . . (What rhymes?)'

Ah—Fortune is a fickle flamin' jade—<br>
I'm not the first to take a bloody toss—<br>
I'll be remembered as the mug who made<br>
The Southern Cross!

But it's a knock or thyself be knocked existence Down Under; the trick is to get in first. Who knows? The ensuing volume may instruct us in a few good, old-fashioned techniques.

So put aside your newspaper with its sizzling hatchet-job on Joe Bighead by Joe Pinhead, stir that smouldering Patrick White novel on your hearth, take a nice long sip of ice-cold vitriol and stick another pin in your friendly fireside Max Harris effigy.

But *please* don't bother to rebuke Keith Dunstan for having documented our most endearing vice between the covers of this book. It won't sell.

BARRY HUMPHRIES<br>
Transylvania, June 1972
