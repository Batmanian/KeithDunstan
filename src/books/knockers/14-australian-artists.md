The painters are now the darlings of the Australian cultural scene. Artists in their second or third exhibitions are asking $400, $600, and $1,000 each for their paintings. A good artist, and often a bad artist, can reap from two pictures what an Australian author will earn from an 80,000 word book that took him a year or more to write.<sup>1</sup>

Even as late as the mid-1950s there were only half a dozen artists who could support themselves entirely from their art. Some very well-known painters were, literally, painters during their spare time. Their art consisted of painting houses, both outside and inside.

So they needed encouragement. James Smith began writing for the *Argus* in Melbourne in 1856 and was there for half a century. Alan McCulloch praises him in his *Encyclopaedia of Australian Art* for being the first to fight for a National Gallery of Victoria and for rescuing the work of both Louis Buvelot and Julian Ashton from oblivion. But he is also remembered for the most famous piece of art criticism written in Australia, the 9 x 5 Impressionist Exhibition of 1889.

Smith was notorious for his contempt of the Impressionists, yet the artists who exhibited at the 9 x 5 Exhibition were among the greatest Australia ever produced — Tom Roberts, Charles Conder and Arthur Streeton.

Here is the complete review, unsigned, as it appeared in the *Argus* on Saturday, 17 August 1889.

<h2 id="an-impressionist-exhibition">AN IMPRESSIONIST EXHIBITION</h2>

Such an exhibition of impressionist memoranda as will be open today at Buxton's Art Gallery, by Messrs. Roberts, Conder, Streeton, and others, failed to justify itself. It has no adequate *raison d'être*. It is as if a dramatist should give a performance on the stage of such scraps of dialogue, hints of character, ideas for incident, and suggestions of situations as had occurred to him while pondering over the construction of a play, or as if a musician should invite people to listen to crude and disconnected scraps of composition, containing the vaguest connected themes for a cantata, a symphony, or an opera, or as if a sculptor should ask us to inspect certain masses of marble from which he had blocked out the amorphous outlines of various pieces of statuary. None of these is to be regarded as a work of art. Neither is a painter's 'impression'. It is simply a record in colour of some fugitive effect which he sees, or professes to see, in nature. But, like primeval chaos, 'it is without form and void'. To the executant it seems spontaneous and forcible. To the spectator it appears grotesque and meaningless.

The earliest impressionist of whom we have any knowledge was Piero di Cosimo, and he had a 'bee in his bonnet'. Vasari tells us that 'he would sometimes stand beside a wall against which various impurities were cast, and from these he would image forth most singular scenes, combats of horses, strangely ordered cities, and the most extraordinary landscapes that ever were seen'. The modern impressionist wants us to see pictures in splashes of colour, in slap-dash brush-work, and in sleight-of-hand methods of execution leading to the proposition of pictorial conundrums, which would baffle solution if there were no label or catalogue.

In an exhibition of paintings you naturally look for pictures; instead of which the Impressionist presents you with a varied assortment of palettes. Of the 180 exhibits catalogued on the present occasion, something like four-fifths are a pain to the eye. Some of them look like faded pictures seen through several mediums of thick gauze; others suggest that a paint-pot has been accidentally upset over a panel nine inches by five; others resemble the first essays of a small boy, who has just been apprenticed to house-paint; whilst not a few are as distressing as the incoherent images which float through the brain of a dyspeptic dreamer.

Here is an impression, for example, entitled 'A Harrowing', which is quite true to its title. It represents a long-legged girl in company with two terribly deformed horses — 'creatures of the slime'? — struggling to get away from a field like those scarecrows which English farmers set up in cornfields when the grain is ripening, and dislocating their legs — uncertain — in the process. And here is a mystery called 'The Milky Way', consisting of two or three spots of pale, saltry colour on what appears to be a sheet of smoked brown paper. Here, again, is something bearing the title of 'Orange, Blue and White', representing a hydrocephalus doll with crooked, wooden hands and a broken nose, almost buried in white lead. And there are landscapes and samples of architecture, belonging to some embryotic world of art, of which, as Pandulph says, 'All form is formless, orderless, shapeless — nature is either unshapen or misshapen.'

Some of the work, however, put to entitle these sketches, show that the artists are capable of much better things. Such is the clever 'Looking over to Williamstown' of Mr R. E. Falls; the 'Fisherman's Bend in Richmond Paddock', and 'Australian Bush — Good-bye', and 'Going Down to the Treasury', 'Early and Damp', 'The Dear Lady Disdain' and 'Myosotis', of Mr Tom Roberts; and the plastic sketches of Mr C. D. Richardson. These and a few others afford something agreeable for the eye to rest upon; but the exhibition as a whole, would leave a very painful feeling behind it, and cause one to despond with respect to the future of art in this colony. We do not believe with Mr W. P. Frith, R.A., that 'impressionism is a craze of such ephemeral character as to be unworthy of serious attention'.<sup>2</sup>

The Impressionists fought back bravely. They pasted Mr Smith's critique on a board and hung it outside Buxton's Gallery in Swanston Street. This inspired so many people to pay the sixpence fee to go in, and so society ladies would say to each other: 'Have you seen the Impressionists' exhibition? Do go, it's so funny.' They had one champion, Mr Edward A. Vidler of *Table Talk*.

He gave the Impressionists vast space. He said the Impressionists were a much abused group and a number of Victorian artists were getting 'coldly aloof'. Particularly he liked Arthur Streeton. He also had James Smith almost as much abused. He was only twenty-two, a protégé of Fred McCubbin, and an artist who had a remarkable future, according to Vidler. He said there had never been an exhibition like it in Victoria.

Mr Culla Hill very kindly supplied the furnishings at Buxton's. He put drapings of soft Liberty silk 'of many delicate colours' on the walls, so that they were knotted and drawn tastefully around the paintings. He also had Japanese umbrellas, screens, and handsome *jardinières* to complete the scene. There was a piano for recitals on Wednesdays.

He described in full the opening for the Press. Mr James Smith was the *Argus* critic, a man of immense erudition, a friend of both Dickens and Thackeray, stepped quietly on, taking notes. His opening salutation to Mr Tom Roberts was, 'More eccentricities.' Mr Alex Gustafson, the well-known temperance advocate and social reformer, was there. According to Vidler: 'He took a lively pleasure in sneering at anything and everything, remarking that if his little boy of five could not do better than that he would hang him.'

As for the refreshments: 'The afternoon tea was evidently not on its best behaviour and was referred to rather slightingly as non-impressionist "tea", to be taken without milk if an impression were desirable. One of two decanters of amber-coloured liquid, which may have been tea in its undiluted state, found favour with the male literary element.'<sup>3</sup>

Mr Vidler said Messrs Roberts, Conder and Streeton had to call upon all their philosophy to bear the abuse which they received. Yet most of the pictures sold: Streeton's and Conder's went for one to five guineas and Tom Roberts' paintings were available for one to nine guineas.

In 1906 Dattilo Rubbo poured out his troubles to the Red Page in the *Bulletin*. Rubbo was a Sydney artist, Italian-born. His son was named after that town, Dattilo Rubbo, was to become Professor of Biology at the University of Melbourne. He told the *Bulletin*:

In Italy there are thousands and thousands coming to buy your picture. The King of Italy buys for 30,000 or 40,000 francs. At every exhibition he is asked to open. Everybody who is asked to open an exhibition buys a picture; it is a great compliment to them. But here, if you invite anyone they are paying you a compliment. The Governors do not buy — if they do I have never heard of it. And when you go to an exhibition you hear them say:

'Look! Look! There is the man who painted that picture!'

'Where?'

'Oh, THAT fellow.'

That's all you get for your trouble. Art is not prized here as it should be. . . . The rich man never comes to buy the pictures. We are put down in Australian art that here we are a lot of frauds. What have they got in their houses the rich? I know they go to the Continent and buy refuse . . . and Australians are good for nothing.

You find many people here who say, 'Oh, we love art very much'. I hear them — Pooh! Talk! Talk! In Italy it is genuine. Here it is sham.<sup>4</sup>

Rubbo said that in Italy money flooded into art but in Sydney there was just the £30 Wynne prize for landscape and sculpture. That was the only prize for landscape and sculpture, year after year after year.

If Streeton, Conder and Roberts needed to be philosophical in 1889, here are some more reviews which must have greatly assisted the artists in their philosophical powers.

**1902. Bernard Hall, J. H. Scheltema, Walter Withers, Hal Waugh.**

Bernard Hall is a capable painter with really excellent technique but paints only still-life studies, tea-cups, vases etc., nude figures sitting on wicker chairs, nymphs in impossible meadows. His work reeks of the French studio and has no intimate charm. He never paints Australia.

J. H. Scheltema gives us a 'Landscape with Cattle'. It is probably meant to be Australia but it is purely German in feeling.

Walter Withers too rarely paints Australia. All his pictures are sweetened with English sentiment, suggestive of the happy homestead nestling among the trees, the plough plodding his weary way, and the old rustic bridge by the mill.

Hal Waugh suggests the real Australia in his large canvas of 'The Drought', and deserves commendation for good intentions, but his picture of countless dead sheep and bony horses on the parched plain has hardly sufficient poetic insight to transmute such material into a work of art.<sup>5</sup>

**1911. Mr Rupert Bunny.**

The painting of 'A Summer Morning' which N.S.W. Gallery Trustees, on second thought, have bought for 400 guineas, emphasises Mr Bunny's defects; the figures lose their inferior place, and, endeavouring to put on some semblance of humanity, claim attention as subjects more or less endowed with life.

A seated lady on the right is contemplating with a languid sneer a lapful of kittens. A standing lady, on the left, on close inspection is discovered to have a right arm in which she holds a bowl. Like the first lady, she is in the first stage of catalepsy. A maid-servant behind and in the centre, so badly placed that she looks like the spirit-photograph of a maid-servant, has been struck rigid in the act of pouring milk from a jug, with the result that she is pouring, not in the bowl but carefully outside it. All the figures, including the kittens, look precisely like figures excavated at Herculaneum — slain in the attitudes of life by an overwhelming shower of ashes.<sup>6</sup>

**1914. Mr George Lambert.**

In the *Sydney Morning Herald* a correspondent presses the claim of 'The Australian Velasquez'. Yes, we have the 'The Australian Velasquez' and 'The Australian Leonardo'. We had the Australian Whistler also; but him we have lost — poor chap! — unfortunately he couldn't whistle. His omen casts its baleful shadow over 'The Australian Velasquez' and 'The Australian Leonardo'.

'The Australian Velasquez' is Mr G. Lambert — who singularly enough isn't an Australian. Mr Lambert was brought to this country as a boy, and taken to the backblocks. Told to 'Copy that cow,' he copied that cow. Nothing distracted him from copying that cow. He copied that cow in the morning; he copied that cow in the afternoon; next day and next week he copied that cow.

He added other objects to the cow; he added the pen to the pencil, the brush to the pen; and when at last he came to Sydney, the Society of Artists annexed him — and taught him how to draw!

There are obvious reasons why the Society of Artists could not teach Mr Lambert how to draw; but it tried to teach him how to paint. He learned to paint so badly that the first thing he did when he won the Government scholarship and went to Europe was to alter his style of painting completely.

Look at his paintings in N.S.W. Gallery and see how cold, how timid, how insignificant they seem in colour! An oil-painter who is no colourist certainly is not 'The Australian Velasquez'.<sup>7</sup>

**1913. Mr Hans Heysen.**

Mr Hans Heysen of Adelaide, apparently would-be poet without the poetic nature. We judge Mr Heysen for a literal landscape painter who is essentially of the Lister type and who has understood that he must flee the Lister method at any cost. Last year he fled at the cost of Claude. This year he flees at the expense of Turner. He has tried to make bits of Australian bush and sky passionate with colour. He has made them sentimental with conscious colour effects. By our halidom, we would sooner have Mr Lister's crudity than Mr Heysen's sentimentality. Mr Lister at least is absolutely honest; he does not disguise his artistic nakedness by one thread of a borrowed garment. Mr Heysen has covered himself with a splashed robe that clearly does not belong to him and does not fit him. He is driving his nail in the wrong direction; apparently he is trying to drive it further than it will go. His earlier work was not very good, but it was better than this pretentious falsity. Let him end artifice and paint what he sees truthfully to the height of his vision.<sup>8</sup>

**1913. Mr W. L. Lister.**

Of invention, imagination, emotion, most of the work holds not the faintest trace. Dullness and incompetence are the keynotes of the exhibition. (N.S.W. Artists' Society.)

The worst offender, measuring his offence by its area as well as by its guilt, is the President of the Society, Mr W. L. Lister. He exhibits a view of gum trees and a coloured landscape which has the effect of a section cut out of a coloured plan. The trees are carefully painted, but they have no more tree feeling than if they were firewood. The design is as painfully literal, as obtrusively barren, as if the scene had been photographed. There is no beauty in the foreground, no mystery in the distance; the thing is a map, plus those faithful and unhappy gum trees. No person with artistic intelligence could conceive this topographical work as a piece of fine art. N.S.W. Art Society performs artistic suicide by exhibiting the monster.<sup>9</sup>

**1917. Mr Harold Herbert.**

While Press and politicians were daily urging upon us the necessity of rigid economy, Mr Harold Herbert, a young Victorian artist, journeyed from Ballarat to Melbourne with a large consignment of pictures and proceeded to give an exhibition of wartime extravagance at the rooms of the Fine Arts Society. Mr Herbert is a young man who uses up water colours by the gallon, regardless of the consequences. In this exhibition of 114 panels the results of his gay adventures in his chosen medium were often quite pleasing, but the general impression left on one after emerging from the riot of colours, is that the artist would do better if he were to lessen his production and aim at a higher standard.<sup>10</sup>

**1919. Mr William Frater.**

Mr William Frater, a gloomy enemy of light who reports the weather with a stain on its character. His ten canvases had neither commercial nor artistic value. If there is any truth in the Hindu theory of reincarnation, Mr Frater should return to earth some day in the form of a white elephant.<sup>11</sup>

**1920. Mr Tom Roberts.**

Mr Tom Roberts has misused paint to the distress of his fellow creatures in some apparently recent studies of the Sassafras country wherein he cannot repress the virulent greens of the fern fronds and sentinel sameness of tall straight guns. The Sassafras canvases, like 'The Master' and a flat formal view of Circular Quay, might have been painted to the order of a retired ironmonger who wanted something to silence his wallpaper.

For his couple of nudes 'The Bather' and 'The Woodland Bath', one has no particular affection. Both are a night-school education in academic stodginess. Barring an occasional contour that would intrigue a cheese merchant, one would say they were hardly worth undressing.

'Shearing the Rams', unkindly lent by Edward Trenchard Esq., to remind us that the artist has a past to condone. Surely the canvas is now something that the artist would wish to forget. Sentiment apart, it is a portentous futility. Personally, when looking at the mild, chubby-faced shearers, one finds it difficult to tell which are really the sheep.<sup>12</sup>

**1924. Mr Arthur Streeton.**

An exhibition of the work of Mr A. Streeton is to be opened by Dame Nellie Melba at 100 Exhibition Street today at 3 o'clock. The exhibition contains some new work and some old achievements that we have seen many times. There are numerous studies of flowers and fans, and one portrait titled 'The Violinist', all done in the brilliant and slick method usual to this painter. Taking the exhibition as a whole, there is no sign of the realisation of the promise made by the artist when he painted 'Purple Moon' and 'The Hawkesbury River'.

At this exhibition Arthur Streeton's landscape 'Golden Summer' sold for 1,000 guineas, which was a record price for an Australian painting. Others went for 250 guineas and 200 guineas.<sup>13</sup>

<h2 id="norman-lindsay">Norman Lindsay</h2>

The artist who suffered most critical displeasure at this time was Norman Lindsay. On 24 July the South Australian Society of Arts put on a special exhibition. The Society planned it as a grand affair and artists from all over Australia were invited to exhibit. Mr Sydney Ure Smith, president of the New South Wales Society of Artists, was invited to Adelaide to perform the opening.

The sensation came a week before. The South Australian Society rejected three Norman Lindsay etchings — 'The Funeral March of Don Juan', 'The Apex of Life' and 'The Festival'. The president, Mr John White, said the committee recognized the excellence of Mr Lindsay's work, the high standard of his technique and his remarkable talent, but the etchings had been rejected on account of the subject matter.

*The Advertiser* quoted an anonymous member of the committee who was a little more explicit:

No lover of art objects to nude figures tightly treated, but we could not allow the showing of pictures of this kind, because of the immoral and sensual atmosphere which would be provided by them. The gestures of men and women in the pictures would be offensive to many, and in regard to one picture it might be said that it represented a wild licentious riot of the worst pagan times.<sup>14</sup>

This caused an uproar. Mr Sydney Ure Smith said he read the announcement with astonishment. The Adelaide committee was adopting the attitude of the uncomprehending prude, and he threatened to refuse to open the exhibition.<sup>15</sup>

Newspapers asked distinguished people for comment. Dame Nellie Melba said her indignation was so great she couldn't put her feelings into words.<sup>16</sup> Percy Grainger, the composer, said: 'Personally I regard him as one of the greatest living artists of all time. He is the equal of Titian or Velasquez. Australia will have to get away from the hidebound British traditions of decorum.'<sup>17</sup>

Mr H. E. Fuller, honorary secretary of the Society, said they were entirely in the hands of council rules. He said: 'After the painters and etchers exhibition early in 1922, at which were several of Mr Lindsay's etchings, my council passed a resolution that in future nothing which in the opinion of council was offensive to good taste would be exhibited in our rooms. This was confirmed by the Board of Governors who are our landlords, and the resolution has been observed in all our exhibitions ever since.'<sup>18</sup>

Norman Lindsay was furious. He sent a telegram to John Preece, a gallery director, his representative in Adelaide:

WITHDRAW ALL PICTURES OF MINE FROM EXHIBITION. ON NO ACCOUNT WILL I PERMIT THEM TO BE EXHIBITED. ACCEPT THIS AS FINAL. PLEASE WIRE WHEN WITHDRAWN.<sup>19</sup>

Actually Lindsay had eleven pictures in the exhibition but only three had been rejected. After the Lindsay telegram they all came out.

There was much shaking of heads and talking all over Adelaide. The Reverend E. S. Kiek, Principal of Parkin College, Adelaide, commented that Mr Lindsay had a gift which amounted to genius but it was a wonder to him that the artist delighted to portray what to most men appeared to be the depths of debased sensuality.<sup>20</sup>

Lindsay did not stop with the mere sending of a telegram. He hired John Preece's gallery and arranged for all his pictures to be shown there, opening at the same time as the Society of Artists exhibition. After such publicity he could not fail. Naturally everybody came to see personally the depravity of the Lindsay etchings. He did even better than the main exhibition. An *Advertiser* reporter said the three banned etchings on display at Preece's Gallery were the centre of attraction surrounded by crowds of people. The crowd was so thick it was difficult to get a decent view of them all afternoon.<sup>21</sup>

After threatening that he would refuse to, Sydney Ure Smith still arrived for his formal opening of the Society exhibition. The night before he spoke at a United Arts Club.

He said it was extraordinary to him that the pictures had been rejected, pictures that caused not the slightest offence in Sydney. But he remembered once a gentleman had said to him:

'It would have him "ung, drawn and quartered. It's not so much what 'e does as the awful idea 'e puts into your 'ead. Nudes and all those things. Bakanals I think 'e calls 'em. It would be much nicer to keep to landscrapes. I would not mind a few horses and cows; that and lillies or a boat floatin' down the river. Why don't Norman Lindsay behave like a gentleman?'

In 1925 he was described as being 'one of the most discussed men in Australia'. After an exhibition in March at Swain's Art Gallery in Sydney, *The Triad* launched an attack titled 'Nude and Crude'. Thousands went along to see the exhibition. *The Triad* said:

Most of them came away somewhat sickened from contact with the grossness which they could not avoid, and quite indifferent to the talent which they could not comprehend. Whatever may be said of the morality of Lindsay's drawings it must be admitted that his nudes would not arouse sensual desire in the most degraded mind. . . . The opposition to Lindsay's works both in Sydney and Adelaide, while often that of moral bigotry, was occasionally based on the firmer ground that his nudes were a gross travesty of reality and were incapable of arousing either reverence or delight.

His versatility is astonishing, but the nudes are the same old nudes of his callow youth, a little sentimentalized, maybe, a spot or two changed. In twenty-five years there has been no highly appreciable development of skill or alteration in attitude; and it is now too late to hope for any; for his vision has become fixed with the passing of the years.<sup>22</sup>

Of a Melbourne exhibition, the Herald critic J. S. MacDonald said Lindsay was most displeasing. 'Granting that such terrific limbs exist, why depict them?'

He was rarely out of trouble. In 1928 he had an exhibition in London. R. Gillett, a Londoner, inadvertently, was published in the newspapers. In this letter he described Australia as, on the whole, hardly above the mental level of the backwoods of America. What's more it was a relief to him, after years of extremely depressing Australian antagonism, to be able to send work out of Australia.<sup>23</sup>

This time J. S. MacDonald commented:

Apart from the poor phrasing of this strip of rancour, for one would surmise, from a bulky bolt of tightly-rolled resentment, its general tone does its author small credit. In the first place, where is there evidence of 'years of an extremely depressing struggle with its antagonism?'

Lindsay's reply in a letter to the *Herald* was even more rich and eloquent:

That letter was written at a time when Sydney and Melbourne newspapers were saying a lot of unpleasant things about me. The general public, through their columns, was very busy to the same effect. My brother artists, when appealed to for an opinion, cordially endorsed the general attack.

What can be said for a country as rich as this that refuses to support a national orchestra? — a country that owns one great poet and does not read him? — a country that is notorious as a dumping-ground for third-rate English and American novels? — a country that does not possess one novelist, and, if it did, does not possess a publisher who would publish him? — a country that has not produced a single musician or even a single inventor of note?<sup>24</sup>

Sir Frank Clarke, Bt., President of the Legislative Council, was upset over Lindsay's remarks: 'If I get restive about anything it never occurs to me to curse and wash my hands of it. My only thought is that probably I am wrong. . . . It is regrettable of anyone to speak in that way of the country that has made him.'<sup>25</sup>

There was worse to come. In 1931 the sedate firm of Art in Australia Ltd. was charged with having sold an obscene publication. Art in Australia published their special Lindsay issue in December 1930 and it had been on sale for more than six months before the police moved in and seized all the plates and all available issues from their offices in Sydney.

Looking at that issue now it seems hard to believe how it could ever have been banned, although one newspaper did say that the Lindsay drawings were 'highly suggestive'.<sup>26</sup> Perhaps the most suggestive drawing was one of a gentleman, rather nervously putting his hand down a lady's well-filled bosom. Worst of all the lady did not appear to mind.

All this was too much for Lindsay. His novel *Redheap*, although a best-seller in the United States, had been banned by the Australian censors. He said: 'I am sick and tired of this decidedly wowseristic country.'<sup>27</sup>

It was interesting that the very issue of *Art in Australia* confiscated by the police contained an article by Kenneth Slessor which quoted some pertinent Lindsay remarks:

The only thing disclosed to me over the *Red Heap* business is the astonishing discovery that Australian officialdom is still the pure product of the convict system. A policeman's baton to settle all questions. The departments confer. Quite arbitrary — one medieval baron to another. There is something extremely disgusting in having one's work pawed by policemen.

It is impossible today for any work to get a hearing unless it is attacked; a curious evidence of the use of the hate stimulus to arouse mental torpor. We Australians can take it as understood that everything we produce will be attacked — it is known as Australian and attacked accordingly.<sup>28</sup>

The case against *Art in Australia* eventually was dismissed and the plates returned, but Lindsay announced he was fed up with Australia and he was leaving. 'I don't think there is any hope for art while policemen and minor officials are allowed to dictate to the public what the culture of the people shall be.' He and his wife sailed for America. The Melbourne *Herald* ran a cold little editorial titled 'A GENIUS DEPARTS'.

Whether he will ever create again is doubtful. Probably he will, temporarily annoyed with his native land, he cherishes for it a deep affection. The Australian sun will still shine after he has gone, creating dazzling lights and colours for young artists who are safe from his weird life philosophy. In America his unfortunate novel *Redheap* is said to be a best-seller. If Lindsay intends to produce other stories of the same kind, it is to be hoped that he will rather fancy the work rather than the way it is received in his adopted home.

In the New South Wales Legislative Assembly a Labor MP, Mr C. E. Martin, accused the Nationalist Government of driving Lindsay out of the country. He returned home again in August 1932, slightly chastened. 'I'll admit I left Australia in a very bad temper, but most of the things I said were for Australia's own good. Anyhow I got homesick and here I am back again, convinced that Australia is the world's best country — narrow-minded censors and blundering officials thrown in.'<sup>29</sup>

But Lindsay was to suffer from narrow-minded censors almost to the end of his long life. Even as late as 1966 Melbourne Vice Squad police called at the Southern Cross Galleries over an allegedly obscene Lindsay painting. It was 'Bacchanalian Festival', a vintage Norman Lindsay, filled with big, dairy-country girls and boys having a jolly good time. The publicity was splendid, and the picture was sold for $700 to a man who ran a hotel restaurant at Bentleigh.

<h2 id="menzies-and-modern-art">Menzies and Modern Art</h2>

The artists had to fight a perpetual battle because those in charge of running the country had little interest in or understanding of the arts. In 1937 Robert Gordon Menzies K.C., the dismissed Federal Attorney-General, had been to London and drawn up a plan for an Australian Academy of Art to become a group of select painters, similar to Britain's Royal Academy. So Mr Menzies was a Government spokesman on the subject and he was even prepared to give his opinions about it. He offered at the Fine Arts Society exhibition and once again told the world his apt for modern art: 'Great art speaks a language which every intelligent person can understand. The people who call themselves modernists today speak a different language.' He found nothing but absurdity in this so-called modern art — its evasion of real problems and its cross-eyed drawings.<sup>30</sup>

That was a declaration of war. Vitally and loftily Max Meldrum said it had been generally recognized over a long period for years of practising painting that certain men in Australia are competent painters. Most of them are banded together into the Academy. 'I hardly think they are going to be alarmed by yet indeed aware of what the members of the Contemporary Art Society are; they simply don't seem important enough to bother with.' He pointed out that their art had an extraordinary semblance to the aimless paintings splashed on the walls by little children or those of people 30,000 years ago.<sup>31</sup>

Of all people called upon to write these reviews for the Contemporary Art Society's first exhibition, it chose: Hector MacQuarrie (Herbert), inaugural chairman of the Academy. He rose to the occasion:

There is no popular art in these things. Why doesn't someone tell me? I am lost or have never seen light as far as this exhibition is concerned. So the work is derivative; it lacks vital interest. The painting that I possibly could admire to, Mr Herbert, adopts the crudities of his predecessors, but fails well: A gallery Norman MacGeorge, well out of his element in this spree, is still so much under the influence of green vegetables, faces with no faces, groups of still-life trees to adopt. 'A sympathetic guardian would advise me to find out into the open air.'<sup>32</sup>

Norman MacGeorge, one of those at this exhibition, was a lecturer in art at the National Gallery. He claimed that the Melbourne National Gallery lagged behind every important gallery in the world in its almost complete indifference to Modern art developments.<sup>33</sup>

<h2 id="the-dobell-case">The Dobell Case</h2>

The director of the National Gallery was J. S. MacDonald, the former *Herald* critic, a man opposed to modern art in all its forms. In December 1939 there was a touring exhibition of paintings from Europe. The Contemporary Art Society recommended that the National Gallery should buy nine of them for its collection. One of them was a Van Gogh priced at £1,750 sterling. MacDonald made a formal statement:

Confused hobgoblin stuff, a nightmare . . . recognized by psychiatrists as emanating from disordered minds . . . paintings that simply reflect the artist's degeneracy.

I criticised in my recent report the result of alliance between art dealers and art writers who had been intent on making these artists a nine-day wonder on account of their eccentricities. . . . Their decadence was advertised and their weaknesses and vices were upheld as being good reasons why their paintings should be an attraction. I disagreed with this utterly — why have the madnesses, disease and bad behaviour of Modigliani, Utrillo, Van Gogh, Gauguin and the like, been used to advertise their works?<sup>34</sup>

MacDonald then proceeded to pass comment on pictures that had been proposed for purchase:

Georges Braque. 'Le Table de Marbre': 'A puzzle in black and white — dirty white.'

Pablo Picasso. 'La Petite Cuisine': 'Another jumble of lines and colour confusing to the normal person. It has no interest in the minds of people who report things through their eyes.'

Van Gogh. 'Le Mont Gaussier': 'Slack, wavy atmosphere, mass of ribbon-like corrugations. Its got no atmosphere of tenderness.'

Vlaminck. 'Paysage': 'Deliberately theatrically. Spooky as if painted by a troll.'

Utrillo. 'Caserne de Courbevoie': 'No recession. No proper drawing.'

Jules Pascin. 'Jeune Fille': 'Workmanship vague and weak, smudgy, dirty, lacking in substance, drawing lacking volume.'<sup>35</sup>

Correspondence in the newspapers ran warm again. Albert Tucker was nonplussed as to why MacDonald had picked on the morals of the artists. He said:

Anyone with an elementary knowledge of art history would know that Rembrandt, Jan Steen and Franz Hals were immoral drunkards, Goya a drunken brawler and Cellini all of these and a murderer as well. These shortcomings didn't hinder them in the production of their masterpieces.

However MacDonald had some strong allies. Mrs A. S. Rogers of Hawthorn said the paintings were an insult to one's intelligence and as far as the nude paintings were concerned they were 'an outrage to decent women'.<sup>36</sup>

Over the next few days there was column after column in praise of the art director. Theo B. Hansen thought the Van Gogh would not have fetched £20 let alone £1,750 had it been painted in Australia. And William Beresford said: 'At last a real authority has the courage to say what so many people really know about modern art.'<sup>37</sup>

The really climactic battle in the traditionalists versus the moderns was the William Dobell case. This was, perhaps, the first time that art became a lively matter for public discussion right across the nation. In January 1944 Dobell was forty-four years old. He had spent ten years teaching and painting in England. During the war he painted camouflage in the Civil Construction Corps. He returned from Europe to teach art at the Sydney Technical College. He was well enough known in the art world, but the rest of Australia had not heard of him. In January of that year the Trustees of the Art Gallery of New South Wales awarded him the £500 Archibald Prize for portraiture.

Dobell had painted a fellow-artist and Archibald Prize entrant, Joshua Smith. It was unlike any portrait Australians had ever seen. They were used to staid, stiff, photographic likenesses. People seeing it in King's Hall at Parliament House gasped — it was Joshua Smith! He looked skeleton-starved, ailing, long, face thin, gaunt and overall a strange pallid long-stemness. Yet the effect was that of a character, a spirit of life somehow, the picture revealed the artist within the rather ugly shambles of the body.

The immediate comment was immediate and loud.

Max Meldrum said: 'Everybody I have met is most indignant.' Archibald did not know of modern art and such an award was not in accord with his intentions. Charles Wheeler said he was startled by the picture and did not think Archibald would have approved either. Miss Mary Edwards described the judgement of the NSW Trustees as 'an artistic Pearl Harbor' and there were meetings of artists both in Sydney and Melbourne.

The main supporter of Dobell was the *Sydney Morning Herald*. On 2 February it said in a leading article:

That this has come as a surprise and even a shock to large numbers of the art-loving public is an unfortunate fact for which the Trustees have largely themselves to thank. As a body they have done little to teach the public that the modern or contemporary scene has merit or must be taken seriously or to give adequate representation on the Gallery walls.

All the public money, it said, had gone on buying literally dozens and dozens of the same old paintings from the Australian school.

Two other Archibald Prize entrants, Mary Edwards and Wolinski, took out a writ in the Supreme Court of New South Wales, claiming that the Trustees' decision be set aside: 'It is claimed that the said picture is not a portrait but a caricature of Joshua Smith, having features and attributes similar in some ways to him but having features and distorted and exaggerated. . . . That the said picture does not represent any attempt on the part of the defendant to make a likeness of Smith.'

James Gleeson in his book *William Dobell* described the two plaintiff witnesses: Mary Edwards — 'Specialized in highly coloured, reaching towards red, romanticized Sea Islanders, and in portraits with an air of high sugar content.' And Wolinski — 'A rigidly academic rule follower.'

The case went before the court in October and the first witness called by the defendants, Mr J. S. MacDonald. Mr Garfield Barwick K.C., appeared for the defendants and these were some of the questions and answers:

Mr Barwick: You say it is not in your opinion a portrait of Mr Joshua Smith; are you able to tell us your specific reason or reasons for saying so?

Mr MacDonald: Yes. It is the spirit in which the thing is painted. If I had never seen Mr Smith before, and someone said to me 'Here is a picture of a portrait painter', I would say 'No, that man could not paint a portrait.' I know Joshua Smith can paint a portrait because I have handled his portraits. He sent in to the Archibald Prize when I was at the National Gallery. I know his work. That is a poor creature.

Mr Barwick: Is there any other reason you desire to give for thinking this is not a portrait of this man?

Mr MacDonald: It looks like an elf-person, a person sick in body and brain. Not a man who could paint a portrait fit for entry into the Archibald Prize.

Under cross-examination from Mr Kitto, K.C., Mr MacDonald went even further: 'It looks to me as if he had been cornered by the New York police and beaten with a piece of lead pipe for not coming clean.'

MacDonald admitted that he had already described the portrait as suitable to qualify for a freak exhibit in a circus. At this stage he had been basing his judgement only on newspaper pictures.

Mr Kitto: Do you think it was fair to publish this unrestrained and trenchant criticism when you hadn't seen the picture?

Mr MacDonald: Yes, quite.

Mr Kitto: You have told the court that photographs frequently misrepresent and yet you still think it was fair to criticize on this basis?

Mr MacDonald: Yes.

MacDonald denied that he had been a constant opponent of Modern art. But Mr Kitto put to him another question: 'Have you ever recommended to the trustees of a gallery a picture which you regarded as modernism?'

MacDonald replied: 'No.'

The chief witness for the plaintiffs was John Young, a former Director of the National Gallery of New South Wales. Commenting on the portrait, he said: 'I think I have seen something like that. I remember seeing in a textbook of Psychiatry some pictures of some poor sub-human creatures — who had been allowed to live and maintain one posture — and they developed very strange proportions. But they were sub-human.'

Another witness, Dr Vivian Benjafield, said the picture looked like 'the body of a man who had died in that position and had remained in that position for a period of some months and had dried up.'

When Dobell himself came to the box, he was interesting. He said he liked to know a sitter at least a month before he made his studies for a portrait. He knew Joshua Smith for four or five years as a fellow camouflage labourer in the Civil Construction Corps. He was a very determined person. He would sit erect in his chair and say, 'I will go to Mr Curtin if necessary' to get his point. He tried to reproduce this feeling in the picture.

This was a portion of the cross-examination:

Mr Barwick: You say that the elongation of the arms is also part of the design?

Mr Dobell: From the elongation of the arms you will notice that I have tightened the sleeves, so you could get a tailor there to criticize that too. But I tightened the sleeves to give a further impression of the elongation of those arms. You will notice that the arms are not unduly long; it is the tightening of the sleeves that gives that effect.

Mr Barwick: But if you painted him as he actually is, he was thin enough to see that he was thin without exaggeration, wasn't he?

Mr Dobell: Well, you may have noticed that some very thin people wear very loosely made clothes to hide that effect, and loose, wide trousers.

Mr Barwick: And you have him with loose, wide trousers?

Mr Dobell: Yes, and that is another thing. With those loose, wide trousers one leg hides the other leg, and I did not paint that because I did not see it, any more than I did not paint the hairs on his chest.

Mr Barwick: Any elongation that took place in the tightening of the sleeves had to do with design, as distinct from the portrayal of character in the picture?

Mr Dobell: It is difficult in two minutes to give lessons on design.

Mr Barwick: Is the shape of head, objectively faithful?

Mr Dobell: Yes, I do.

Mr Barwick: The nose?

Mr Dobell: Yes.

Mr Barwick: The length of the neck?

Mr Dobell: That I will admit is elongated; the whole thing, you are taking it bit by bit and I am taking it as a picture. I might just as well criticize the conduct of your case by the angle of your wig, as for you to take individual things like that.

So it went on. The case lasted two days and the Court upheld the decision of the Gallery trustees, which meant total victory for the moderns. But the affair had a disastrous effect on Dobell. He was a shy man and always terrified of publicity. He was frightened to go out of his house for fear people were staring at him. As he said, people would come down to his house at Wangi, stand outside and stare, trying to get a peep at the mad artist. In 1948 *Smith's Weekly* published an article in which Dobell said: 'For about two years I have been a sick man. My physical and nervous condition made it impossible for me to settle down and paint. I had no alternative but to retire to a quiet place and rest. My confidence flopped and I had a long bout of despondency.'<sup>38</sup>

Dobell, although his pictures were to sell later for $20,000 or more, never got over his shyness. Not until I visited the exhibition of pictures in the Town Hall did I realize how passionately I now know what a dead landlord looks like. I have always got the Dobell picture I had eyes for nothing else's great work.

In 1955 he again won the Archibald Prize, with a portrait of Margaret Olley, an artist. The newspapers ran a series of comments. Mr Joseph Wolinski (a plaintiff in the Dobell case): 'All I say is that is a Dobell. I have two portraits in the competition, which I consider to be the best I've done.'

Dr V. Benjafield (anti-Dobell witness in the 1944 case): 'Scant painting and I don't intend to. It reminds me of a woman who is sick — a woman with kidney trouble.'

Mr E. J. Kavanagh, after having gazed at the picture for fifteen minutes: 'I don't know anything about art, but I always understood a portrait was a likeness of someone. If Dobell had painted a picture of my girl friend like that I'd hang it face to the wall in the washhouse.'<sup>39</sup>

In 1960 he painted the Prime Minister, Mr Menzies, for *Time* magazine. The portrait went on the cover and once again there was the same old flow of comment. The Labor Party made a great thing of it. Mr L. E. Haylen: 'What a piece of diluted acetic acid. It should be a must in every home.' Mr F. Crean: 'It captures a lot of the Menzies superciliousness.' Mr D. J. Curtin: 'A perfect picture of Mr Menzies as the Labor Party sees him.' Mr Arthur Calwell: 'Most unfair. He never looked like this no matter how annoyed he might have felt at the criticisms levelled at him at different times by Labor members. Believe it or not, Mr Menzies is much more handsome than Mr Dobell has painted him.'

Lord Casey said: 'Good Lord. How extraordinary.'<sup>40</sup> As for the Prime Minister himself, he declined to comment, and the chill was distinctly noticeable. Yet late in life Dobell did receive recognition from his own country, to become Sir William Dobell.

<h2 id="drysdale-nolan-blackman">Drysdale, Nolan and Blackman</h2>

Russell Drysdale is another knighted artist who has not always escaped a critical battering. In 1944 John Reed attacked both Dobell and Drysdale in *Angry Penguins*.

He accused Dobell of 'slick characterization' and 'looking closely at his recent work we find that he appears to be falling into that fateful error of endlessly repeating himself'. Of Drysdale he said:

Drysdale has always been a 'brilliant' painter, but one who has leaned too heavily on his predecessors. . . . I find his interpretation of the Australian landscape essentially banal and insignificant, having an appeal which is superficial only, and that his lanky figures and gaunt stock — derived as they seem to me from American sources — do not satisfy one beyond their first impression.<sup>41</sup>

The Melrose Prize in Adelaide of 1949 was an example of the eagerness with which Australians will leap to the assault. The judges that year were Hans Heysen, Professor J. Burke and Louis McCubbin, the director of the South Australian National Gallery. The winner was Russell Drysdale with 'Woman in a Landscape', a heavy, but indomitable-looking female, standing alone out on a typical, ferociously arid piece of Australian outback. Professor Burke seemed to be almost holding up his hand against the pending attack, when he announced: 'It will not be popular and it may shock some people because it is not a pretty picture. But art should be a criticism of life and not a sugary imitation of it.'<sup>42</sup>

Next day the *Advertiser* reported 'Grotesque, terrible, insulting, shocking, were adjectives freely used by the thousand people who visited in the first two hours.'<sup>43</sup>

Mr George H. Williams, Melrose exhibitor: 'When Professor Burke told us we would not like it, he spoke the truth.'

Miss A. J. Davis, Norwood: 'I only hope Mr Drysdale takes it home. It should be turned face to the wall.'

Woman teacher: 'Half my children could do better than that.'

Mr E. C. Darrington of Northumberland Street, Tusmore, thought the picture could be of invaluable help to the Commonwealth which, by publicizing the picture in Asiatic countries, could maintain the White Australia policy for it is hardly thought that ever the toughest of such people would want to come to this country and see their womenfolk become like the woman of the painting.

The correspondence ran for the rest of the month, and there was particular anger from outback women. Mrs A. V. Pearce, Wood Green Station, Alice Springs, wrote: 'No outback Australian woman of white blood would be seen except perhaps in the more remote areas of our country with such hideous proportions and scanty apparel as the artist portrays. I am, Sir, an outback woman of ordinary intelligence and a good Australian.'

Mrs H. M. Beatty of Sommé Bridge, Bospide, said Drysdale had never ventured beyond the city. He thought it such a fine thing that he had allowed it to be shown. The most literate letter came almost at the end, from Moody Lord of Blinman, in the Flinders Ranges:

I sacrifice myself on the altar of Art. I am a Drysdale. I can account for the appearance of my sister on canvas. Her somewhat ungainly stature is probably caused by Sandshoes and a couple of bunions on her feet. Simple, corset-free living, has taken notice to the fact that the artist was around the bend lately, and that increased just when her perm had only reconditioned recently. The point is, 'Sneak up on them; cane-cutters with padded feet, and you'll get a dozen Drysdales a day.'

Dutton in his book *Russell Drysdale* said that here was an artist who has often been called the most distinctively Australian of painters, being a large section of the community that tells the truth. Drysdale himself replied to all the angry letters simply: 'I tried to give the woman all the mental and physical stature I could, she simply represents the type of woman of the outback who has lived her life out of nothing and raised a family and carved a basis of guts and character and dignity.' That did not appease the local citizens entirely. The Australian Gallery bought the painting. And soon Drysdale's famous Ned Kelly canvases, the Sidney Nolan series, sent by him before he left them behind when he went to London in 1960. A local businessman had nailed them on the garage wall to get them out of the way. He said they had been there for years.<sup>44</sup>

There are many more examples of Artists Knocking, but the artists now suffer far less than the other creative people in Australia and have less need to make their living 'overseas'. Although the sculptor Clement Meadmore has settled in New York, saying it is almost impossible for him to make an adequate living in Melbourne.

However there are at least six artists in Australia who are earning close to $100,000 a year and large firms almost fight each other for the prestige of giving rich art prizes. While a firm which is spending $4 million on a prestige building may begrudge spending $50,000 on a sculpture for the foyer, the chairman likes to have a Boyd, a Nolan, or a Blackman for the board-room wall.

Or as David Boyd said in June 1971: 'Artists are almost as respectable as sportsmen in Australia now — it's bloody terrific.'<sup>45</sup>
