KNOCKING

Wowsers, whingers, ratbags, narks,<br>
Silvertails, galahs and sharks,<br>
Knockers, larrikins, and chromos,<br>
Bengal lancers, bludgers, homos,<br>
Botts and polers, spielers, lairs,<br>
Advance Australia—you are theirs!

That's a classic piece of Australian knocking, and we don't even know who the knocker was. Sidney J. Baker quotes the anonymous poem in his book *Australia Speaks*. Of course we are not the original knockers. The English are more skilled at it and they enjoy it more. The difference is that Australians really mean it. The knocking comes from the depths of their tortured feelings of inadequacy.

In 1971 the newspaper empire of Mr Rupert Murdoch launched the *Sunday Australian*. Its birth revealed some of the age-old truths and agonies of Australia.

The editor came out from London, an Australian journalist who had been a success in England; he had the pure, clean, London ring of confidence. There, you see, we can't really be sure of the quality unless our Australian first has had overseas approval. The Melbourne evening newspaper, *Newsday*, did precisely the same thing two years earlier. It felt the need to import as editor an Australian who had been a success on London's *Daily Express*. So two aims were achieved. Local Australians were impressed by London aura, the choice salved feelings of Australian chauvinism.

The first issue of the *Sunday Australian* was at least four months in the womb. One can imagine what took place at early editorial conferences. 'What can we do to make the first issue a real clinker?' 'I know,' was one reply, 'Get somebody out from England, Muggeridge, anybody. Somebody who can really write, to do us over properly. He can do Sydney, do Melbourne—get everybody irritated and talking. There'll be replies on radio and TV. And this'll help plug our newspaper. Can't miss.'

So the *Sunday Australian* brought out James Cameron, whom it described as 'one of the world's greatest reporters'. The idea was extremely sound, Australians adore to be noticed, they adore to read about themselves. But it is doubtful whether the idea would work for a new newspaper in Paris, Rome or New York. And if a new newspaper were being started by Mr Rupert Murdoch in London, Londoners would have little desire to read what Australia's top reporter thought about England.

Yet we have this almost desperate desire for approval. If anything good or ill is printed about us anywhere in the world, it is reported back instantly in Australia. Clement Freud, a London gourmet *restaurateur*, came to Australia in 1970 to judge a strange function called 'A Butter White Wings Bake Off'. He specialized in caustic, even offensive, remarks, often directed at his hosts. Yet his every lethal word was eagerly sought after, eagerly used. He was in the newspapers, he was on television, he 'talked back' unmercifully on radio.

When he returned to England he appeared on the BBC for a light evening peak hour talk show. He said: 'Australia is a second-rate country inhabited by mediocre people.' This received instant coverage throughout Australia. Why? If Mr Freud had been talking of the quality of Australian bulk red, or the mysterious texture of the Australian meat pie, pavlova or lamington, then maybe his opinions would have been worthy of respect. But we received his judgement as if it had been handed down by the Prime Minister of England.

There is a similar situation when Australia plays England at Test cricket. The M.C.C. touring side is followed by a group of, maybe, a dozen English cricket writers, capable of committing great savagery on their typewriters. They are mostly an irascible lot. During the 1970–1 tour they had much to say about the ill-manners of Australian crowds, the quality of the umpiring, the captaincy of Illingworth. Australian newspapers had their own cricket writers on the spot, some of them even former famous cricketers. Yet, just as on previous tours, this was not enough. Almost every word cabled home by the English cricket writers was cabled back for printing in Australian newspapers. We had to know what they were thinking of us.

James Cameron wrote with great competence in his three articles for the *Sunday Australian*. Yet this opening piece revealed his sense of guilt at being the umpteenth Englishman who had come to Australia to do the usual piece of analytical surgery. All around him were limp Australians, waiting, almost pining for the usual rubbishing.

A T.V. man asks what he thinks of Australia and he has been here only thirty minutes. 'Nothing pal: I love Australia; I have discerned nothing but perfection.' Was it Nat King Cole who had a similar answer when first he arrived in Australia? He had just walked out of the aeroplane. He was surrounded by reporters, cameras and microphones.

'What do you think of Australia, Mr Cole?'

'I don't know, but you sure have lovely roof-tops.'

However, the reply to James Cameron after he said that he discerned nothing but perfection, was this: 'Then you need your bloody head examined.'

He wrote: 'This is a game you cannot win. Australians watchfully await the standard adjectives. Australia is ugly-crude-parochial-matey-wholesome-materialist-Australian. Yet to by-pass the good old catalogue crap is in itself suspicious; this is being tolerant, which is offensive by implication. Enthusiasm, or even approval, brings forth the wrath of the thinking Australians, who must surely be the most morbidly self-critical people on earth and who despise you for your lack of perception or, more likely, dishonesty. They demand the reinforcement of their own misgivings.'

It has always been thus. Cameron is only repeating what almost every visitor to Australia has written—this desire for approval. Please, please, what do you think of us? R.E.N. Twopeny wrote in 1883: 'It is a law in every Australian city that no visitor shall be allowed to rest until he has seen all its sights, done all its lions, and, above all expressed his admiration and surprise of them. With regard to their public institutions, the colonials are like children with a new toy—delighted with it themselves and not contented until everybody they meet has declared it to be delightful.'

D. H. Lawrence found that cab drivers were asking him what he thought of Sydney Harbour even before he had seen it. Jack London, however, did better than that. 'My first impression of Australia was of Sydney Harbour, but I received it long before I saw it. It was in Fiji—no, it was in Samoa—that I was told repeatedly and at length of Sydney's beautiful harbour. On the steamer from the Solomons to Sydney, and the Solomons, there was no escaping the reiterated and manifold glories of the harbour that were impressed on me by my fellow passengers.'

Robert Ruark also was stunned at the way Australians always were looking for his opinion. He wrote in 1954: 'The man asked seriously, as they often do, "But as an American, would you really like to live in Australia?" There seems to be something of an inferiority complex in this constant question, possibly because you are all by yourselves out here surrounded by broad waters and implied hostility from swarms of dark people because you are a continent entirely on your own.' Robert Ruark has identified the problem. Australia has a huge sense of inferiority and it has always been thus. The gush of immigrants that came to Australia during the gold rush of the 1850s were simple people, tin miners from Cornwall, farmers from Yorkshire and, of course, the Irish. They were escaping, seeking something better, so constantly they wanted approval, they wanted confirmation that they had made the right decision.

This national sense of unsureness developed a capacity for knocking. The newcomers did not like discipline and the Australian gained skill at hitting at tall poppies, attacking all those who showed a special talent or those who rose to positions of importance.

John Foster Fraser noticed it back in 1910. He wrote: 'Sad to acknowledge, but the fact should be recognized that in Australia there is a section of people who are deservedly sneered at by the rest of the community as the "stinking fish party"—folk who are always sneering at their own country, always dilating upon the hardships of life in the back blocks, always scoffing at those who praise their land, and who make no secret that they simply want to obtain enough money to clear out of the country.'

Robert Sherrod came to Australia in 1953 to write a long article for the *Saturday Evening Post*. He was interested in this capacity for knocking.

Australians are given to criticising themselves and each other. Periodically they indulge in an orgy of self-abasement that is likely to prove embarrassing to the visitor, who finds himself defending the country against its own citizens. I noted this censorious tendency on my first visit, shortly after Pearl Harbor, when a prominent editor and leader of public opinion, now deceased, blurted out, 'This country is no damn good'.

Sherrod groped for reasons, and he quoted such authorities as Sir Frederic Eggleston. Australia was an egalitarian country, everybody was as good as anyone else. Good things came easily, there was little need for vast effort and the ordinary man could live as well as the man with brains and energy.

Sherrod clearly was stunned by an article he had read in the *Bulletin* which said Australia had few heroes outside jockeys and racehorses. They had stopped breeding statesmen of enduring reputation because few engaged in politics could expect to survive unless they were prepared to pander to the lowest intelligence and the selfishness of the majority. The number of scoffers and mendicants was the most conceited in the world and the greatest offence any Australian could commit was to show himself palpably superior in intellect and capacity to the ruck. The sentence was normally life exile, unless the culprit was willing to deliver himself into local bondage as a panderer to the gullible and a confidence man to the golden age.

After these potent lines Sherrod expected action. But, he said, nobody even wrote him a terse letter.

The Australian's desire for self-examination is so great that he gets used to the hard knocks as well as the soft. Al Capp, creator of the American comic strip 'L'il Abner' has a gift for producing extraordinary creatures. One of them is the kygmy. The kygmy adores ordinary human beings and is the real answer to the miseries of mankind. It has a hard, solid behind. So any human who wants to get rid of his neurotic frustrations can kick a kygmy and a kygmy is really only happy when it is being kicked. So Australians have fine kygmy characteristics, they really like to be knocked. They are always hunting for more.

In 1924, a Sydney journal said it was fed up with supercilious references to Australia so it wrote to literary men around the world and asked them what names first occurred to them when Australia was mentioned and why. The answers were satisfyingly insulting. The only name Arnold Bennett could think of was Sydney. Hilaire Belloc thought of Captain Cook and this was because 'the sons of well-to-do families in England are taught a few scraps of history but nothing about the contemporary world'.

E. V. Lucas came up with Victor Trumper and Henry Lawson and added enigmatically 'but I have met none of your blacks'. Masefield ... 'Anzac, Lone Pine and Pozieres'.

H. L. Mencken said: 'Hughes, the war premier. He seems to be a political mountebank of the true American type, almost worthy to be classed with Coolidge.'

A. A. Milne found Trumper, Captain Cook, Ned Kelly and Adam Lindsay Gordon. For these names, he said, he could make neither apology nor explanation.

George Bernard Shaw's entire answer was this: 'What name occurs to me when Australia happens to be mentioned? My uncle's. He emigrated there. Why? Why not?'

Thomas Burke did rather better: 'Adam Lindsay Gordon, Emu bush, Norman Lindsay and Melba. As to why these things occur to me, I do not know; but the above ideas come in one flash at the first mention of Australia, just as the word Cuba flashes cigars, and Italy flashes grand opera.'

This was an example of yearning to be noticed, a searching for recognition. We hope that the overseas sophistication will wash off on us so nothing in Australia is ever quite good enough. We have had a passion for importing everything in sight. It has always been better to have a French name for a restaurant, like Maxims, an American name for a hotel, like the Sheraton, and our picture theatres are the Palladium, the Roma, the Forum and the Paris.

In resorts like Surfers Paradise, an American visitor must wonder why he ever left home when he sees Miami, Las Vegas, Santa Barbara, Santa Ana, Bali Hi and Aloha. And any lonesome American touring through Kings Cross can gather such a wealth of imported culture that he gains the nice satisfaction of feeling he has never left home.

But it was always thus. Back in 1917, E. E. Pescott, Director of Horticulture in Melbourne, said Australians did not know what went on in their country and believed everything had to be imported. He said that as far back as 1857, a Sydney nurseryman advertised in his catalogue plants the Swan River daisy—from seeds imported from England. Then during the First World War a Melbourne nurseryman sent to England for seeds of Sturt's desert pea, which covered untold acres close to Broken Hill. He received the reply: 'None available now that the German market is closed.'

Warming to his subject, Mr Pescott said before the German market closed another Melbourne nurseryman imported the seeds of one hundred varieties of Australian wattle—the cultivation of some of them had not been attempted here. Then there was the local nurseryman who imported Japanese irises, and with great pride he presented one to Mr Pescott. Why import it from Japan? The nurseryman could have found tons of the same iris growing wild around Gembrook. The lotus lily in the Melbourne Botanic Gardens was another. It had been imported at great expense from Japan, but it was an Australian native which grew wild in Queensland and the Northern Territory.

Yes, something from overseas is always best. Donald Horne, in his book *The Next Australia*, wrote of the Australian intellectuals' exaggerated respect for that absurd word 'overseas'.

Having constructed Overseas out of all the nations, one then picks the eyes out of them, so that by comparison Australia becomes the country without qualities, the least of them all, the world's one non-nation. They write better overseas; look at Dostoevsky. They paint better overseas; look at Rembrandt. They cook better overseas; look at the Chinese. They're more liberal; look at Denmark. They're better at social services; look at Sweden. The Japanese can arrange flowers better than Australians; Filipinos are better at batik; Paraguayans are more musical. Does Australia have mosques as good as Pakistan's? If there is some relaxed style between persons, or in growing wool—this becomes a fault because of its oddity. The mineral boom was acceptable.

Overseas accents are good. At least until the 1950s it was nigh impossible to get a job as a news reader on the Australian Broadcasting Commission unless one had an accent with the refinement of Sir John Gielgud. It wasn't until 1969 that the ABC discovered that a man like Bill Peach could be a huge success on a national news show. His accent came from Albury and he called himself plain Bill. But overseas is still good for disc jockeys. Young announcers adopt the American accent coupled with the 300-word-a-minute-gotta-keep-moving style as if a second of dead air space would cause a national power failure.

J. C. Williamson's over a period of eighty years has discovered that it is always better to have overseas stars in stage shows. Australians are dead meat at the box office unless, of course, they have been overseas and are returning as triumphant stars. Ron Randell, Robert Helpmann, Peter Finch or Diane Cilento are acceptable under such circumstances. Dame Nellie Melba; she was revered as a goddess. Yet after her death not one Australian city, not even her native Melbourne, thought to build a statue to her memory. Yet almost every city has a statue of George V and Robbie Burns. Overseas, you see.

The Church and our great public schools have always liked overseas. Until the 1960s Australian-born bishops were a rarity and it wasn't until 1966 that the Anglican Church found an Australian who was worthy of the office of Archbishop. Schools like Geelong Grammar and Melbourne Grammar still look to England for their headmasters; the English background is necessary for the training of Australians and no doubt gives tone to the school bill. In these schools the teachers still talk of Captain Cook 'discovering' Australia. That was the good thing about Captain Cook. He came from overseas.

Australian television is dominated by the overseas product, so that the Australian child can gain a deeper insight into the life of the U.S. cowboy, the American neuro-surgeon, the neuroses of the U.S. Navy and the agonies of the London rag-and-bone merchant, than the culture of his own country. In 1970 there was a revolt amongst the T.V. performers. They held protest meetings in Melbourne and Sydney and they made the appeal that all they wanted was work in their own country. They produced an astonishing list of names of Australians who had made their name in England and U.S.A., but not in Australia.

Alwyn Kurts, one of the leading speakers at the Melbourne meeting, told the story of the successful Australian pop group, the Seekers. He said Hal Lashwood, then president of Actors Equity, heard them singing at the Federal Hotel in Melbourne where he was staying. They had been unable to get work on television, and as no one knew who they were, he arranged for them to appear in a brief spot on his own show. The Seekers turned him down. The offer had come too late, they were booked to sail for Britain the next week.

Alwyn Kurts said: 'There is a parochial type of thinking in our management here. If a T.V. artist comes from overseas he is accepted here without question, whereas a local is not. Yet many of these overseas performers are incompetent. They come here and claim talent that they do not have.'

The T.V. proprietors in answer to all this told of the cost of producing programmes in Australia, and besides, there was a dearth of writers. Kurts said the problem wasn't the dearth of writers but the lack of demand for their talents. It was interesting that of the top script writers in Britain, 25 per cent were Australian.

Connoisseurs of neat items of irony were interested in the 1970 Logie awards for excellence in the industry. On 26 March 1971, Maggie Tabberer received the National Gold Logie for the best female star on television, and the Logie for the best female star in New South Wales. She said: 'You never know, somebody might offer me a job tomorrow.' Except for guest appearances she had been unable to get work in Australian television for seven months.

It is this self-doubt, the lack of confidence, that has produced a race of knockers. When Melbourne bid for the Olympic Games of 1956 the whole project almost perished under an avalanche of knocking. No great project has suffered such a continuing, often brilliant, barrage of witticisms as the Sydney Opera House. Not one Australian statesman has been revered or granted the respect he would receive in European countries. Australian writers of the stature of Patrick White go almost unknown. One Melbourne newspaper conducted a survey in 1971. The first dozen people asked knew about Philip Roth and his novel *Portnoy's Complaint*, but of the author of *Voss*, *The Tree of Man* and *The Vivisector* they knew nothing.

Our humour, too, has a mocking, derisive style. We like it to be cutting and slightly destructive. The television programme which hit the highest ratings was 'The Mavis Bramston Show', famous for its savagery. The most successful performer on Australian television since its inception in 1956 was Graham Kennedy, and his skill came from a clever mocking wit.

Because of this knocking it is harder for Australian soloists to get their start with Australian orchestras. It is downright difficult for Australians to get posts as conductors in their own country, easier for them to make a name as novelists in London than in Melbourne or Sydney and more rewarding for actors to perform away from home. Charles Osborne, perhaps the leading Australian emigré in London, claims that the Australian artist or intellectual finds the Australian scene so stultifyingly conformist, his audience so unappreciative, he is almost forced to emigrate.

Whether the audience is stultifyingly conformist or just plain dull, the critics are not. Many visiting concert artists are quite unused to their knocking skill and claim that our critics are the most savage in the world.

There should be a better word for all this than knocking. There should be some fine Australian witticisms as an American term meaning 'to criticise unfairly ... an unfair critic'. An American dictionary more simply says 'a knocker' is 'a fault-finder' and gives its first use in *Everybody's Magazine*, 1900. A discontented actor who receives less than £40 a week. Maybe it is not the best word, but we should still admire it. After all, it comes from overseas.
