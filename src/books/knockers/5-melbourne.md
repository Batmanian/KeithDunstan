Of all the cities in Australia Melbourne is most splendidly knockable. Perhaps it is the dignity of the city, perhaps it is the primness of the layout of the streets, perhaps it is the worthiness of the clubs, but almost every visitor finds knocking irresistible.

Indeed Melbourne likes to be knocked. Knock material is easy to find. Perth has led a charmed life, Brisbane has gone practically unknocked, but Melbourne is so used to being bruised, newspapers keep fat files labelled 'MELBOURNE—CRITICISM OF.'

The most famous of all knocks, and no Melburrnian ever forgets it, comes from Ava Gardner. Miss Gardner came to Melbourne to make the film version of the Nevil Shute novel *On the Beach*. She found it hard to conceal her distaste for the place, and this was the end of the world. Very likely this is a myth. There is no record in print that Ava Gardner ever said such a thing, but Melbourne rather hopes that she did. It has a lovely ring about it. She is also reputed to have flown to Sydney at the weekends just to get away from the place.

Another film star, Terence Stamp, said: 'Melbourne—it's a [unclear]. Melbourne is such a conventional town I'm surprised you didn't ask the Beatles to shave off their hair.'

Robert Morley, who one time was King of Moomba, said:

> I don't care for Melbourne people because they are spineless. They are all Yogis. They get used to their spiked beds and finally begin to worship them. I found Melbourne a boring town, an unpainted town, a town with bad roads. I got tired of the methylated spirit drinkers lying round the stage door. . . . Your public houses are Hogarthian in their horror. Why don't you paint the Town Hall?

That comment was made in 1949, and irate Melbourne quoted it again and again. On his second visit for Moomba 1967 he was much more amenable. He liked the idea of being a King. As he said, it was something he was awfully good at. At various times on stage and screen he had been Louis XII, Louis XIV, Louis XV and George III.

This time he merely restricted himself to such comments as: 'Yes, I prefer Sydney. It is a very pretty place. No doubt Melbourne has all sorts of hidden charms and graces, but somehow they escape me. You all live in little boxes that look exactly the same and they go on for eternity. . . . I don't know what you do all day in Melbourne without poker machines.'

As for his comments on the city:

Flinders Street Station: It looks as if they were setting out to build St Paul's then suddenly changed their minds, realizing they had to do it on the opposite corner.

Swanston Street: Haven't they chosen the route well? This street has the brewery at one end and the Shrine at the other. It is really a saga of Australian life.<sup>1</sup>

There are a number of recurring themes in all the writing on Melbourne, particularly, the Melbourne Sunday and Melbourne beauty. James Fitzpatrick said Melbourne had a tranquillity, a pure beauty; it was a work of art like the Winged Victory of Samothrace, the Two Minutes' Silence, or La Gioconda.<sup>4</sup>

William Kelly, an Irishman, wrote in the early 1850s:

> For a new, rollicking, harum-scarum, devil-may-care country I marvelled to witness the air of quiet serenity and decorousness which pervaded the capital on the Sabbath morning. I could almost fancy the echo of my footfall was reproving my trespass on the prevailing stillness. Even when I got into Great Collins Street there was not a soul astir.<sup>5</sup>

In 1906 *Truth* said:

> It is neither a Scotch Sabbath nor a Jew's Sabbath, neither a Christian Sunday nor a white man's holiday; but a mixed-up abortion of a thing brought about through the promiscuous intercourse of all sorts of sects and interests. Morning trains only carry folks to church, trams will not run until afternoon. And there shall be no drinking in any hotel, and no selling of any Sunday newspapers. . . . How long, oh Lord, how long?

Yet perhaps the most eloquent of all was the poet, Chris Wallace-Crabbe. In 1963 he wrote:

> Melbourne's Sunday is a national joke. Apparently kept up in the hope that it will keep people at home mowing the lawn and trimming the hedge, or drive them unwillingly to church, it has become a little less burdensome now that families can flee into the country by car. But teenagers, migrant bachelors and flat-dwellers are likely to find Sunday merely the fag end of the week. If they scan the sad little column in Saturday night's *Herald*, 'What's On Tomorrow', they will hardly derive much consolation or inspiration. Sunday is typified by the closed doors of shops, cafés, theatres, dance halls and pubs, by notices in parks saying NO ORGANISED SPORT, by the Dandenongs and Mornington Peninsula crammed with traffic, and by disheartened young men trailing up and down the footpaths. The following exchange, reported in a recent *Herald*, took place outside the National Gallery, a favourite stalking-ground:

'What do you do on Sunday I asked. Look for sheilas, one answered.

'Findany?—Yeah. Seven of us started out, and now there are only four of us.

'What are you going to do now?—I dunno. We might have a game of billiards.'

'What would you like to do if you had a choice? Look for sheilas.'<sup>7</sup>

The feature of Melbourne which inspires most attention is its weather. Now Melbourne, officially, is in a temperate zone. It does not receive the ferocious extremes of New York or Chicago. Rarely, if ever, does Melbourne have snow and nobody skates on the Yarra, yet the Melburnian weather is a national joke, a constant topic of interstate conversation.

Mark Twain once said that there was something about the weather of New England that compelled admiration. It was always getting up new designs and trying them on people to see how they would go. In the spring he counted 136 different kinds of weather inside twenty-four hours.

How many indeed, 136 different kinds of weather for this city would be most ingenious. People not understanding its remarkable complexity have been only too eager to knock it.

It is very interesting to note how visitors to Melbourne have complained about it over a very long period, all of them stressing its extraordinary variety. Richard Henry Horne, who found Melbourne intolerable in practically every respect, sent a dispatch to *The Times*, which was published in London on 11 June 1853:

> It was a fine summer morning in Melbourne on Sunday, the 19th of December, 1852, but about midday the air became very hot, and oppressively so about two o'clock. Having occasion to visit an encampment in the bush to the northward of the town, I rode up leisurely in that direction. I had not proceeded far, when the hot gusts became more frequent, and in less than five minutes there came a gale of wind, bringing with it a long streaming volume of hot, stinging dust, so dense that, when I could manage to peer up from beneath the broad brim of my hat, I could not see a yard in advance, nor could I see below my horse's ears.

> I saw on the high swelling ground a dirty red cloud. The line reddened and they became darker. It was no cloud. The whole mass was evidently advancing. Of course I immediately became but too well aware that this was some new specimen of the hot-blast, of a kind far exceeding any I had met before.

> I was obliged to leap across a long winding gully, amid the giddy drift of the preliminary gust of hot sand, and had just time to half-vault, half-scramble over the fence, and crouch down behind it, my hat pulled over my eyes, and my face pressed towards my knees. With a dry rushing noise, like a land-storm sweeping through a forest in autumn, the hot blast struck the fence at my back. Half opening my eyes to look at the ground, in consequence of a strange sensation of things all about me, I saw that I was covered and the ground too, all round me with myriads of flies, beetles, ants, grasshoppers and other insects. Great numbers of them were dead, especially the flying grasshoppers, beetles, and a great grassy-looking fly, something like a long half-starved wasp; but most of the smaller flies, and nearly all the ants, were in a lively state of dismay . . . so I sat enclosed in a hollow edge of reddish-grey, hot, stinging dust, beyond which nothing was visible. It seemed to me this lasted nearly half an hour. I found my horse without much difficulty. He had been half paralysed with alarm.

> After a period of moderate warmth, cooled by occasional torrents of rain, the thermometer rose, on the 30th of December, to 113 degrees, in the shade of a veranda. I hung it up against a house in the sun, two doors from Captain Chisholm's office in Swanston Street, and in less than 20 minutes it rose to 126 degrees. Next day the heat was much the same till the afternoon, when the wind changed to the south-west. On the day after this, January 1, 1853, we had rain and the wind was as cold as in the early part of Spring, some three months ago. On January 5 I felt cold all day, and on the 6th I fairly returned to the same under-clothing I had worn in the winter.

And the poor man added that only his English training could accustom him 'to such caprices in this heaven on earth'.<sup>8</sup>

White-skinned English ladies found the climate particularly trying. Ellen Kean, wife of the distinguished Shakespearean actor, Charles Kean, wrote on 20 November 1863:

> Since I last wrote to you this lovely climate has given us every variety of bad weather except snow, and they have that in some parts. When the rain ceased we had hail, then a lull would come with dreadful sunshine, then the bay would be quickly lashed into wild disturbance, and then it would calm down coldly upon the water, the wind howling piteously and the sky threatening. The only really settled weather we had was when it continued raining.<sup>9</sup>

However, the writings of Miss Isabella Bird should be required reading for meteorological students in Melbourne. She was really the first of the great travel writers. There was hardly a corner of the globe she did not visit. She sent home regular writings to an English magazine called *Leisure Hour*. She was the James Fitzpatrick of her day, but more so. It was a daring thing for a single lady to travel alone to the most exotic parts of the world, and Melbourne was considered considerably exotic. She came to Melbourne in 1872 and the climate struck her thus:

> The number of heavy thunderstorms with torrents of rain averages twenty-six a year, while the Geelong folk on the same harbor escape with three. My own experience of the Melbourne climate was very unfavorable, though I left it before the summer heats set in. It produced at once such irritation of the nerves as to make me dream of a bath of laudanum and such lassitude that 'even the grasshopper was a burden'. Brilliant skies, fierce sunshine, torrid heat, dewless nights, sullen skies, resulting in thunderstorms; rains which devastated rather than refreshed; almost constant winds; humid sunless heat; dust storms; hot gales from the north, and cold gales from the south, are the meteorological record of my diary.<sup>10</sup>

Also in Melbourne at this time was Marcus Clarke, author of *His Natural Life*, a very grim volume. It has been forgotten that Clarke was also one of Australia's funniest writers. He wrote satirical columns for the *Argus*, the *Australasian* and the *Daily Telegraph*. His relations with the *Argus* became somewhat strained in 1867. As drama critic he criticized the performance of Mr and Mrs George Case. This would have been all right, except that the performances he criticized never took place. Mrs Case, the previous evening, had been 'indisposed'.

One of the famous Marcus Clarke columns was titled 'Our Glorious Climate'.

5 a.m. Awoke to find the window open, wind blowing in like a truant tornado, looking-glass broken and cold in my left ear. Tral-la-la. Glorious climate.

6 a.m. I must have been asleep. 'Tis as hot as a furnace. Pooh!

7 a.m. Open the window. The wind has changed.

I should say it had. Dust! Ugh! It's a north wind now, and the floor of my chamber is an inch deep in sand. Glorious climate!

8.35 What! No water—?

8.30 Certainly not. A thin pipe-stem of the cooling stream only flows.

9 a.m. Nothing like jollity. Where are my boots? Choked with sand. The wash-stand full of gravel, and my writing-table a drifted ruin like the Sphinx in the desert. Glorious climate.

11.59 My hat blows off. A fat man running after his hat is the most despicable object in nature. My boots are full of protoplasm, and my heart is full of woe. Thermometer is 104.

2.30 Been drinking. Glorious climate. Thermometer at 120. I should like to sit under Niagara and eat ice-creams with a cold chisel.

5.30 The wind has changed. It is now blowing from five places at once, and Bourke Street is like a flour mill. Glorious climate.

6.30 Home. No water. House full of dust. . . . Can't have dinner for an hour, for the boiler has burst, the oven is full of sand, and the meat has turned bad.

7.10 Stroll out in white flannel and cigar! Cool as a cucumber.

7.11 This IS a glorious climate.

Looks dark in the south. Sou'-west, atmospherically interested cabman says, 'We'll get some rain!' I hope so.

7.15 We do. Buckets full of it. It's come down with savage persistence. Gutters run over. Half-crowns hopping on the pavement. Omnibuses full, and my flannel soaked to the skin—my skin.

8 p.m. Home again. Everything burst. Bathroom flooded. Kitchen like a watercourse, and the dinner floating down the backyard.

8.30 Message from Jack. My uncle, Rumbelow, who promised to leave me £20,000, had a sunstroke when going to his solicitors to make his will, and is dead. Just my luck. Fancy black, too, in weather like this.

9 p.m. Drank.

10 p.m. Drunk.

11 p.m. Come to conclush'on s'was a glor-i-oshus climate.<sup>11</sup>

There were those who believed that the white man could never work in the fetid, tropical heat of Queensland. Therefore black kanaka labour was imported to cut the sugar cane. Also there were those who believed in the debilitating effects of the Melbourne climate. John Foster Fraser, who wrote *The Making of a Nation* in 1910, had no doubt that the climate affected the character of all Europeans. After seeing the 'loungers round Sydney' he had no doubt whatever.

Foster Fraser was impressed with the opinions of Dr Alexander Buttner of Melbourne, 'whose authority to speak on the subject no Australian will dispute'. He said fifty years' residence in Victoria had forced upon Dr Buttner the conviction that, in cases where both parents were Australian-born, the weakening effect on the climate showed itself more and more strikingly with each succeeding generation.

The published opinions of the doctor went thus:

> It is, therefore, my firm belief that the number of children born will steadily decline as the distance from the European family stock increases. An absolute necessity exists, then, that we should once more secure a continual stream of European immigration, if only for the sake of self-preservation. Were it possible to cut off the Australian continent from the rest of the world for a few centuries, I am convinced that not a single descendant of its four million white population would be living. The race would have become extinct.

> Our horses, cattle, sheep, pigs and poultry are undergoing this regular process of deterioration, which can only be stopped by the introduction of fresh blood from time to time. Our Australian-born population is, so to speak, an exotic one, which, in order to preserve its original vigor of physique and mental stamina, will require an influx of the Teutonic races from which it has sprung. Without this influx the white race here is doomed.

> Being one of the old colonists, and a close observer of the deteriorating influence of this climate, so soon as my six children had passed through their primary education, I made, with pleasure, great paternal and material sacrifices in sending them to Europe for the purpose of there fortifying their constitution during the period of this principal development.<sup>13</sup>

The old doctor may have been completely right. When the Third Test Match was washed out with torrential rains over the New Year of 1971, Mrs Frances Clarke of Windsor was quoted at length in the newspapers. She said, and many were inclined to agree: 'I think the end of the world is coming. Man has upset the balance of nature. I see terrible things. The air is filled with electricity, the clouds are thick and ugly and the sun is getting weaker. Little people like myself are becoming too scared to venture out of doors.'

For the benefit of future knockers, perhaps one should give a run-down on the character of Melbourne. The Reverend F. Lancelott Esq., 1852:

> Little etiquette is practised: all persons act with great independence, and regardless of appearances follow only the dictates of their own feelings and sentiments. A most unbounded spirit of avarice actuates all classes; nothing is considered disgraceful but the want of money.<sup>14</sup>

Richard Henry Horne, 1859:

> The haunts of villainy in Sydney are not surpassed by those in Melbourne; but with regard to drunkenness and prostitution the latter place is far worse than Sydney, or any other city in the world. The Theatre Royal has a drunken orgy every night. It is too vulgar, maudlin and debased to merit description. One peculiarity only shall be mentioned, and that is the extraordinary ugliness of the young women, who constitute the most prominent characteristic of the scene. Not only are they ugly morally, and from their vice and grossness, their drunkenness and insolence, but their personal ugliness in face and figure and sometimes without a single exception. The population of Hamburg is much the same as that of Melbourne, viz., 120,000, and in the former there were in 1846, 500 registered women, which closely corresponds with the number in Melbourne. The scenes that are witnessed, even during the day, half-way up Bourke Street, actually surpass anything in St Giles' or Covent Garden after dusk.

Mr Horne then devoted himself to the intellectual attainments of the young men of Melbourne.

> They have no relish for learning or philosophy, or science, no taste for reading anything but trash, or seeing anything on the stage but burlesque, and no ruling impulse with respect to literature, the fine arts, manners, the respect and delicacy due to ladies, and personal habits in regard to the bath, and some self-command in drinking and smoking, no ruling impulse, let me say, but one, and that one is desecration. The majority of the young men possess no educational knowledge, no talents, or taste, and they cordially hate all those who possess such acquirements.<sup>15</sup>

R. E. N. Twopeny, 1883:

> The river Yarra runs through the city, and is navigable as far as its centre by coasting steamers . . . below it is uninteresting and chiefly remarkable for the number and variety of perfumes which arise from the manufacturers on its banks. Next to the monotony of the Suez Canal with which it presents many points of resemblance, I know few things more tiresome than the voyage up the Yarra in an intercolonial steamer of 600 or 700 tons, which goes aground every ten minutes, and generally as if on purpose, just in front of a boiling-down establishment. If the Australian cities can claim a sad eminence, if not an actual supremacy in the number of their public houses, of which there are not less than 1,120 in Melbourne, I am sorry to say they are as much behind London in their ideas of the comfort of an hotel as London is behind San Francisco. Melbourne is certainly better off than Sydney or Adelaide, but bad are its best hotels.<sup>16</sup>

In modern times the quality of the knocking has been sustained splendidly. In 1963 Chris Wallace-Crabbe wrote a paper for the *Current Affairs Bulletin*. Papers on most of the other cities had been charming and complimentary. He gave Melbourne something to do for at least three days. Newspapers and television stations gained from everyone in sight reassuring comments that their city was really exciting, beautiful, cultured and superior to all other dwelling places.

Among other things he said:

One cannot focus on any place, any situation, and say 'Here indeed is the centre of Melbourne,' for the city is not merely large, but to an extraordinary degree, sprawling and centreless. The mile-square grid of the city proper is somehow far less permanent than the hundreds of square miles of vast suburbs into which the population flees in the evening, draws the puritan blinds and settles itself before the blue doom of the television screen.

There is little about the place of which he can really boast, except for its ubiquitous prosperity and its eminence as the world capital of Australian Rules football. He could hardly recommend it to a tourist, since it has no exciting or exotic features at all—the Melbourne Cup and the vaguely restrained Moomba festival are the biggest events in a quiet year. He must, in fact, admit that it is a drab, uninspiring city.

Mr Wallace-Crabbe said Melburnians bitterly resented any criticism offered by overseas visitors. He found that they also bitterly resented criticism from local poets.

Others were more succinct. Max Bygraves, 1965: 'I've always wanted to see a ghost town. You couldn't even get a parachute to open here after 10 p.m.'<sup>22</sup>

After this what is there left to say about Melbourne? Well, Clement Freud, the garrulous gourmet from London, said:

> No wonder Biggs used this place as a hideout. Verily, as they say in the good book, Melbourne is an ideal city for an escaped convict. There is a general air of incompetence, the permanent promises on the part of strangers to do things which are never done, and above all the pervading ailment of suffering from delusions of grandeur. Could Biggs be driving a taxi? No Melbourne taxi-driver seems to look a passenger in the face. I leave the city to Ronald Biggs. And yet, I wonder whether the feller mightn't have more fun in Dartmoor.<sup>23</sup>

Then in the words of our friend Barry Humphries: 'Seriously friends, Melbourne is unique from the spiritual point of view. It's the only place on earth where the visitor from abroad can close his eyes and wonder if there really is life before death.'<sup>24</sup>
