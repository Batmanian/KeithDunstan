**The Glorious Days of Abstinence**

<!-- Pages 49–55 covering the founding of the Port Phillip Temperance Society in 1838, the early campaigns of Mrs Dalgarno and other women reformers, and the origins of the Blue Ribbon movement in Victoria, are partially illegible in the scan due to spine curvature. -->

The Women's Christian Temperance Union had enormous influence on the shape of Australian public life for fifty years. Across the colonies, the WCTU organised, petitioned, lobbied, and marched. Its members wore the blue ribbon as a public pledge of abstinence, and their public prayer meetings at the doors of hotels were a regular feature of colonial Saturday nights.

The great dream of the temperance movement was not simply to reduce drinking but to replace the hotel altogether. The answer was the Coffee Palace — a hotel without a licence, offering lodging, meals, reading rooms and billiard tables, demonstrating that respectable commercial hospitality needed no bar.

James Munro was the man who made the Coffee Palace his personal crusade. Munro was Premier of Victoria, a total abstainer, a man of prodigious energy and great personal charm who went bankrupt — and ruined a great many of his friends — in the service of his temperance ideals. He launched the Victoria Coffee Palace in 1888. In the 1880s Coffee Palaces were all the rage. There was the Victoria, the Melbourne, St Ann's, the Prahran, the St Kilda, the South Yarra, and in New South Wales the Sydney Coffee Palace, and in South Australia the Adelaide Coffee Palace in Hindley Street and the Grayson Coffee Palace in North Terrace opposite the railway station.

Mr Munro started the first of the Coffee Palaces, the Victoria, in 1888. In 1889, Melbourne's greatest hotel was the Grand, in Spring Street. In 1886 Mr James Munro brought a company he had built up to shareholders and had formed the Grand Coffee Palace Company with a capital of £950,000. Its hostess was to be the Windsor, with 5,000 shares at £5 each. Here was a hotel with 155 bedrooms, 6 sitting rooms and 7 bathrooms. There was a range with a grand entertaining programme, and the *Daily Telegraph* commented: 'The Directors have not misjudged the value of sacrificing comfort to appearance and the splendid building contains many a cosy nook where the weary traveller may seat himself by a sunny fireside and feel himself at home.'

The great controversy of the era was the barmaid. The barmaid was regarded by the temperance movement as the supreme instrument of seduction — the secret weapon of the licensed trade, deployed to entice young men into bars and keep them there spending. The trade regarded her as simply a skilled employee. A Royal Commission on the matter was appointed in 1906.

**Prohibition**

Governor La Trobe made the first serious attempt at prohibition in Victoria in 1852, but it came to nothing. The experiment that came closest to working was not in Victoria at all, but in two remote irrigation settlements on the Murray River that had been founded on the explicit condition that no liquor licence would ever be granted.

The Chaffey brothers, American irrigation pioneers, founded the Renmark colony in South Australia and the Mildura colony in Victoria in the 1880s. Both colonies were established with a clause in their founding agreements with the respective governments forbidding the granting of any liquor licence to any business within the settlement boundaries. It was believed by the Chaffeys and their supporters that sober colonies would be more industrious, more stable, and more attractive to the kind of steady settlers who would make the irrigation enterprise a success.

Renmark held its ground. In a poll at Renmark, NO beat YES by 265 votes, and the colony remained dry.

Mildura was more turbulent. In 1894, the settlement's proximity to the border and the easy availability of liquor from just outside the boundary meant that a sly-grog trade had grown up regardless of the founding agreement. When Mr Geary opened a grog shop within the settlement boundary, the police moved against him. In seconds it was ablaze from end to end. The police entered in rank with fixed bayonets and the Superintendent reduced the troops to push the cars into the fire from the far side. The situation was managed eventually. Mr Geary lost his business; the Superintendent was criticised; the Commissioner of Goldfields inquired. Dr Geoffrey Searle wrote about it later in *The Golden Age*.

In 1894 the Colonial Secretary organised a Select Committee to inquire into the management of the goldfields. The committee found that so many licences had been granted on the goldfields, and that managing and maintaining the hotel, often the best house in the settlement, was a cry for the police. The Mildura Coffee Palace did get its wine licence and it opened for trade on the first of January 1897. From then on, the *Coffee Cultivator* had been rather blamed for its downfall and truly. The next course was a choice of onions or consommé or spaghetti marinara. Mr J. Lyman Bingham quite recently had made a special study of the situation there.

After another eleven years of agitation, Mildura had a chance to change. The Temperance forces all came to town to hold a poll. There were two polls — one for Mildura west and one for Mildura east — and in both the prohibition side was heavily defeated. Mildura had gone for licensing, and the long experiment of the irrigation colony without liquor was over.

**Sober by Law**

When everything else failed, the Temperance men turned militant: the only answer was to make men sober by Act of Parliament. The Reverend Francis B. Boyce, the leader of the Local Option League in New South Wales, wrote: 'In shutting out intemperance, it is the plain duty of Government to prevent the sale of intoxicating liquor by an Act of Parliament, in spite of a minimal portion of this kind. Every licence refused is a distinct advance on the road to sobriety, and every new licence granted is a distinct step backwards.'<a href="#1"><sup>1</sup></a>

Except perhaps for the tenure of land, and the railways, there was probably no subject in Australia on which politicians spent more effort to less effect. Politicians passed and re-passed laws which the customers ignored, so that a lawyer needed a lifetime just to get a fair grip of the subject. In New South Wales alone there were 27 Acts passed between 1825 and 1885.

This is section 39 of the New South Wales Pension Act of 1907: 'If any pensioner be convicted of drunkenness, or of any offence punishable by imprisonment on any two occasions after 1 January in any year, no pension shall be paid to him or in respect of his family during the ensuing period of one year.'<a href="#2"><sup>2</sup></a> It went on to make it unlawful for a publican to supply a pensioner who had been convicted of an offence. 'The pension was to be reduced or suspended for a time on account of such person's drunken or intemperate habits.' The loss was likely to be four shillings a week.

Of course all this was the result of a rich tradition: the legislators had much to work on. Prior to the eighteen-eighties, the number of hotels in proportion to the population, and the amount of liquor consumed, could only be described as remarkable. The hours went almost around the clock. Take Victoria. For £5 only, practically any grocer could obtain a licence and this enabled him to sell liquor by the bottle, which could be in any quantity from a pint to two gallons. Temperance men raged against this. There were 200 hotels in Ballarat West, one for every 130 people, in Sandhurst one for every 64. If one took in the beer shops and the public houses in Ballarat West, there was a public house for every 25 people.

Hotel hours were from 6 a.m. to midnight, but it was also possible to get a triple pass which ran from midnight to 6 a.m. So the publican who possessed a combined licence did not have to close at all until Sunday. Beer was available at 2s. 6d a hogshead, and it was possible to buy a gin bottle for less than a shilling.

The hotel hours were from 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. in South Australia, 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. in Queensland. In New South Wales hotels were open until midnight until 1882, when the new Licensing Act reduced the hours back to 11 p.m.

Sunday drinking was a big issue in the nineteenth century, as big as illegal gambling. The public refused to tolerate the law, and almost invariably, despite the official prohibition, it continued behind closed doors. Victoria abolished Sunday drinking by law in 1854 and it caused such chagrin that in 1858, 15,000 people petitioned for the hotels to be reopened. New South Wales stopped it in 1882 and Queensland from 1 January 1886.

In 1867 the Intoxicating Drink Inquiry Commission of N.S.W. reported that in 1885 there were 872 hotels in Sydney. Only 103 observed the law, and the rest carried on a systematic Sunday trade.<a href="#4"><sup>4</sup></a> The publicans employed scouts to watch for the police, used special pass-words to gain entry through locked doors, formed clubs with a nominal subscription paid at the bar, and having the front doors closed would admit through side entrances.

Witnesses put forward some fascinating excuses for Sunday opening. 'The poor man likes his draught beer freshly drawn on Sundays. If the hotels are not open then he has to drink flat, stale beer, or go without.' There is more drinking now in the home. 'Those who remain to drink carry on for indefinite prolonged periods when they would be more likely to finish their drinks at hotels within a few hours.'

The Commissioners were not very sympathetic to any of these ideas, for the inquiry was heavily larded with total abstainers. The Report commented: 'The best method for checking drunkenness in Australia is not, we believe, hard to find, but the plain duty of Government is to reduce the number of hotels wherever necessary.'

One of the witnesses was Dr Barry, Anglican Bishop of Sydney. The *Bulletin* pointed out that unless a Bishop had been arrested for being drunk himself, he could not know much about it. On the other hand a correspondent of the *Bulletin*, who described himself as having been found hopelessly drunk on five occasions, said he should be called to give evidence along with the confirmed drunkards of Sydney.<a href="#5"><sup>5</sup></a>

Curiously enough, the best place to go for a drink on Sunday was South Australia. Nobody thought it would be wrong for hotels to be open on Sunday, but the law restricted service: a man could get a drink from 1 p.m. to 3 p.m. and between 8 p.m. and 10 p.m. But then provision was made for bona fide travellers — and a traveller who was not resident within a mile of the hotel. Anyone who was not prepared to walk a mile for a beer could hardly have been very thirsty; therefore, in effect, hotels were open all day Sunday.<a href="#6"><sup>6</sup></a>

In 1877 South Australia had a Royal Commission on Sunday Trading. Parliament moved progressively towards Local Option. The legislators began gently in 1881. The Temperance Act declared they wanted more hotels, but little harm was caused because most bars could be built just outside the vote zone. In 1885 the anti-drink forces rallied. They united all the forces from the Rechabites and Good Templars to the Salvation Army and formed the Local Option League.

In the elections of 1889, the League worked progressively towards Local Option. However the licensed victuallers fought back and they erected their own large calico sign. The spot they chose was strategic: at the intersection of King and Castlereagh Streets in Sydney, where there were hotels on all four corners. Their sign read:

**VOTE FOR THE DEVIL AND LOCAL OPTION**

It would be a mistake to say on whose side the devil was working. Local Option did not come to Sydney in 1885. But it did come to Queensland. In that year the reformers moved. Local Option polls could decide: (a) reduction, (b) increase, (c) no licence. The no-licence option needed only a two-thirds majority, but Queenslanders never proved themselves enthusiastic for prohibition. Nor did the West Australians, although they had opportunities to vote in 1911, 1921, and 1950.

In 1888, Victoria had an ingenious idea for the liquor problem. Sir Graham Berry introduced a Licensing Bill which fixed a statutory number of hotels for each district, one for every 250 persons up to 1,200 and one for every 500 thereafter. The Temperance men were jubilant at the idea, but there was a catch. One-fifth of the ratepayers could petition for a ballot and then vote for a reduction to the statutory number. The trick was simple — it was up to the publican and his family and his customers to keep away from the poll. Folk at Port Melbourne, South Melbourne and Williamstown made it a supreme test and the trade could not get 25 per cent of the ratepayers; absentees total or otherwise. The Berry Bill, as it was known, was the result.

The Victorian Alliance rallied and prepared a 'monster' petition to be signed by every female they could find, a scroll of petition that finished with the plea that the Liquor Traffic be reformed. Alfred Deakin took it into the Assembly. It was a roll 1,860 feet long.<a href="#16"><sup>16</sup></a>

**Six O'Clock Closing**

Six o'clock closing in Australia is now only a memory, but one of the richest and most colourful memories in our history. It was unbelievably grim and gruesome, and it lasted for over half a century, from 1915 to 1966.<a href="#28"><sup>28</sup></a>

Imagine the scene, a large room with a cold, lavatory-like atmosphere, no chairs, no tables, no frills of any kind. The bar is filled with shouting men who have snatched a few minutes from their work in order to get a drink; the choice was one beer or nothing, and as a point of honour each man must shout — that is, each man must buy a round of drinks. This is the very heart of six o'clock closing. The scene in the last moments before an execution, there are always people in the school who tend to want to get out of the place. Getting everybody served becomes a desperate affair.

At 5.15 p.m. into the crowd, after fifty minutes of pushing, even the most skilled human beings and barmaids, all equipped with the fastest taps on plate-glass counters, a system designed to distribute two thousand handfuls of change, find that five to six is the hour of the beast. Five to six is when the bar is ten deep with pushing bodies, all thumping hands towards the barmaids: 'Here!—Here!—For Chrissake here!' You know this barmaid pretty well, and she serves you first, but then you have to shout for your mates. All infinitely more hazardous with each passing moment. There are dozens of men as desperate as you. Yet amongst the canny there was an alternative to this. Immediately upon entering the bar at 5.30 p.m., a man stood up and bought five sheer pints one at a time and had no further trouble. The six o'clock swill was the most effective hotel-killing machine of all time.

Six o'clock closing came to New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia and Tasmania during the First World War, and it lasted for forty-nine years. During the war, Sir Alexander Peacock said that the early closing of hotel bars would help defeat the Kaiser. The Victorian Chief Secretary made this quite clear. Temperance organisations had been fighting for shorter trading hours for thirty years and while they had been unable to get this measure through in the years before the war — the volume of licensing legislation between 1900 and 1914 was the most controversial in Parliament — the mood of the war gave them that extra talking point.

Australia was not alone in this. There was a wave of patriotic anti-drink fervour, and the example came from the highest places in London. In April 1915, King George V banned wines, spirits and beer from the Royal Household for the duration of the war. His private secretary, Lord Stamfordham, announced: 'His Majesty is concerned, that the rich and poor may have equal cause to share the sacrifice.'

The *Argus* reported that the fact of His Majesty's example had caused a remarkable anti-drink uprising throughout the United Kingdom. A majority of the shipyard workers in Glasgow had refused to go into public houses. Nearly all the members of the Asquith cabinet in London had intimated that they would follow the King's example, and announcements were made by leading Government officials and judges that they would abstain. Mr T. E. Varley was giving a lecture at the Temperance Hall entitled 'The World's War With Drink'. Mr Varley directed attention to the cabled message and the audience sang the national anthem and gave three cheers for His Majesty.

'We are fighting Germany, Austria and DRINK, and the greatest of these is DRINK,' cried the Reverend at the Manly Methodist Christian Temperance Union, and it was in agreement with this remark that they sang a Maori haka on the war wagon.

The *White Ribbon Signal*, official journal of the Women's Christian Temperance Union, was in ecstasy about all this. NOTICE — NO MORE BOOZE UNTIL THE WAR IS OVER. Soldiers with their tongues hanging out will be singing: 'It's a long way to Tipperary.'

The *Bulletin* was horrified. The so-called Anti-Shouting Bill, or 'Licensed Premises Act', was proposed in the Legislative Council. Its operative clause was this: 'No person shall sell or supply liquor to any person on licensed premises, for consumption on the premises, unless the liquor is ordered and paid for by the person to whom it is supplied, and by whom it is consumed or intended to be consumed. Penalty £5.'

The *Bulletin* was horrified: 'Without the shouting the liquor traffic will come to look something like a chemist's shop with a different smell, and the delightful chief customers will be sent solitary to the temple of long communing in silence with their own thoughts, until, in course of time, they fall down or are carried away to look forward to the friendless night.'

'None!' shout a number in the crowd, and then one identity standing near round says — 'John, you're right, every word you say is gospel. Look at me, there's the hide, and you can see right through her clothing. While one round have I had a bit and my boots are down at heels, but I'm going by, and to you know I've got brains.'

'By crippy you have,' says John, 'and what's the trouble?'

'The beer!'

'You're right again, John, but I'm wrong on it, so I can't fill. Shut the bars altogether. Yes, I want to fight it out and I'm glad to have a chance to do it.'<a href="#41"><sup>41</sup></a>

'Hear! Hear!' from the crowd.

It was an easy victory for the 6 o'clock closing, the assault on cotton socks had begun and Temperance organisations everywhere were thrilled.

The *White Ribbon Signal* commented: 'Apart from Prohibition it is probably the fairest reform ever obtained in the Empire. England, in spite of Lloyd George and the Temperance Party, now shows at last that King Alcohol and all his acquiescent cause are in retreat.'<a href="#42"><sup>42</sup></a>

*An Anzac or a Shirker*

The hotels were closed on the Saturday for the first time. Melbourne Hotels were closed from 6 p.m. on the Monday following. According to *Melbourne Punch* it was the driest weekend Victoria had ever known.

<!-- Pages 113–117 covering the NSW and Tasmanian referendums, the vote on closing hours in those states, and the passage of the legislation through the various colonial parliaments, are partially illegible in the scan. -->

In New South Wales the referendum on 12 December 1923 resulted in an absolute majority of 138,485 votes for six o'clock closing.

Yet the liquor situation was never allowed to become wholly inactive. According to the rules of the Licensing Act there was to be a Local Option vote at the first general election after 1 January 1920. The electors had the choice of: no licence, continuance, or reduction — and whether hotels in their districts should continue, be reduced, or close. The result in Victoria was:

Continuance of licences: 278,577

For no licence: 127,234

For reduction: 36,025

If the Anti-Liquor forces wanted to win a district for total prohibition, they had to score 60 per cent of the votes, and out of 20 licensing districts in Victoria this happened only in 2: Booroondara and the neighbouring district of Nunawading.

This was a fascinating local study. Booroondara included, and still does, the City of Camberwell, taking in Canterbury and Surrey Hills. Camberwell now has a population of over 100,000 and it is Melbourne's biggest city. A Monash University lecturer once described Burke Road as 'the longest stretch of well-stuffed suburbia in the world'.<a href="#50"><sup>50</sup></a>

Geoffrey Blainey in *A History of Camberwell* wrote that here was the place for the small farmers, tradesmen and city businessmen, closer to middle-class landholding than any other part of Melbourne. The land was cheap, and there was a bevy of foundational most disastrous financial depression it had ever seen.

James Munro had come to Camberwell in 1888. The man who had launched the great temperance dream in bricks and mortar — Coffee Palaces, the Federal Bank, building societies — had lost most of what he had. By the end of 1893, Munro, who had held Armidale House in Kooyong Road, was starting a small estate agency, doing his rounds on a second-hand bicycle. Camberwell went dry in 1920, six o'clock closing notwithstanding, precisely because men like Munro had so thoroughly shaped its character over the preceding three decades.

The Option polls were only held every nine years. In 1922 the Government decided that no new licence should be available in Camberwell at any time. This founded, and the Local Option polls did continue to be held every nine years instead of three. The polls in 1929 and 1938 both voted to remain dry; it was not until 1946 that the majority in Camberwell slipped below 60 per cent.

<!-- A passage on pp. 122–123 covering the continuing struggle over licensing in other Melbourne suburbs and in New South Wales through the 1920s and 1930s is partially illegible in the scan. -->

In 1952 there was a licensing Royal Commission. Mr Justice Maxwell was appointed. It was not quite the same as the previous Royal Commission because this one actually listened to some surprising witnesses. The *Argus* was right behind the show. Among those invited were Mr Kevin Coleman, assisting the Commission, and Mrs Coleman, Mr F. E. G. Fogarty, chairman of the Carlton Brewery, Mr S. W. Johnson, senior lecturer in Criminology, Mr S. S. Sharp of the Wine and Brandy Producers' Association, the Reverend D. Hume of the Presbyterian Church of Christ.

Before dinner there was a choice of mulligatawny soup or minestrone. The next course was fresh water trout, which was served with Rhine Castle Victorian Bin 42.

<!-- A passage on pp. 126–127 covering the Commission's dinner and P.D. Phillips' police breath-testing experiment is partially illegible in the scan. -->

Mr Phillips was pleased with the whole affair. Everyone drank varying amounts of alcohol, but no one consumed more than the equivalent of two and a half seven-ounce beers. So it proved that if liquor was consumed slowly in relaxed conditions in a warm atmosphere and with food, a person might manage very well indeed.

Yet the most interesting thing that happened was the change of mind of the Reverend John Westerman. Mr Westerman was secretary of the Victorian Temperance Alliance and he was representing the Church of England, the Presbyterians, the Methodists, the Churches of Christ, the Congregational Church, the Baptists and the Salvation Army before the Commission. On 15 April, insisting on the continuance of 6 o'clock closing. After listening to the evidence for two months, he had come to the conclusion that the hours of trading had little to do with how much a person consumed. 'Six o'clock closing, drinking too much — men had a tendency to gulp. It was against the clock, drinking too much in a limited time,' he said. 'We have become convinced that our case was not solid enough. The only honest thing to do was to give evidence to the Commission and say so.' The Reverend M. C. Trenholme, superintendent of the Methodist Social Service Department, said Westerman's expression of neutrality was 'one of the most serious setbacks received by the temperance cause in years'.<a href="#58"><sup>58</sup></a> Yet the Methodist Conference in October also switched to the Westerman policy.<a href="#59"><sup>59</sup></a>

Now there was very little to stop the launching of a new era. Even the most timid members of Government were now wary of further restrictive legislation. The referendum in Victoria was held on Saturday, 10 February 1966. As in Sydney, there was no carnage on the roads, no drama, no excitement. Some of the suburban hotels like the Tower Hotel, at the Cathedral Hotel, among those who opened their doors, called for 10 o'clock closing. And it came to Melbourne on 1 March 1966. It was quiet at the Cathedral Hotel at 9 p.m. Of course, South Australia could not accept as truth anything that had taken place in New South Wales and Victoria. Its findings were similar to all the other Royal Commissions; honour was satisfied. Every night now there was the exodus from dry South Australia into wet Victoria.

Mr Donald Dunstan, Premier of South Australia, launched his new licensing bill which provided for the longest hours in Australia — 9 a.m. to 10 p.m. In March 1967, ten o'clock closing came quietly and smoothly to South Australia.

The Kaiser had been defeated. The lights in the bars were on again, all around Australia.
