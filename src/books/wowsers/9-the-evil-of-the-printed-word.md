The Victorian era was only moral on its beautifully ornamented exterior. It was prepared to tolerate all kinds of sex so long as nobody wrote about it, or talked about it. There were laws to deal with obscenity, but the censorship of books did not become a real issue until the full flowering of prudery in the eighties, and from then on there was always the fear that the young would be corrupted by what they read.

On 17 May 1888, William Whitehouse Collins was convicted and fined £5.5 by a Sydney magistrate for selling a pamphlet entitled, 'The Law of Population: Its consequences and its bearing upon human conduct and morals'. It was by Mrs Annie Besant, who had already become famous in England for being a freethinker and an advocate of birth control. 'The Law of Population' described the poverty of the East End of London, the starvation, the over-crowding, the misery caused by excessively large families. It told how parents brought into the world families of eight, ten or more children, then found it impossible to adequately house or feed them. It told how this caused a complete destruction of morals, how it created disease, how it created crime and a general debasement of society. Mrs Besant said the answer was birth control and in chapter three of her pamphlet, in precise, clinical terms, she described how to go about it, which, of course, included the description of the sex act and the necessary equipment.

The point that horrified good people at this time was the thought that anyone should indulge in sex for mere pleasure. Prosecuting counsel said: 'The very *ratio essendi* of this book is immoral, because its purpose is to enable persons to indulge their passions and yet avoid what is the normal consequence, in fact the natural complement of such indulgence; and lastly, the book is likely in its effects to be ruinous to the morals of the community by teaching persons how they can sin without fear of detection.'

The case went before the Supreme Court of New South Wales, where the decision was in favour of Collins. First there was a dissenting judgement from the Chief Justice, Sir Frederick Darley. He told the history of Mrs Besant, how with a man named Charles Bradlaugh she had published a similar book, entitled *Fruits of Philosophy.* They were prosecuted before the Chief Justice of England and a jury in June 1877, and found guilty. The conviction was set aside on a technicality and *Fruits of Philosophy,* which had been selling at the rate of 1,000 a year, vastly multiplied its circulation. Sir Frederick pointed out that in 1878 Mrs Besant came before the court in an application for the custody of her eight-year-old daughter. It was refused, and the Chief Justice gave the words of the English appeal judge: 'I do not think I should be right in saying that it would be beneficial for any young girl to be brought up by such a woman and I think I would be guilty of a dereliction of duty if I allowed a young girl to be brought up and educated in that way'.<a href="#1"><sup>1</sup></a>

The Senior Puisne Judge, Judge Windeyer, then gave his judgment, which quickly became famous in Australian law. From London, the *Westminster Review* commented:

The Judgment by Mr Justice Windeyer . . . is, to my mind, one of the noblest judicial pronouncements ever delivered in a court of justice. It is animated by the very spirit of enlightened wisdom, and well indeed would it be for this country if we had more often upon the English bench men not only as wise and enlightened as this colonial judge, but also of equal courage in giving fearless and outspoken exposition of the great principles of freedom and justice.<a href="#2"><sup>2</sup></a>

Here are some quotations from the judgment which were brave indeed in 1888:

The prevention of conception may be secured by total sexual abstinence, or by sexual abstinence at certain periods of female life, and it is therefore idle to argue that the mere suggestion that families should be limited by the prevention of conception is an idea that is itself obscene, unless it is contended that to advise total sexual abstinence is itself obscene.

No natural function of the human body is obscene in itself. In the physical constitution of man, including all his natural instincts, there is nothing unholy or unclean. Only in the diseased mind of the unnaturally living ascetic, with his distorted views of religion did God's handiwork in the human frame become an object of shame and disgust.

The time is past when knowledge can be kept as the exclusive privilege of any caste or class. The fact that a book may excite prurient thoughts if used for that purpose by the low-minded and the young does not make it obscene . . . No one thinks of prosecuting a vendor of Lempriere's Dictionary or the Bible Society as publishers of obscene books.

That nature has formed us with organs and propensities, which, if abused, lead to the ruin of health and the destruction of morals is no imputation upon the wisdom of God in so constituting man. The same argument could be used with equal force against the teaching of writing and the art of photography because they assist people to commit forgery.

Judge Windeyer's judgment could have been compulsory reading for literary censors for the next eighty years, but alas, it was not. The books which really started the long, weary battle of literary censorship were the so-called 'Naturalist School' of French novels, which began with Gustave Flaubert's *Madame Bovary* and led to the works of Daudet, Zola, Dumas, *fils,* and the rest. They dealt with the lives of prostitutes, with bedroom passion, and worst of all, with women who preferred other men to their husbands.

In order to condemn them thoroughly, Melbourne's *Daily Telegraph* quoted an English judgment that they were 'soaked and poisoned by a beastly sensuality'. There was a suggestion in most of the condemning articles that the greatest danger was that all these French novels were simply the most exciting reading that was around. The *Telegraph* continued: 'We object to having the Pacific poisoned by the deporting of French criminals to an island 2,500 miles distant from our shores. Are we prepared to allow the more subtle and deadly infection of French literary vice to be emptied upon our bookstalls?'<a href="#3"><sup>3</sup></a>

The *Argus,* always a great protector of morals, felt that Emil Zola's *Nana* and *Pot Bouille* were real offences against humanity, and that even amongst the none-too-squeamish, Zola was really more than anyone could stand. But why was Zola like this? The *Argus* had the answer. 'His fondness for filth is due in a great measure to his Italian blood, poisoned by the corrupt atmosphere of a French Lyceum.' Then the *Argus* put another question. Were the French really abnormally depraved? But then it came up with the thought that publications like this did less harm in France because the young there did not have the same 'independence of freedom of intercourse' that they enjoyed in England.<a href="#4"><sup>4</sup></a>

The full story of literary censorship in Australia is well told in Peter Coleman's book, *Obscenity, Blasphemy, Sedition.* One of the first acts of aggressive literary censorship came in July 1889, when the Victorian Customs descended on Coles Book Arcade in Melbourne and confiscated a consignment of 162 of those French naturalist novels, including Daudet's *Sapho* and Guy de Maupassant's *A Lady's Man.*

There was an onslaught from the newspapers, not against the Customs department, but in protection of Melburnian morality. Even the *Age,* which did wonder that such matters were left in charge of Customs, said: 'Probably every sane person agrees that a good deal of Zola is not only filthy but revolting',<a href="#5"><sup>5</sup></a> and Cole himself agreed to have the books destroyed. As Peter Coleman records: 'It was a moral victory for the Customs Department and an era of deputations to the Minister for Customs demanding prohibition of other novels and novelettes began.'

Mr Cole, however, did express a thought that was to become the tortured cry of the bookseller for evermore. How was a bookseller to know what was in the mind of the Minister before he imported his books? If they were going to ban Daudet and Zola, then they might as well be consistent and ban Boccaccio, Rabelais, Fielding, Smollett, Balzac, Swinburne and all the rest that had been coming in for years.<a href="#6"><sup>6</sup></a> But the Customs Department was the arbiter of good taste, and if did not do a good job, then the *Argus* was quite capable of stepping in.

Melbourne's moral tone in the eighteen-nineties, as exhibited for public view, was such a pure and beautiful thing, one had to believe that babies did not come into the world by sexual contact, they were delivered on Thursdays by the Mutual Store. One man who discovered this was Professor George William Marshall Hall.

In 1890 Marshall Hall arrived in Australia to become first Ormond Professor of Music at the University of Melbourne. He was only twenty-eight, son of a surgeon, and grandson of the physiologist, Marshall Hall. He had studied music in Berlin and at the Royal College of Music in London. He was brilliant, some called him a genius, and he is still remembered for the enormous impression he made on the musical life of Melbourne. He began work in 1891, but immediately realized that he could do little without a Conservatorium, and this he started in 1895. It was a big success; his students always described him as an inspiring teacher.<a href="#7"><sup>7</sup></a>

He must have found the colonial atmosphere stifling, for he took a delight in throwing rocks through cultural windows. The first major window-smashing took place on 1 August 1898. He made a speech which would have seemed rather extreme even for Adolph Hitler. He was at the Melbourne Town Hall, and it was his official duty to present a baton to the acting conductor of the Melbourne Liedertafel, Mr August Siede. He said that to his mind the baton was like a field marshal's baton, and suddenly away he went on an incredible martial tirade:

Life means war and war is a good thing. Nay! the best of all things. It is a sympton of vitality, energy, super abundant strength. It determines the worth of men. It differentiates the bold man from the coward; the capable from the incapable; the commander from the demagogue; the aristocrat by instinct from the plebeian by instinct; strength from weakness; the full blooded from the degenerate; the virile from the emasculate. Peace is for invalids and parsons.

How healthy it is to see, even in the midst of our modern, puling, knock-kneed, pseudo religiousness, the vast armaments of nations preparing for the struggle for power; to see the enormous intellectual output of scientists, artists, philosophers—all bursting with energy, vitality, hostility.<a href="#8"><sup>8</sup></a>

He went on like this for some time, while the audience sat stunned. After all, he was the Ormond Professor of Music, and the late Hon. Francis Ormond, who had made his chair possible, had been a God-fearing, Bible-loving Presbyterian elder.

Eventually his speech attacked the stuffiness he detested:

There is something profoundly absurd in the modern petticoat movement—in these dear creatures, with their nerves, their exquisite sensibilities, their hysterics, and scent bottles and artistic yearnings and, above all, their mission. For the modern woman is dying to play the part of savior to unregenerate man—to make him mild and moral. He must not read naughty books, oh dear no! And she shall see that he is tucked up in bed at half past eight o'clock out of harm's way . . . He must conquer or perish. In truth, there is nothing in life worth having but what is gained by conquest, and held by main force.

The *Argus* leader writers were non-plussed. 'To make this speech artistically complete the Professor should have tattooed himself and executed a war dance at appropriate intervals throughout his oration.' The *Argus* even suggested that plainly he was in need of medical treatment.<a href="#9"><sup>9</sup></a>

It was all very well to advocate war and bloodshed—that barely raised comment—but to advocate sex was something far, far worse and that brought down the roof. A few days later he brought out a book entitled *Hymns Ancient and Modern.* It was a collection of poems, and as he was a non-believer, even the title was a gentle piece of sacrilege. In the introduction he mocked the idea of an afterlife and pointed out that the only thing really eternal was sex.

Unconsciously to those of lower intellect, to those of the higher, consciously, the mutual caresses of lovers are the physical expression of the deepest intellectual truth. In the supremest moment of passion the ego is annihilated. They are one—emblem of the eternal principle of all Life, all Beauty—Love, which for a single moment blends the phenomenon Man with the phenomenon Woman. It is but a symbol of Death, which shall make him eternally one with the entire Universe. Art presents to us the sexual relations in this their real, innate, lasting significance—as a beautiful and splendid allegory of the most sought after of all truths. But it is the misfortune of our times that such works are open also to the prurient, the immodest, the eternally vulgar.

The *Argus* carried a news-review item headed A PUBLIC SCANDAL. The title, said the *Argus* had been insultingly appropriated from a well-known hymnal to cover a collection of verses, which to use mild language, could be described as immodest and irreverent. Any father would have to keep the volume away from his family.

And working up to a passion, the *Argus* said: 'We shall certainly not lend our columns to the immodest and lascivious verses. The attention of those concerned may however be called to such a piece as that printed on page 10. If this is considered moral the writer has strange ideas about morality. If it is considered manly, he has still stranger ideas about manliness. There is a lewdness which is insulting to womanhood, which causes the gorge of the true man to rise; it is such a lewdness which these lines contain . . . the reality is a Circe like animalism.'

There was a flurry of letters. 'A PUBLISHER' said the 'low and blasphemous doggerel should be withdrawn from publication'. 'A VICTORIAN MOTHER', anxious that her children should grow up pure and strong in mind and body, wanted Marshall Hall removed from his position as Ormond Professor. 'IN LOCO PARENTIS' said that Mr Marshall Hall obtruded upon a much-tried community his coarse atheism and erotic suggestions. He appeared to have saturated himself with the decadent tone of Charles the Second's reign.

This was the poem on page ten of *Hymns Ancient and Modern* which so horrified Melbourne and caused manly *Argus* gorges to rise

<p style="text-align:center"><strong>SAPPHICS</strong></p>

<blockquote>
Soft the air blows here as the sighs of lovers<br>

When twixt sleep and wake nodding wavelets droop and Drowsily murmur:<br>

</blockquote>

<blockquote>

There was one other rather rude poem titled 'To a Parson, on His Refusing a Glass of Wine with Contumely':

<blockquote>

And has more in it of the true divine<br>

Or mine!<a href="#10"><sup>10</sup></a>
</blockquote>

Only *Melbourne Punch* and the *Bulletin* came to Marshall Hall's defence. The *Bulletin* deliberately printed the poetry that others were too shocked to print and it called the *Argus* 'an illiterate old maid'.<a href="#11"><sup>11</sup></a>

Next the Professor was summoned to appear before a meeting of the University Council. Among the members of the Council were the Anglican Bishop of Melbourne, Dr Goe and the headmaster of Scotch College, Dr Morrison. The atmosphere was frosty. Marshall Hall was equally cool. He refused to accept the invitation, replying: 'There is no toleration and no freedom where men must echo conventional views of life, religion and politics or hold their peace.' He thereupon asked for twelve months' leave of absence and at the end of that time he promised to hand in his resignation.<a href="#12"><sup>12</sup></a>

The Council accepted this on the understanding that Marshall Hall 'abstained from giving musical instruction in Victoria'. This time Marshall Hall was really angry. He withdrew his resignation and pointed out that section 23 of the University Act of 1890 read: 'No religious test shall be administered to any person in order to entitle him to be admitted as a student of the said University, or to hold office therein, or to graduate thereat, or to hold any advantage or privilege thereof.' Clearly Marshall Hall was ready to take legal action.<a href="#13"><sup>13</sup></a>

The Council then made a statement referring to Marshall Hall's 'libidinous poems', 'the ostentatious parade of disbelief in Christianity', all tending 'to make parents shrink from allowing their children to attend the teachings of the University', but then went on to say that Marshall Hall had pledged to refrain from such conduct in the future and he would remain until the end of the term of his appointment in 1900.<a href="#14"><sup>14</sup></a>

The *Argus* was horrified. The Congregational Union was horrified, and the horrified Presbyterian Assembly passed a resolution expressing abhorrence of Marshall Hall's writings, and regret that he had not been dismissed immediately.<a href="#15"><sup>15</sup></a>

In June 1900, Marshall Hall—now a big name in music in Australia—came up for re-appointment. There was a rallying of forces pro and anti Marshall Hall. Leading the antis was the *Argus,* and it gathered resolutions from all over Victoria. On 12 June, Trinity College council 'emphatically protested'. The Presbyterian Ladies' college 'unanimously protested'. On 13 June, the Council of Melbourne Grammar 'emphatically protested'. On 14 June the council of Wesley College 'unanimously resolved to emphatically protest'. On 16 June the Methodist Ladies' College 'protested' and the council of Geelong Grammar 'viewed the reappointment with consternation'. Scotch College 'emphatically protested' and the Elders of the Presbyterian Church of Victoria 'begged to emphatically protest.'

A massive deputation was organized to wait on the University Council on 18 June. Dr Leeper, warden of Trinity College, led the deputation, and entered a protest on behalf of eighty girls' schools and colleges in Melbourne and Geelong, warning that the deputation represented institutions which supplied ninety to ninety-five per cent of the students at Melbourne University. The *Argus* commented: 'If the council were to yield to unseen influences and to quietly ignore the crushing condemnation which it has placed on its record it would stultify itself and it would injure the University.'<a href="#16"><sup>16</sup></a>

All the Professors of Melbourne University but two (one was away and another had just been re-appointed) signed a statement in favor of Marshall Hall, and there was a counter deputation which included the artists Tom Roberts, Fred McCubbin and John Longstaff. They said Marshall Hall had lifted the colony high in the musical world.

The council sat in private, but meanwhile 100 University students sat downstairs. They gave three cheers for Professor Marshall Hall and three groans for the Leeper deputation, and then they sang, 'For Hall's a Jolly Good Fellow'. When the Leeper deputation went into the council room, the Vice Chancellor ordered that the students be put out, but this was beyond anybody's powers, and they continued to give cheers and groans.

The votes tied even, nine for Marshall Hall, including most of the professors and nine against, including Bishop Goe, Mr Murray Smith, M.L.A., Dr Morrison of Scotch College, the Reverend Professor Andrew Harper of Ormond College, and Dr MacFarland, master of Ormond. The casting vote was in the hands of the Vice Chancellor, Sir Henry Wrixon. He gave it with the 'Nos', and the motion to reappoint Professor Marshall Hall was lost.

So Marshall Hall departed, and a number of his teaching staff resigned in sympathy. He then devoted all his attention to the Conservatorium which he had started. No longer was it connected with the School of Music: now it was the Marshall Hall Conservatorium, later to be the Melbourne Conservatorium.<a href="#17"><sup>17</sup></a> In 1912 he went home to London and in 1914 he was offered back his old job as Ormond Professor of Music, to resume work again in 1915. But it was too late; he died of appendicitis on 18 July of that year.

Come Federation, the Federal Customs was able to take a hand in protecting the purity of Australian minds, and in July 1901 they seized a consignment of French novels destined for the book sellers George Robertson & Co., in Little Collins Street, Melbourne. They were charged with having imported prohibited books contrary to sections 49 and 210 of the Customs Act. It was 'those French books' again: this time Balzac's *Droll Stories* and Paul de Kock's *Monsieur Dupont.*

Such books, said Mr W. H. Croker for the prosecution in the City Court, were literary garbage of the worst kind. They had no literary qualities to redeem them and the importers had endeavored to make money by catering for the tastes of a certain depraved class.

He called some high-powered witnesses. There was James Smith, described in the Court as a littérateur for the past forty years. He thought Balzac's *Droll Stories* were an 'elaborate panegyric of licentiousness and lust'. Edmund Armstrong, librarian of the Melbourne Public Library, agreed with this.

Another witness was Edward Ellis Morris, professor of the Professorial Board at Melbourne University. Professor Morris said that he had looked through both books and he thought they were thoroughly indecent. In his opinion they corrupted morals. Balzac's *Droll Stories* should not be in any library. The book should be destroyed.

'I felt I defiled my mind by reading them. I needed a bath after it.' (*Laughter*).

*Counsel:* 'Was your moral nature in any way affected by it?'

*Professor Morris:* 'I don't think it lowered my moral tone because I was on my guard.'

*Counsel:* 'Time will only tell whether you have fallen from your high estate.' (*Laughter*).

*Professor Morris:* 'Yes.'

The manager of the book department at Robertson's, Mr James B. Symonds said they had been importing *Droll Stories* for nineteen years and nobody had ever objected before. Also *Monsieur Dupont* had been coming in for a long time, and the suggestion was put to the Court that they had no knowledge of anyone's morals having been corrupted.

The Magistrate, Mr Panton, made it clear he was unimpressed by the whole affair, and he fined Robertson's the minimum under the law, £25 without costs.<a href="#18"><sup>18</sup></a>

Yet these were quiet years. It was not until 1929-30 that censorship moved into high gear; eventually Australia was to acquire an international reputation on a par with Ireland's, as the moral literary capital of the world. Between 1929 and 1936 there was a remarkable range of bannings, from James Joyce's *Ulysses* to Defoe's *Moll Flanders.* Also on the list were Aldous Huxley's *Brave New World,* Hemingway's *Farewell to Arms,* Orwell's *Down and Out in Paris,* Erskine Caldwell's *God's Little Acre,* Richard Aldington's *All Men Are Enemies,* Colette's *The Gentle Libertine,* John O'Hara's *Appointment in Samarra*—young minds were very well guarded.

The Melbourne *Herald,* on 14 April 1930, gave warning that Norman Lindsay's novel, *Redheap,* was dangerous. The scene of the book, it said, was a decayed mining town near Ballarat, obviously the home town of the Lindsays, Creswick. 'It is not a nice book to leave on the family bookshelf for all to read,' said the *Herald* London correspondent primly.

He predicted well. By 22 May 1930, *Redheap* was banned by the Minister for Customs, Mr Forde, and the *Argus* approved heartily: 'Australia has too little as yet of what is really to be prized; such foulness as besmirches it should be kept in its gutters, if no more can be done with it.'

Norman Lindsay was in for a bad time. Next his novel, *The Cautious Amorist,* was banned, and prosecution was launched against *Art in Australia* magazine for running an issue of the saucy Lindsay nudes.

Lindsay was furious, and he announced that he was leaving for the United States. 'I am disgusted with the treatment I have received in Australia,' he said. 'I am sick and tired of this wowseristic country.'<a href="#20"><sup>20</sup></a> Actually Lindsay did not stay away for very long, but the censor earned a mention in his next novel, *Miracles.* The book opened with Walter Gresham and his architect partner, Floyd, discussing Australian shortcomings:

'You see what's happening; the people are rationally sober, and prostitutes hardly exist. The wowser's secret ritual of debauch is gone; he is turning the spleen of his repressed lust on an effort to destroy free expression in the arts; look at these imbecile censorships of books and motion pictures.'

'Yes, very funny,' admits Floyd. 'I see Shakespeare's Venus and Adonis was censored the other day as an indecent work.'

Gresham replied: 'Yes, it is funny. Funny as the spectacle of a people morally damned can be funny.'

Later he returns to the attack: 'The only sign of activity I can discover here is a determination to destroy anything that might lead to a tradition. Your intelligent minority is invertebrate; it lets the lowest type of official moron wipe his boots on it. Policemen as the arbiters of our culture! Lord! What a country!'

In 1933 there was a Book Censorship Advisory Committee to advise the Customs Minister, and in 1937 an even grander Literature Censorship Board. Many books previously banned, including *Ulysses,* were released. Yet the Minister was all-powerful; he was never bound to take the advice of his Board.

Indeed some very odd things happened. Suddenly in 1941, at a very grave time in the Second World War, the Minister for Customs, Harrison, re-imposed the ban on *Ulysses,* as a result of some complaints. 'This book', he said, 'holds up to ridicule the Creator and the Church. It ridicules the whole moral standard of civilisation, citizenship and decency.'<a href="#21"><sup>21</sup></a> Reporters asked him whether he realized that many critics regarded *Ulysses* as the outstanding novel of the twentieth century. Mr Harrison said, yes, he did, and if some of the newspapers did not think the book obscene he challenged them to print a few selected extracts.<a href="#22"><sup>22</sup></a> The *Sydney Morning Herald* was one newspaper that gracefully declined. It said: 'The point is so palpably absurd as to be undeserving of serious comment.'<a href="#23"><sup>23</sup></a>

Mr Harrison conceded one point. The ban on *Ulysses* was not complete, he said. Universities and students of literature who could prove their bona fides could still import the book but there would be a ban on its sale in bookshops.<a href="#24"><sup>24</sup></a>

Immediately after the Second World War there were two famous court actions. The books involved were *Love Me Sailor* by Robert S. Close and *We Were the Rats* by Lawson Glassop. *Love Me Sailor,* which could hardly be described as a good novel, was the story of a lone nymphomaniac aboard a windjammer. Mr Justice Martin in the Criminal Court in Melbourne fined Close £100 and sentenced him to three months' imprisonment. The publishers, Georgian House Pty Ltd, received a £500 fine.

Passing sentence, Mr Justice Martin said: 'You have been found guilty of publishing a book containing obscene matter. I feel little doubt that you selected the plot you did because it gave you opportunities for salacious writing which you considered would appeal to a number of minds in this community which would result in a large sale. The morals of the community, especially the youth of the community are to be safeguarded. I regard your book as a gross assault on that morality.'<a href="#25"><sup>25</sup></a>

Robert Close was led away in handcuffs, and many people were appalled at the sentence. Had he blown a safe or assaulted someone with a broken bottle in Collingwood, he would have received kinder treatment. As the *Sun News Pictorial* said: 'Few will feel comfortable when they think of Australia being known abroad as a country where an author can be manacled and treated as a potentially dangerous criminal.'<a href="#26"><sup>26</sup></a>

The Court of Criminal Appeal later wiped out the goal sentence, and raised the fine from £100 to £150. The publishers had their fine reduced to £300.<a href="#27"><sup>27</sup></a> The court action was a terrible financial blow to Close; but the *Argus* reported, three years later, that the book was making a small fortune in Europe. A German translation published in Munich yielded 4,000 dollars for the first 20,000 copies in advance royalties, and the novel had sold 40,000 copies in France. There was a special note on the dust jacket: 'The author of this book went to gaol'. <a href="#28"><sup>28</sup></a> Yet perhaps he should have claimed damages, for the book received the all-clear from the Department of Customs on 23 November 1960.

Lawson Glassop's war novel about men besieged in Tobruk, *We Were the Rats,* had been on sale for seventeen months and had sold 10,000 copies by the time Mr Farrington S.M. declared it obscene in the Central Summons Court, Sydney and fined Angus and Robertson Ltd £10 with 8/- costs. The book had received very good reviews and the acting state president of the R.S.L., Mr Bolton, commented: 'To classify *We Were the Rats* as obscene literature is the height of absurdity. This book portrays the day-to-day life of Australian soldiers living under the constant strain of war. It would lose all realism if it were pruned to schoolgirl standards.'<a href="#29"><sup>29</sup></a>

The case is remembered for a remarkable exchange between Mr Dovey K.C. and a Sergeant of the Sydney Vice Squad, who said that part of his job included going round seeking obscene literature, but he admitted that he did not read much.

*Mr Dovey:* Have you ever heard of Chaucer?

*Sgt.:* No.

*Mr Dovey:* You did not hear of him while in the Vice Squad.

*Sgt.:* No.

*Mr Dovey:* Have you ever heard of Byron?

*Sgt.:* No.

*Mr Dovey:* He was a Lord?

*Sgt.:* Yes, I've heard of him.

*Mr. Dovey:* Do you know if he was on Lord Mountbatten's staff?

*Sgt.:* I don't know.

*Mr Dovey:* Do you know if he was a writer?

*Sgt.:* I don't know.

*Mr Dovey:* Do you know if he was a war correspondent?

*Sgt.:* I don't know.

*Mr Dovey:* Have you heard of Shelley?

*Sgt.:* I know a man named Shelly, but I take it you refer to an author or something. I have never heard of the name Shelley as a man who wrote anything.<a href="#30"><sup>30</sup></a>

An appeal to the Supreme Court was dismissed, and from then on *We Were the Rats* was selling under the counter for £5 a copy. Yet here again what was obscene in 1948 was causing no depravity a few years later and it was released as a paperback by Horwitz publications in 1961.

The agony for the bookseller, the university lecturer, or say, even the pornographer, was this: only a few people knew what was actually banned. There was a list of banned books, but this too was banned, top secret, locked away in the vaults of the Customs. The theory was, presumably, if evil minds knew what was dirty, they would find a way of buying it. In December 1952 John Hetherington of the *Argus* got hold of this secret list, not as formidable as the Irish official list which one time covered 2,000 titles, but formidable enough, just the same.<a href="#31"><sup>31</sup></a> On the Customs list were such titles as Alberto Moravia's *The Woman of Rome,* George Orwell's *Down and Out in Paris,* James M. Cain's *The Postman Always Rings Twice,* *Forever Amber* by Kathleen Winsor, *God's Little Acre* by Erskine Caldwell, *The Colonel's Daughter* by Richard Aldington, *Alexanderplatz* by Alfred Doblin, *Appointment in Samarra* by John O'Hara, *Butterfield 8* by John O'Hara, and so on.

John Hetherington said: 'I take it that is the general idea isn't it? You exclude the crude, the impure, and the downright horrid, and Australia becomes a kind of Pacific Shangri-la where nobody hears evil, speaks evil, or thinks evil.' Then Hetherington noted with amazement that *Redheap* was still banned. This was a novel that 'could be read to a Gentlewomen's Sewing Circle without raising a blush'. The *Works of Rabelais* also were banned (cheap paper covered editions only).

'You get the idea?' said John Hetherington. 'If a man of means likes to pay three of four guineas for the *Works of Rabelais,* it's all right with the Customs Department. If a poor man wants a copy for the few shillings he can afford, he isn't allowed to have it. The theory seems to be that the poor man's morals are frailer than those of the man of means.'

Six years later the Customs Department relented and released its list of banned books, now pruned to 172. *Redheap* and *God's Little Acre* were no longer on it, but *Peyton Place, Lady Chatterley's Lover* and the *Decameron* were.<a href="#32"><sup>32</sup></a> Nor did it prevent books being added to the list. Before the end of the year *Borstal Boy* by Brendan Behan was banned. Behan, told about this in London, said: 'I do confess that I am at an unaccustomed loss for words over this ban. Where is Australia, anyway?'<a href="#33"><sup>33</sup></a>

As for *Lady Chatterley's Lover* by D. H. Lawrence, this was always thoroughly banned, and had been condemned for many years. Mr Piddington K.C. in the Banco Court, Sydney in 1935 said: 'Speaking of Lawrence's influence on the English language and character I say it is a good thing he is dead, and he should have died years ago.'<a href="#34"><sup>34</sup></a>

After an English jury had decided in 1960 that *Lady Chatterley's Lover* was not obscene, the Reverend Gordon Powell said on the 'Meet the Press' TV programme in Melbourne that it was being published in a Penguin paperback edition, and because of this he had decided never to buy a Penguin book again. He made the vow because he had felt 'such moral indignation about the book condoning the activities of an adulteress'. Furthermore, he had decided to write to the chairman of the Penguin company, Sir Allen Lane, to tell him of the vow.<a href="#35"><sup>35</sup></a>

Actually the Literature Board approved the release of *Lady Chatterley* but the Minister did not. Even the report of the London Court Case, a Penguin entitled, *The Trial of Lady Chatterley,* was banned. The Customs Department released it on 20 May 1965. Apart from two exceptions, none of the famous purple passages were in the book; yet Police raided two Melbourne bookshops.

The booksellers were waiting for the Court Case to come up on 1 June 1965, which destined to be a *cause célèbre,* when the Victorian Government called off the action.

The biggest un-banning came two months later. Cabinet suddenly reversed its policy and the Minister for Customs announced the release of *Lady Chatterley's Lover, Borstal Boy,* Vladimir Nabokov's *Lolita,* and *Confessions of a Spent Youth* by Vance Bourjaily.

The *Age* became quite nostalgic, the triumph was so unexpected. It pointed out that Lady Chatterley and her gamekeeper had been appealing for visas now since 1933, and it recalled some of the reviews that came out when the Lady first appeared in 1928. John Bull called it 'A landmark in evil'. But then *Field and Stream* said:

This pictorial account of the day to day life of an English gamekeeper is full of considerable interest to outdoor minded readers as it contains many passages on pheasant raising, the apprehending of poachers, ways to control vermin and other chores and duties of the professional gamekeeper. Unfortunately one is obliged to wade through many pages of extraneous material in order to discover and savour those sidelights on the management of a midlands shooting estate and in this reviewer's opinion the book cannot take the place of J. R. Miller's 'Practical Gamekeeping.'<a href="#36"><sup>36</sup></a>

There were many vigorous letters for and against but the most vigorous communications of all came from Alan Russell, a Brisbane dentist. Already he had fought to defend Brisbane's morals over an African Ballet group dancing in Brisbane bared to the waist. Brisbane was the only city in Australia where they were forced to cover up. Alan Russell sent this telegram to Canberra:

YOUR GOVERNMENT'S ASTOUNDING RELEASE OF FOUR SEX-SOAKED BOOKS CONSTITUTES MAJOR CONTRIBUTION TO POTENTIAL CAUSES OF DELINQUENCY BOTH JUVENILE AND ADULT STOP CUSTOMS MINISTER WAS APPARENTLY GUIDED BY FOUR UNIVERSITY INTELLECTUALS INCLUDING THREE PROFESSORS WHOSE CAPACITY TO MAKE RIGHT DECISIONS ON MORAL ISSUES HAS NEVER BEEN ESTABLISHED STOP NO PARENT CAREFULLY REARING CHILDREN WILL ENDORSE GOVERNMENT'S ACTION STOP IF AUSTRALIA'S MORAL DECLINE IS TO BE HALTED THE PRESENT MALFUNCTIONING CENSORSHIP MACHINERY OPERATED BY FOUR INTELLECTUALS MUST BE REPLACED BY A COMMONSENSE DOWN TO EARTH BODY DRAWN FROM AVERAGE HOMES NOT UNIVERSITY HOT HOUSES STOP SUCH A BROADBASED BODY COULD SPEAK FOR THE PEOPLE AS PARLIAMENTARIANS DO STOP I CONSIDER THE MORAL ATMOSPHERE OF THE AUSTRALIAN NATIONAL UNIVERSITY IN CANBERRA AS SHOWN BY THE CAMPUS ACTIVITIES NOT CONDUCIVE TO DECISIONS AND HIGH MORAL VALUES STOP SUGGEST GOVERNMENT SEEK ADVICE FROM SOURCES CLOSER TO AUSTRALIAN HOME LIFE AND FAMILY RAISING STOP I INTEND SUPPLYING THIS WEEK-END TO 349 AUSTRALIAN NEWSPAPERS ACTUAL EXCERPTS FROM THE GRACE METALLIOUS BOOK 'NO ADAM IN EDEN' PASSED BY CENSORSHIP BOARD WITHOUT BLUSHING STOP LET THESE EDITORS JUDGE YOUR GOVERNMENT'S FALL SHIP POLICY FROM FIRST HAND EVIDENCE STOP I REGRET YOUR GOVERNMENT'S FALL FROM GRACE MAKES PROTEST NECESSARY STOP

The books, as soon as they were available, were on sale in New South Wales, South Australia and Tasmania and, with some show of Government reluctance, they were on sale in Victoria a month later. *Lolita,* like *Lady Chatterley,* had been a famous banned book for years, almost the favourite thing to bring home for friends from overseas. Customs officers at airports put them on a priority level almost with transistor radios.

The release must have made interesting news for the Australian author, Dal Stivens. The Minister for Customs, Senator Henty, had said that certain books would be available to those people who needed them for serious study. So Dal Stivens decided to apply for *Lolita.* The book was described in the House of Representatives by Mr J. Killen, member for Moreton, as a morally corrupting, cheap, scrubby, little novel.<a href="#38"><sup>38</sup></a>

Stivens described his experience in the *Sydney Morning Herald.* He wrote to the Minister, pointing out that he was a writer and a critic, and the book was necessary for his work. He filled in forms in triplicate. The month was August. Most courteously a customs official called at his house.

'The Inspector', wrote Stivens, 'asked such questions, was I a writer, as I had claimed, and what had I written? He said he hadn't heard of me. But then he didn't know the name of any other Australian writer. He wanted to know where I would keep *Lolita.* I nearly said my bedroom, but instead showed him a cupboard with a key where I kept some of my rarer books.'<a href="#39"><sup>39</sup></a>

The Customs inspector was impressed with this, because he did not want the book to get into the hands of adolescents. Just before Christmas, Stivens received permission to buy *Lolita,* and he was able to hand a form to a bookseller to import it. The book reached his hands in June, so the whole process took ten months. Stivens commented: 'I have estimated that we probably spend £250,000 a year in trying to find smut in books.'

Yet what took place in Canberra did not necessarily work in the States. In Canberra there was a Board responsible to the Minister for Customs, and this dealt only with books imported into the country. At the same time each State had its own machinery for checking on obscene books. For example, Queensland had its own Literature Board of Review, and Victoria dealt with the matter under the Chief Secretary's Department, and action could be taken under the Police Offences Act. So the incredible situation arose where a book which was banned in one State was not banned in another. This happened with *God's Little Acre,* and with *Carlotta McBride,* a novel by Charles Orson Gorham. They were released by the Commonwealth Board, and all right for sale in New South Wales, but not in Victoria—a dream situation for any author. The Vice Squad seized copies of *Carlotta McBride* from six Melbourne booksellers in July 1960. The book was barely known at the time, and one seller reported that it was doing so badly they were about to put it on the remainder table. After the splendid Melbourne publicity, 500 copies sold in Sydney in three days.<a href="#40"><sup>40</sup></a>

*The Group* by Mary McCarthy was another example of this, and at one stage it was estimated that eight out of ten copies sold in Sydney were going to Victoria. *The Group* was not banned in Victoria, that is, not officially. On 12 March 1964, the Vice Squad advised Melbourne booksellers to withdraw copies from sale. Melbourne newspapers were outraged. They quoted overseas reviews. *Time* magazine had said the book was, 'First rate sociology about the depression and fascinating previously unrecorded female lore'.

And, in the now routine fashion, they contacted the author for comments. Mary McCarthy was in Paris and she said she was pained but not surprised that Melbourne police had seized *The Group.* 'My publisher said I might have trouble in Australia about which I know little, except the kangaroos.'<a href="#41"><sup>41</sup></a>

The Chief Secretary, Mr Rylah, had bought a copy and he made the comment; 'If I had a teenage son or daughter, I would not like to see them reading it.'<a href="#42"><sup>42</sup></a>

On 18 March there was a debate on obscene literature in the Victorian Legislative Assembly. Mr Rylah said: 'Over the past few days I have been through a rather frightening experience, and an experience which I am afraid, in these days of sensational television and newspaper presentation of news, will be shared from time to time by any man in public life, who is prepared to carry out his duty without fear or favor.'

Some teenage girls were ushered from the Gallery and he quoted the seduction scene from *The Group.*

Mr Rylah explained that obscenity under section 164 of the Police Offences Act meant (a) tending to deprave and corrupt persons whose minds are open to immoral influences, and (b) unduly emphasising matters of sex. He told how the real problem was keeping out the mass of sexy comics, magazines, weekly newspapers and such.

But the debate had one interesting result. His speech was now on record in *Hansard.* Anyone pornographically minded, if unable to buy a copy of *The Group,* could at least buy a copy of *Hansard,* circulation 2,000, price fourpence a copy. The *Bulletin* even suggested that bookshops should now sell expurgated copies of *The Group* and toss in a free copy of *Hansard* with each one. Indeed perhaps some silly fellow might even complain to a magistrate or JPs that *Hansard* had corrupted his child.<a href="#43"><sup>43</sup></a>

Yet once the political anger had subsided, *The Group* did go on sale in Victoria, and many enthusiasts, intrigued by the publicity, were a little surprised to discover in reading that it was a solemn, even earnest book.

In November 1967, after years of discussion, the States and the Commonwealth finally signed an agreement for a uniform censorship system, with a National Literature Board of Review. This was to have nine members, including the chairman and two deputy chairmen. Six, including the chairman and his deputies, were to be nominated by the Commonwealth; the rest to be chosen by the Federal Government from a panel of nominees submitted by the States. The States agreed that they would not prosecute books cleared by the Board, although they had power to do so. The new arrangement did not receive hearty enthusiasm. For example the *Age* gave it 'three subdued cheers'. There were still the same old problems. The Minister for Customs was still all-powerful, the States if they wished, could still go their own way, and there was still much secrecy about book censorship.<a href="#44"><sup>44</sup></a>

The Board went into action in January 1968, and it certainly proved to be a much quieter year for censorship. The classic Indian book of sex, *The Kama Sutra of Vatsayana* went on sale in March all around Australia, even in Victoria. There was no fuss, no clamour for copies, and little publicity.

Furthermore that same month in Melbourne there was a book sale in Bourke Street, Melbourne. It was called 'The Book Sale of the Century'. Among the books for sale were *Lolita,* Vladimir Nabokov, 50 cents; *The American Dream,* Norman Mailer, 95 cents; *Lady Chatterley's Lover,* D. H. Lawrence, 40 cents; *Love Me Sailor,* Robert S. Close, 70 cents; *Another Country,* James Baldwin, 70 cents.

They all had this in common, all books were handsomely bound in hard covers. All had been banned within the past five years, and all had been subject of much controversy in Victoria. Now they were on the remainder table. Nobody cared, and there was not a wowser in sight.<a href="#45"><sup>45</sup></a>
