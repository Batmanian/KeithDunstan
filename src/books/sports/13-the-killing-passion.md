The early settlers found Australia a strange harsh, ugly country with a demonic climate. The trees were ugly to look upon; they shed bark instead of leaves, that's if they could be called leaves. They threw no shade and their green was a tired, dank green, dreary through lack of water.

The birds made strange uncanny wailings, and the animals were as peculiar and unnatural as the country itself, most of them unsatisfactory for sport and unsatisfactory for eating. Yet the old English passion for shooting transported very well to Australia, and the early destruction of all forms of wild life, kangaroos, koala bears, duck, wild geese, even lyre-birds, was enormous.

Aborigines also came under this heading. They were as ugly and as strange as the country, how could they be human? Shooting Aborigines was also good sport. Dame Mary Gilmore had a childhood memory of this. She was on her grandfather's property when she saw an Aboriginal man and his wife bathing in a river. A white settler was on his way home. He had been out shooting with no success, and seeing the black woman swimming in the water, he shot her. The husband let out a cry of anguish, went to her help, so the white man shot him also. It was just good sport. Even in Queensland as late as 1882 the situation had not changed. George Ernest Morrison recorded this in his diary on the way to Cloncurry:

> The wretched blacks are shot without mercy. One night I was at a station, whose owner is said to have shot more blacks than any two men in Queensland, when the mailman came in and reported he had seen a black prowling about the stockyard.

The station owner loaded his rifle, went off and returned an hour later 'quite disappointed' that it was too dark to follow the trail of the black.

The non-shooter does not always understand the joy to be had in killing a beautiful bird. Lieutenant-Colonel Godfrey Charles Mundy gives an extraordinary account of how he killed a black swan north of Wollongong, in 1852. He told how he admired the wildness and seclusion of the area. Suddenly, way out on the face of the water, he saw this splendid black swan. He was 'electrified by the sight.'

There was still plenty of light, he had a fast Arab horse and there was time to dash back two miles for his gun. But even though 'I had already committed cygnicide in my heart,' said he, he couldn't do this thing on the Sabbath.

So he broke off twigs, made a careful trail to help find his way back and all night he dreamt of swans. Next morning at dawn he dressed drably from head to foot as camouflage and returned with his telescope and a double-barrelled gun. There, right across the other side of the lake, he saw 'the long sable neck and bright crimson bill' he was so anxiously waiting for.

He fired the first barrel as the bird rose and the second when it was in full flight. He heard the shot strike and saw a broken feather whirling towards the water. 'But it never pays to take your eye off a bird; every old shooter knows that,' said the colonel. Sure enough, the swan made a sudden turn in the air, then tumbled with a heavy thud into mid lagoon.

But the swan was still alive and it paddled its way across to the further reaches. The colonel was crafty, he rushed as fast as he could to meet the swan as it paddled towards the shore, and he made it just in time. He said to himself: 'Sing your death song, beautiful bird! Ebon, lord of the flood, sing your dirge—for your hour is come.' The bird tried desperately to rise, but it could not because one wing was useless. The first barrel rang through the bush, and as he put it, the long graceful neck and neat small head dropped on the water.

He wrote:

> It was something, I thought, to have shot and retrieved my own rara avis. Moreover it enabled me to make a sporting boast involving no contracted sphere of travel—namely that I have shot in their naturally wild state, in their own homes, specimens of all the domestic birds now found in the English farmyard.

One comes across other examples like that in early reminiscences. One colonist reported that lyrebirds sadly were becoming rare in Gippsland, one had to go right into the forests to find them. He went into raptures about their beauty, about their extraordinary qualities, how they made their own dancing grounds.

> The ground is worn completely bare by the bird's feet, and has all the appearance of a well-swept earthen floor. It is almost absurd to watch the extraordinary antics of the bird when capering about on these circular spaces. They dance, and jump and strut round and round, they twist their heads in all sorts of attitudes, all the while keeping up singularly correct imitation of sounds of every other bird in the bush. This is the time to shoot them if anyone has the heart to do so, for they are the most comical and most enchanting of Australian birds. Nothing is more charming when camped out in the ranges than to hear the stillness of the early morning broken by their one solitary, but most melodious note.

Having said that he gave precise instructions how to kill them and eat them. It was important to acquire a dog, a small terrier that had been used to hunting opossums.

> Send the dog into the scrub where a bird is heard whistling and let him hunt about until he gets on to the scent, which is from this bird very strong. The bird will not move until the dog gets close in when it usually runs for a short distance, and then flies up into a tree, the position of which is pointed out by the yapping of the dog, and a peculiar harsh noise made by the lyre-bird when alarmed. The next thing is to get to the tree as quickly as possible, not an easy thing to do in the tangled scrubs of Gippsland, but once within range a charge of number three usually settled the business. The lyre-bird if tolerably young and hung for some time is by no means bad eating, the flesh being dark in colour, and has a very perceptible gamey flavour.

Indeed the Englishman would try to eat everything. P. R. Cunningham, R.N., surgeon, gave a good run down on colonial eating in his book *Two Years in New South Wales*:

| Kangaroo: | Fine eating, being clear of fat, except about the tail, tasting much like venison. The favourite dish being what is called 'a steamer', composed of steaks and chopped tail. |
|---|---|
| Possums and Flying Squirrel: | Good eating, much of the taste and flavour of rabbit, but requires to be soaked in water some time previously. |
| Bandicoot: | Tastes somewhat like a suckling pig and makes a delicious dish with a well-prepared pudding in its belly. |
| Koala bear: | Fat and unwieldy. Flesh much esteemed by natives. |
| Emu: | Little fit for culinary use, except the hindquarters which are of such dimensions that a mile distance, once, proved to me such a tiresome task as I ever recollect to have encountered in the colony. The flesh is like beef, both in appearance and taste, nothing can be more delicate than the flesh of the young ones. The yolk and white of the eggs can be mixed into excellent pancakes. |
| Wild turkeys: | Lay one egg at a time and are excellent eating. |

Yet even Cunningham did not eat lyrebirds. A. H. Chisholm has written that when the white man came in 1788 lyrebirds were found all along the Great Dividing Range from Port Phillip to Queensland. It was like a huge natural concert hall and the top members in the choir were the lyrebirds, the most powerful and versatile of nature's singers anywhere in the world. Aborigines had depleted them very little. They did not use their plumes for decoration, nor did they find them very good eating.

Yet by the 1830s they were becoming rare in the Blue Mountains and the price for the tails rose to twenty shillings and thirty shillings a pair. Victoria gave the lyrebird legal protection in 1888, and in New South Wales they were sacrosanct under the Bird Protection Act of 1893.

But a mere act of Parliament was not enough to stop the shooters. Before the First World War it was common to see Sydney hawkers with baskets full of lyrebird tails. A. H. Chisholm reported that at a Sydney conference of the Ornithologists Union of 1911, one member complained that in 1910 two Sydney dealers had sold 1,298 lyrebird tails and 2,000 had been exported in three years. One could buy a lyrebird tail in London for half a crown.

There was a clear advantage over the old country; here shooting was for all classes. There were no problems about poaching, anyone with a gun could get out and start slaughtering. However it was also a sport for the rich, and when visiting royalty arrived, a gun was pushed into the royal hand at once.

In October 1867, H.R.H. Prince Alfred, Duke of Edinburgh, the 23-year-old second son of Queen Victoria, arrived aboard H.M.S. *Galatea* and he spread his carnage across five colonies. In South Australia the Governor took him to the Government Farm in the Adelaide Hills. The ideal situation for possum shooting was a moonlight night. One looked up through the trees, silhouetted the possum against the moon and fired. It was difficult to miss.

And yet, as explained earlier, Australian wild life was thought too dull for really good shooting. Eric Rolls in his book *They All Ran Wild* quotes the Hon. G. H. Cox speaking in the New South Wales Legislative Council on the Animals Protection Bill of 1881:

> We are so destitute of the means of enjoying country life as to have no deer, pheasants, quail, partridges and other creatures which afford so much enjoyment to gentlemen elsewhere. There is nothing here in the way of game for people of sporting proclivities and I have always found these gentlemen to be a most desirable element in a community.

One man who worked hard for this desirable element was Thomas Austin of Barwon Park, the gentleman who provided the shoot for Prince Alfred.

He was so rich that in 1869 he built a vast bluestone mansion near Winchelsea. It was panelled in solid cedar. It had a huge classic staircase surmounted by Corinthian columns. It had stained glass windows, lofty cedar doors two inches thick and a living room said to be a replica of a living room in Buckingham Palace. There were quantities of beautiful cast-iron, and in one piece was woven the family crest and motto and the initials of Thomas and Elizabeth Austin. The cedar came out from England in a specially chartered ship. The mansion still exists and has been bequeathed to the National Trust.

Austin believed that one of the sad features of colonial life was the paucity of decent creatures to shoot at. In 1859, he imported aboard the fast clipper *Lightning* seventy-two partridges, five hares and twenty-four wild rabbits. He did not have great success early on, but he persevered, importing partridges, hares, rabbits, deer, pheasants, blackbirds, foxes, thrushes, and in 1865 he killed twenty thousand rabbits on his property.

*Bell's Life* was delighted, and on 3 June 1865 it produced a very interesting leading article; interesting for two reasons — it explained the need for gentlemen to have something to shoot at, and it explained the contempt for Australian wild life.

> Mr Austin and those like him who have endeavoured to stock our barren bush with animals that shall administer to our pleasure and serve for food, deserve the gratitude of all classes. Probably there is no country on the face of the globe so sparsely supplied with animals that come under the definition of game as Australia. The kangaroo is almost worthless, except as regards its tail for soup, and its skin for leather, and when we enumerate the duck tribe, quail and snipe, we have exhausted the catalogue of birds which the sportsmen will care to fire at. The magnificent game which abounds in the forests of Asia is unknown to us.... We need to create in the southern hemisphere an exact counterpart of that country which is so dear to most of us.

Bell called for the propagation of the fox and added: 'We welcome the day when that sly animal will usurp the place the kangaroo now holds, and become the legitimate object of the chase.'

Love of field sports, a country well-stocked with English game, was an absolute necessity said Bell. Otherwise what inducement would there be for wealthy men to stay in this country?

Despite Bell's contempt, the slaughter of the kangaroo around Melbourne was a particularly popular pastime in the 1860s and 1870s, though not so much later on because there weren't any left. Hunt clubs, beautifully dressed in the proper English regalia, would run them down with dogs. But the sport written about in the most enthusiastic terms was the kangaroo battue.

The best place for doing this was around Dromana and Arthur's Seat and in the wild country through to Cape Schanck. The well-to-do people would set out from Melbourne by steamer at night, have themselves landed at Dromana just before dawn, and there would be met by stockmen and horses.

An *Illustrated Australian News* of 1865 printed 'a letter from a shooter back home' which told how they had seventeen guns, arranged in a semi-circle, each man a hundred and fifty yards apart. Then they had two stockmen, Billy and George, both six foot four inches in their socks. They carried immense fifteen foot stockwhips which they cracked over their heads, with reports louder than gun shots. Cracking their whips, shouting and yelling 'like Maoris' they drove hundreds of kangaroos towards the guns. The shooter proudly tells in his letter how they bagged two hundred kangaroos in a day, and when this was done they went off shooting possums.

Then there's an 1860 account by a naturalist, Horace Wheelwright, who signed himself simply as 'Old Bushman'. He wrote that in two seasons on the Mornington Peninsula his party shot considerably more than two thousand kangaroos. For his battues he always used a beater and a brace of dogs.

[illustration: S. T. Gill even painted it with extraordinary charm]

> I always had two guns ready, and have sometimes brought down four at a drive. The best 'family shot' I ever saw was made by old Pete. He shot right and left into a mob coming up to him, and got four old does, three of them with heavy Joeys in the pouch, so that he bagged seven kangaroos at the two shots. It is a good plan if the kangaroos are coming up gently to whistle, and then they will often stop in a line, and hold up their heads like seals.
>
> The great secret of kangaroo shooting is never to be in a hurry; load with as much powder as your gun will stand, and never fire until the kangaroo is well within distance. (I used to kill more within 20 yards than over it) and aim well at the neck. But I cannot say that I ever really fancied kangaroo shooting much as a sport. There is a sameness in it which when carried on month after month is very wearying, even if followed as an amusement.

There were many ways of killing wild life, one was open coursing. During the 1870s and 1880s this became a winter passion, a craze, something one had to do to remain among the socially elite. The passion reached its most passionate state in Victoria and South Australia.

The first coursing meeting took place in South Australia at Mr Harry Jones' property at Binnum. Legend has it that they were staging a kangaroo hunt with eleven greyhounds. The kangaroo had so much pace, and eluded the hounds with such skill, a member of the party said, 'if these things can run like this, I see nothing to hinder the formation of a coursing club.'

So they formed Australia's first coursing club at Naracoorte and they were not at all shy. They called it the Waterloo, after Britain's greatest coursing event. It was like honouring the Gunn's Gully bush pony race with the title 'Melbourne Cup'. They coursed a beautiful specimen of scrub wallaby, named 'Toolaches', and it had a pretty shaped V on its face. For a time it was very clever in avoiding the greyhounds, it would even jump right back over its pursuers, and undoubtedly gave its money's worth before it died.

Early on the problem was to get suitable hares. They did not care for ship life and most of them died before they reached Australia. But more and more came, so by 1873 when coursing became legal in Victoria, they were around in large numbers. But the *Australasian* was still fearful:

> Hares are being destroyed in all directions—young and old. They are coursed with mobs of curs by Cockney sportsmen and shot down also. Any person who prefers hare with currant jelly to seeing the animals run wild, can gratify his taste that way also. Fortunately there are estates such as those of Hon. W. J. Clarke, where they can find shelter.

The Hon. W. J. Clarke, later Sir William, was the great man behind coursing in Australia. His father, the Hon. William Turner Clarke, was one of Australia's pioneers. He had properties in New South Wales, South Australia, Tasmania, Queensland, and his 31,000 acres at Sunbury ran from Williamstown to the Macedon Ranges. He was the wool king of the world, but he had a terror of spending money. When he died in 1874 he left young W. J. one and a half million pounds.

William Junior immediately set about getting rid of the cobwebs on all that money. He gave thousands to charity in cheques up to £10,000 at a time. The *Bulletin* in 1883 described him as not as clever as his father but more liberal. 'He bought a baronetcy by sundry timely and public liberalities. He is a good fellow enough and perfectly harmless.' He bought a string of racehorses. He bought a great yacht. He built a mansion in the city, called Cliveden, designed to be on a par with Cliveden of Buckinghamshire. Its ballroom could be used to house a thousand people. Most charming of all he set up his own regiment, the Rupertswood Battery. He bought three Nordenfeldt field pieces and imported a famous British soldier, Sergeant-Major Daley, as drill instructor. His son Rupert became commanding officer.

He went about coursing with the same vigour. He appointed Captain Dick Banner, skipper of his yacht *Janet*, to handle his hounds and take over his coursing activities. He brought out George Warwick, an English judge, at a cost of £300, plus first-class passage, for the season of 1873. Mr Warwick returned home two years later with remarkable stories about the fecundity of Australian hares, how they bred two and three times a year and were four pounds heavier than English hares. Ah, but they were not so good for eating, a dry insipid stick compared with the English leveret, nor would the most cunning chef make them more edible even by juggling.

The first coursing meeting was at the Clarke property, Rupertswood, on 29 May 1873. There was a special train to Sunbury. Excursion rates were seven shillings and sixpence, second class five shillings and sixpence, dogs one shilling each way. According to one report the scene at Spencer Street Station was very curious. There were all the dog owners, plus the finely bred hounds 'clad in all the coxcombry of dog clothing'. The essential thing to do was to run one's hand along the dogs, and remark in wonder 'my that dog is very fit.'

It wasn't easy. The coursing range was covered with rocks and tussocks. There were holes full of water and the hares very indecently made use of these obstacles, not eagerly offering themselves up for death by greyhound. But Sir George and Lady Bowen followed the judge around 'fearlessly' on horseback and were covered in mud.

Towards the end of 1876 there was trouble in the coursing set and some of the more influential members broke away and formed the Australian Coursing Club. According to the English Greyhound Stud Book there was so much bitter feeling it was like the Capulets and the Montagues.

The powers behind the new club were Thomas and Andrew Chirnside of Werribee Park. The Chirnsides were not as rich as the Clarkes, but very nearly. Thomas Chirnside built his hundred-room mansion near Werribee in 1873. It too had a drawing room like Buckingham Palace, a tower like Melbourne's Government House, and even more accommodation. His specialty was importing fox and deer. The deer bred with such speed that in 1877 there were seven hundred of them in the vicinity of Point Cook. So he organised a battue, done in the classic kangaroo style. They had beaters, they had dogs to urge the animals up to the guns. In one afternoon they shot over two hundred.

In 1887 Thomas Chirnside suffered a bout of depression and shot himself in the old bluestone laundry at the back of the mansion. The family motto, with eagle ready to pounce, was 'Do or Die.' But in his coursing days there was no depression. The Australian Club set up its own big event in opposition to Victoria's Waterloo Cup. The Waterloo was a sixty-four dog stage, entry twenty-five sovereigns, first prize £400 and a piece of plate valued at £100. The Australian restricted entry to thirty-two dogs, first prize £300 and a piece of plate worth fifty guineas donated by Mr Chirnside.

A day at either place would be a very grand affair. There would be marquees, there would be elevated covered boxes for the press, and a long refreshment bar, and Mr Chirnside would entertain four hundred to lunch.

Then as time went on there would be two special trains to Sunbury instead of one, with a crowd of over a thousand for a meeting. You could get lunch in the marquee at Sunbury for three shillings and sixpence or there was a sumptuous invitation lunch for fifteen shillings. According to one commentator only about a tenth of the people were really interested in using their legs and following the coursing.

Almost every ship that came to Australia now brought greyhounds. There were country meetings all around Victoria, New South Wales and South Australia. The original headquarters of coursing in New South Wales was Bathurst and a meeting took place there with hares imported from Victoria. The New South Wales Coursing Club established itself at Woodstock Estate, Rooty Hill, and the most successful dog owner was Mr Sam Bladon, a taciturn gentleman, who won a record eleven Waterloo Cups. He had an abhorrence of tobacco. When travelling to Melbourne he used to reserve a special carriage for himself and his dogs, so that they would get no taint of tobacco fumes during the whole journey.

At some meetings there would be up to fifty courses in a day and a big affair could be spread over three days, which meant a large number of hares would meet sudden death. Under the ancient rules a hare was to have 'twelve score yards' before the dogs were loosed, and a hare was never to be coursed by more than a brace of greyhounds. However there was a complaint to the *Australasian* about coursers who let five and six dogs after the single hare. 'A man who would slip more than a brace of dogs to a hare deserves to be blackballed at every coursing club in the country. It is barbarism that scarcely a black fellow would be guilty of.'

Coursing was very much under vice-regal patronage, but just occasionally someone would complain. This story titled 'Coursing by a Lady' appeared in one of the weeklies:

> A certain start is allowed to the terrified animal, and then the dogs are let loose. This is when the fun begins, my dear. Imagine the swift greyhounds, each about four times as big as the hare, pursuing it with fierce eagerness and ravenous haste, while the smaller animal—which has such beautiful brown eyes—strains every nerve to avoid being torn to pieces by the dogs. When they have gained upon it so that its death appears to be inevitable, it makes a double, that is to say it turns back, and the dogs, who have overshot the mark, because they have so much 'way' on, cannot retrace their steps so readily. But their superior speed soon atones for this disadvantage, and they once more gain upon their victim. She is quivering with terror in every limb. Her heart is a prey to horror. The anguish that looks from her eyes is quite pathetic. Probably she has a family of young leverets whose death will be involved in her destruction, and she thinks of these with a yearning tenderness and passionate grief as she feels her strength failing her, sees that there is no escape for her, and is at length seized by the faster of the dogs, flung into the air, and falls to the ground a maimed and mangled wreck.
>
> It is such a delicious sport, my dear, so exquisitely feminine, so becoming to our sex, and such a fine education for the feelings. I am in hope that in a few years time we will be able to establish bull fights in Victoria.

One man who campaigned long and hard against coursing was Paris Nesbit, Q.C., the man who defended 'Plugger' Bill Martin after he thrashed Frank Ridley. When the South Australian Government made legal closed coursing with live hares, he produced a furious pamphlet, which he dedicated to the Governor of South Australia, Sir Archibald Weigall, 'the man who was not man enough to promise not to attend any more coursing matches.'

He referred to the Governor as a man of little intrinsic interest or importance, to the Premier, Mr Barwell, as a man of pitiable insignificance, and this was one of his better lines: 'I am told by one who has been to a coursing match that the attendance comprises the very dregs and scum of the community; bookmakers, Governors, members of Parliament, and the like.' And another good line:

> The Police, with the Chief Secretary and Commissioner of Police at their head, spare no time or trouble or expense in hunting down little boys guilty of the appalling crime of playing two-up; but turn a deaf ear to the shrieks of the criminally tortured hare.

The peak year for field coursing was 1882, when money was no object and £10,000 was distributed in stakes during the season. Although coursing never quite disappeared it never had the same popularity again. Plumpton, closed coursing, became the thing and open coursing by 1898 was almost dead.

Apart from hares, there was always something to kill. At one time the koala bear population in Australia ran to many millions. They dwelt happily in South Australia, New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland. By 1920 they had been completely exterminated in South Australia, so a belated attempt was made to re-establish them on Kangaroo Island. They were very scarce in Victoria, almost extinct in New South Wales and plentiful only in Queensland, but resourceful Queenslanders were quick to solve that problem.

The big trouble was that the furriers of the world liked koala bear as the fur was dense and warm. Horace Wheelwright, 'the old bushman', wrote: 'The skin is very thick and tans to an excellent leather, the fur short and close.... They are extremely difficult to shoot on account of the thick hide; and it's cruelty to shoot at them with shot.' But he assured his readers that a bullet would bring them down very quickly.

In 1908 sixty thousand koala bear pelts went through the Sydney market, but this was a comparatively small number, no doubt the last of the local New South Wales koalas. In 1924 altogether two million skins were exported from the Eastern States. In one year three million were listed for sale in America.

The enormous crime came in 1927 when the Queensland Government declared the month of August an open season on koalas. Professor Jock Marshall, Professor of Zoology at Monash University, described this as perhaps the most sordid episode in the history of Queensland. He swore that he would find out the names of the politicians who were responsible for this thing and make sure their names were preserved for ever for posterity. The Queensland Premier was Mr W. McCormack, but he was away at the time, so Professor Marshall named Forgan Smith and A. J. Jones.

When attacked through the newspapers Mr Forgan Smith gave a classic politician's reply. He said the Animal and Birds Act of Queensland provided a degree of protection for native fauna equal to that accorded to the fauna of any other country in the world. The fur industry was a valuable one in Queensland, and it was worthy of note that the royalty collected did not go into general revenue but was earmarked for providing funds for the future protection of fauna and sanctuaries.

Naturally trappers prepared for months for this and had their pelts ready long before 1 August. The final Government figures for the big shoot were these — Opossums: 1,014,632. Koalas: 584,738.

The Government royalties 'for the protection of wild life' were £18,897 12s. 9d. The koala population of Queensland never again recovered from the slaughter.

Another slaughter was the emu war of Western Australia, one of the lesser known Australian military campaigns. In the early days emus were found right across Australia, and were quite plentiful near Sydney, but even in the days of John Gould, around 1860, they were becoming extremely scarce.

The story was different in Campion, Western Australia, 180 miles east-north-east of Perth. In 1932 the wheat farmers claimed that they were being invaded by an army of twenty thousand emus. Rifles, guns, nothing could stop them and they appealed to the Government for help.

The Federal Minister for Defence, Sir George Pearce, despatched an offensive unit, under the Commanding Officer of the 7th Heavy Battery, R.A.A., Major G. W. P. Meredith. His second in command was Sergeant S. M. McMurray, plus Gunner O'Halloran. They were equipped with two Lewis guns and ten thousand rounds of ammunition.

Major Meredith told 'war correspondents' in Perth that he was keen to see an emu. Years ago he saw a notice in Taronga Park Zoo, Sydney, which pointed out that emus rapidly were becoming extinct in Australia. Emu feathers were so hard to get that Colonel Hoad of the First Cavalry Division had asked him to send a hundred sets for Light Horsemen's hats.

The campaign opened on 2 November and the Army scored twelve kills on the first day. But by the second day war correspondents were beginning to sound a note of disillusionment. Forty years later, perhaps, similar things could have been said about the Viet Cong. The *West Australian* correspondent wired:

> It would be perfect shooting country if only the quarry were content to be passive victims. Each mob has its leader, always an enormous black-plumed bird standing fully six feet high, who keeps watch while his fellows busy themselves with the wheat. The way the birds can carry the wound caused by a nickel encased .303 bullet is astounding. Frequently a burst of machine gun fire will hit the brown mass of hurrying birds, causing a few to go head over heels, and others will falter but keep going. One small mob fired at today appeared to go unscathed but half a mile down the track, two were found in a dying condition. One had been shot clean through the neck and another through the body. Only a hit in a vital spot will stop the birds.

On the third day Major Meredith's patrol at last encountered the enemy, a huge force of a thousand emus, all bunched together with no hope of escape. They started to open fire, a dozen birds fell and the Lewis gun jammed. Major Meredith now sounded even more despairing; he had expended two thousand rounds with no great result. He told correspondents: 'If we had a military division with the bullet carrying capacity of these birds, it would face any army in the world. They are like Zulus, whom even dum dum bullets would not stop.'

Fourth day and fifth day, nothing achieved, blazing sun with temperatures over a hundred degrees. Sixth day, thirsty emus were coming closer and the Army cornered fifty at a waterhole and opened fire. There was a terrible confusion of wounded flapping birds, struggling to escape. The Army men went in and clubbed to death with waddies those that were not killed outright. Score of kills: fifty.

But there was alarming news; they did not appear to be getting anywhere. A boundary rider spotted big flocks of emu reinforcements coming along the rabbit-proof fence.

Eighth day, there was a call for a ceasefire. Emu G.H.Q. made no statements but Campion farmers and Western Australian parliamentarians were furious. 'City pussyfoots,' growled Mr G. J. Lambert, M.L.A.

Canberra picnickers. Oh I can appreciate the attitude of the brass hats in Melbourne in killing any attempt to do anything useful with military equipment which they consider should be held sacred to less worthy purposes.

But Senator Sir George Pearce had to endure a difficult time in the house. One member shouted: 'The emus have won every round the shooting party has gone so far.' Another asked who was responsible for the birds with machine guns? And Mr Jones Lang (Labor, N.S.W.) was curious to know whether a campaign medal would be struck for all who took part in the campaign. Sir George reported that three hundred emus had been killed.

The Premier of Western Australia asked the Army to return, which they did. This time their tactics were better. They waited in ambush for the emus and their kill rate was higher. Charles Barret, a naturalist, commented how sad, what a waste it all was. The birds in London would sell for £85 a pair.

One could devote much more space to the Australian sport of killing. I have never seen a platypus in its native habitat; few Australians have. In the old days it used to take maybe sixty platypus to make a good, glossy platypus rug, and you could still buy platypus skins in Sydney as recently as 1923.

Quail used to be in vast abundance all over Victoria and New South Wales. The shooting seasons were long and regular and I can remember my mother's sighs of agony when quail were left in her kitchen by shooting friends. She thought quail something that needed to be eaten under a magnifying glass and she preferred them on the wing.

The *Australasian* referred often to the enormous bags that used to be shot close to Melbourne in the 1870s. The issue of 20 May 1871 reported that Mr Thomas Grimwood shot two hundred brace of quail on the Tuesday and Wednesday, then followed it up with two hundred and thirty brace at the weekend.

The sporting editor made the wry comment: 'This is downright slaughter, however we are exceedingly grateful for the two brace kindly left at our office.'

Even those figures could not have been impressive, for the following week, a gentleman who signed himself 'One fond of good day's sport,' wrote that back in 1854 he had shot five hundred and sixty brace in three days, using muzzle loaders.

There was the destruction too of the straw-necked ibis, the swamp parrot, bustards (plains turkey) and the Cape Barren geese. The bustards and the geese are now almost extinct in Victoria; they had this tremendous personal disadvantage — they were tasty to eat. Dr A. Norman McArthur, who was a keen shooter in the Western district from the 1890s to the 1930s, has recalled how he has seen over a hundred wild turkey on the Wiridgil Flats between Leura and Wiridgil. Tom and Willie Manifold used to shoot them with rifles. They really killed more than they were aware of, because afterwards the thistle cutters would come across several that had not been collected. If a wild turkey ever arrived on the tussocky flats of Meningoort there was general excitement. The hooded buggy was commandeered and driven with great danger to its springs and frightful discomfort to the shooters over the dreadfully rough country while the bustard would be stalked round and round, closer and closer.

The doctor said they were hard to get without a good rifle and he had only a gun. He remembered also organised shoots after Cape Barren geese at Nerrin Nerrin. The guns were posted behind stone walls — the geese were sent along from the Lake by stockriders, but more often the apparently slow flight of the geese could beat the horsemen.

In February 1969 one shooting journal even gave instructions on how to get your bag of Cape Barren geese. It advised taking a trip to the Furneaux Group of Islands in Bass Strait. There the clever thing was to station shooters on each island. Being domestic birds the geese would only fly off to another island; so the shooters could send them back and forth all day with their shots. Furthermore, these birds were very faithful. If one was hit it would always come back for its dead mate. Hence another easy victim.

Professor Jock Marshall described this beautiful grey bird as uniquely Australian as the platypus or koala, and in a great deal more danger than either. It breeds nowhere but on the lonely islands in Bass Strait and off the southern coast of Australia.

In 1965 the Tasmanian Animals and Birds Protection Board authorised a two-day season for shooting Cape Barren geese. It took place on 24 and 25 April and a hundred and forty birds were shot. The official reason was this. The farmers had been complaining. There were too many birds on the islands; they had been fouling pastures and eating the crops. As soon as he heard about it Professor Marshall mounted a splendid attack. He said:

> The Tasmanians have a black record for this sort of thing. They exterminated their aboriginals, they exterminated their emus. More emus were brought from the Australian mainland and they exterminated them too.... This last outbreak of barbarity was not due to geese eating crops. It was because of the pressure by Tasmanian shooters.

The Board replied that no damage was done, that the shoot was carefully controlled. Each shooter was only allowed three birds and the area was restricted to three islands in the Furneaux Group — Woody, Badger and Kangaroo.

Professor Marshall retorted:

> 'Control under supervision', according to the protection authority, involved two officers on each island to count the slain, and a light aeroplane overhead for reasons that are hard to guess. All in all, it was a jolly three ring circus with a happy time to be had by all except, of course, a native bird that is approaching extinction.

In 1969 Tasmania discontinued grazing licences on Chappell Island, near Flinders Island in Bass Strait, so it could become a sanctuary for the geese. Naturalists estimate that now there are perhaps five to six thousand Cape Barren geese left in the whole world.

Another bird that has suffered has been the wedge-tailed eagle, the master of the skies. Unquestionably the most exciting of all outback scenes is to see the lord himself, five hundred feet, a thousand feet up, great wings outspread, sensitively gathering every updraft, gliding, swooping, rising easily into stall turns, almost stopping in mid air, then down again in a graceful limpid display of aerobatics. Yet here is a bird that has been loathed, with the sort of loathing given to the shark or the trapdoor spider. Robert Goleman has written that there is an instinctive distrust of eagles. Some psychologists have seen deep-lying symbolic motives, that through history the eagle has been the power emblem of tyrannical leaders much hated by those whom they oppressed.

Whatever the motive there has been an Australian custom, even a tradition with eagles, similar to the displaying of the heads of traitors and enemies on pikes on London Bridge or the Tower of London. Australians display the cadavers of eagles shot upon their wire fences or pin them out on the sides of woolsheds.

In 1971 the CSIRO reported that as rabbits were the main diet of wedge-tailed eagles they had 'scarcely any adverse impact on sheep'. Most if not all the lambs taken by eagles for food were already dead.

All these years farmers had been shooting one of their greatest friends. A pair of eagles could have killed anything up to two thousand young rabbits a year. So the eagle is now protected in every State except Queensland. Graham Pizzey has written that wedge-tails were declared vermin in Western Australia in 1919. Between 1928 and 1960 bonuses of five shillings were paid for the deaths of a hundred and thirty thousand of them. Throughout Australia we killed them at the rate of thirty thousand a year.

In Queensland in recent times they have paid ten thousand ten-shilling bounties in a single year and a modern sport has been to shoot them from light aircraft. Two graziers near Longreach scored 133 kills in three weeks in 1953. Lord Casey also caused a stir in 1955 by shooting an eagle with a shotgun from the open door of an Auster aircraft. 'A wonderful thrill,' he said.

Another creature that has suffered from bad public relations is the crocodile. The slaughter of the saltwater and the smaller freshwater crocodile has been so complete that now they are close to extinction in the Northern Territory. Graham Pizzey reported that in 1965 in a five-month search of Northern Australia he was unable to find a single saltwater crocodile. He also reported that in 1966 thousands of young freshwater crocodiles were slaughtered for souvenirs. They were stuffed, lacquered and fitted with glaring glass eyes and snarling scarlet jaws.

One of the few places still remaining where one can see crocodiles is at a crocodile farm on the west coast of Cape York Peninsula. The farm, with Federal Government backing, is operated by a Hungarian, Joe Bredl.

He is hatching baby crocodiles from the eggs, and has a 'maternity ward' of hundreds of baby crocodiles. Joe Bredl has no time for the professional shooters; he believes they have destroyed one of the north's greatest tourist attractions.

To the uninitiated the crocodile hardly seems one of the most lovable creatures and when one suggests to Mr Bredl that they could be highly dangerous he says: 'It's nonsense. You could go back to the turn of the century and I bet you could count on the fingers of two hands the number of people who had been killed by crocodiles.'

He asked one to look at the score. For every million crocodiles killed and skinned by humans, there would be less than one human killed and eaten by the crocodile. Yet the alleged menace of the crocodile remains; those red eyes staring from the water by night, the unearthly honking noise that it makes on a hot still night, make it still so fascinating to the tourist.

In December 1972, the new Labor Government banned the export of crocodile skins, which was a move in the right direction, but there were hardly any left to export.

When one talks of the slaughter, the ultimate slaughter has been of the kangaroo. No creature has worked so hard for Australia. Apart from carrying the Qantas image, the kangaroo is being turned into wallets, purses, handbags, moccasins, desert boots, cushions, car mats, belts, rugs, passport covers, and there is a line in kangaroo-fur hot-water bottle covers. Furthermore one can buy him cut into little furry squares — the dinkum Aussie postcard. Then to remind us in more practical fashion of the slaughter there is the kangaroo bottle opener. This is the actual paw of a kangaroo, maybe eighteen inches long with the paw on one end and a chrome bottle opener can-puncher on the other.

Kangaroo skin is a best seller, and, of course, from kangaroo all the best toy koalas are made. This is an interesting irony. No longer can toy koalas be made of koala skin because the koala slaughter ended fifty years earlier. So kangaroos that are being slaughtered in the 1970s provide their fur for toy koalas. You can get all sorts of toy koalas — koalas with little koalas down their backs, koalas that zip down the middle so that your toy also serves as a pyjama case. Then there are koalas with alarm clocks in their stomachs, plus wind-up koalas that play 'Waltzing Matilda.'

Then there is the handbag trade and the shoe trade. One of the more recent inventions was the 'metalizing' of kangaroo skin so that it would shine like something out of a scene from *Goldfinger*. Again the pet food trade has been so great that some conservationists have even suggested that the kangaroo on the Australian coat of arms should be replaced with a can of a proprietary brand of pet food. Perhaps the pet food firm would even be happy to pay a royalty to the Commonwealth for the privilege.

Recently at a motel I met some Canadians who said they had driven across Australia from East to West and back, but they had seen very little wild life, and as for kangaroos they had seen only three or four and these had been in a zoo behind bars. There has been considerable controversy about this. Mr Alan Crompton, president of the Kangaroo Industries Association, said in 1970 he had seen letters from people complaining they had driven thousands of miles without seeing a kangaroo and he commented:

> These writers do not seem to appreciate that the kangaroo is not only shy of humans, but is also largely nocturnal and is rarely seen during daylight hours near human habitation. The letter writers might point out with equal significance that they have travelled thousands of miles without seeing an owl.

This, of course, is absurd. Kangaroos tend to be a little shy when they learn that the white man's hospitality is a .303 rifle fitted with a telescopic sight. Dr Francis Ratcliffe, a former head of the CSIRO wildlife research section, says kangaroos are often active well before dark and again after dawn has broken.

In a paper for the Australian Conservation Foundation he made an extremely important point — the mammal fauna of Australia is one of the most remarkable in the world, but mostly they are small, nocturnal and unco-operative, except for the kangaroo. This is different. When you get down to real facts, to most Australians, Australian wildlife is the kangaroo, this is what we think about.

It is very easy if one is sitting in a comfortable house in Melbourne, two hundred miles from the nearest kangaroo, to complain about kangaroo slaughter. 'Bleeding hearts' is the technical term for such people. Dr Ratcliffe explained that with the coming of civilization most native animals went into a decline, but the situation was different for the big kangaroo. It actually benefited from the ecological changes, the provision of water, the planting of crops, the new pastures for the grazing of sheep were also ideal for the grazing of kangaroos.

So the kangaroos multiplied to such a degree that graziers had no choice but to call in outside help. This is where we reached a situation of over-kill, in which the professional shooters took over. The really big kill started in 1957-58 and ultimately it worked up to more than two million red and grey kangaroos a year, and the big red kangaroos certainly were facing extinction.

In 1962 Professor Marshall made a plea to the Prime Minister, Mr R. G. Menzies, in which he said that if the killing continued at the same rate, and certain drought conditions followed, the kangaroo could go the way of the North American bison. He wrote this savage line about the professional kangaroo shooters:

> Here we have a group of near peasants — highly mobile in their Land Rovers, equipped with high-power rifles to which are fitted telescopic sights and spot-lights who, after rabbits became scarce, took to making a living by killing anything that hops.

By 1971 it was reported that the Australian kangaroo industries had a capital investment of fourteen million dollars, five hundred four hundred direct employees and an annual wage bill of $12,175,000. The 1969-70 export income for kangaroo skins was $1,560,000 and $362,000 for kangaroo meat. These figures were large but from the point of view of the economy, and the potential use of kangaroos live to the tourist industry, pitifully small.

Dr Ratcliffe believes professional shooters should be licensed and the killing regulated so that it does not go beyond the level of the natural increase in kangaroo population.

Yet perhaps the most famous massacre takes place annually at the Fingal Valley in Tasmania. It happens on a Sunday every June and it is organised by the Avoca Football Club. Two hundred Tasmanian shooters pay two dollars each to take part and the funds go to the club.

In 1967 the club shot a thousand wallabies and the *Herald* in Melbourne caused a fury of protests by printing on its front page a photograph of a shooter bashing a wallaby to death on a rock. Then when all was over there was a barbecue for the shooters and a couple of kegs of beer.

The club in its defence explained that there were forty thousand wallabies in a two hundred thousand acre area. How else were the farmers to keep them down? Would it be kinder to lay down poison?

Perhaps shooting is the only way, yet there seems to be something peculiarly Tasmanian in the way it is turned into an annual carnival, a picnic, and that people will actually come and pay money, to kill the inoffensive wallaby. As the *Australian* put it:

> Is the great god football so bereft of following and support that it needs so many sacrifices? Surely not, not even in a State where — judging by the recent election results — conservation and wild-life preservation rank nowhere.

Yet there is hope for the kangaroo. R. G. Menzies in 1962 replied to Professor Marshall that in his view the peak of the export trade in kangaroo meat was well past, but ministers were conferring and 'your representations will not be forgotten.'

Eleven years later the Federal Government did ban the export of kangaroo skins as from 1 April 1973. They had been leaving the country at a rate of a million a year.

Now the killing of kangaroos will no doubt be carried on just for the joy of it, like the strange shooting party which took place at Currarong, twenty miles east of Nowra, N.S.W., on 8 January 1973. A seventeen-year-old from Sydney was kangaroo shooting with some friends. He shot and killed a kangaroo and when he came near, a little joey jumped out of the dead mother's pouch. The youth took his rifle by the barrel and proceeded to club the baby kangaroo to death. As he did this the rifle went off, and shot him through the eye. He died on the way to hospital.

Australian wild life at last had struck back.
