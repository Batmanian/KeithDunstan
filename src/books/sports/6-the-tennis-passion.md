Tennis until the early 1900s was of no interest to the sport-mad watching public. Tennis was for the very nice, well brought up people, for those with money. Tennis was the feeling, a milk and water game.

The man who changed all that was Norman Brookes, the son of wealthy parents, yet a young man of unusual dedication. Even at nine years he was a good player, and as a Melbourne Grammar school boy he was brilliant. He played in his first tournament when he was only fourteen. He was a member of the comfortable Royal South Yarra Tennis Club, not the South Yarra Tennis that we know now, but a grubby little place by the railway line. It had a small wooden club house, asphalt courts and the enthusiasts played with black, soot-coloured tennis balls.

His wife, Dame Mabel Brookes, has described his fierce desire to win, to win at everything. 'In his younger days he had a strong temper, almost an obsession to have his own way, but years steeled that into determination.'

Tennis champions in search of fame had to pay their own fares. Norman Brookes first toured in 1905 and he caused much astonishment with his skill. It had not occurred to the tennis greats of England that anyone in the colonies could play the game. Norman Brookes won right through to the Challenge Round at Wimbledon to play off against the champion and trophy holder, the great H. L. Doherty.

Sir Norman Brookes recalled that nowadays all sorts of transport and grand treatment would be laid on for a Wimbledon finalist, but he had to find his own way to the courts, and naturally he went by train. At the railway booking office the clerk told him: 'We aren't selling any more tickets for Wimbledon.' 'But I'm playing in the Challenge Round,' pleaded Norman Brookes. The man was sceptical and only after much hesitation did he sell him a ticket. Along with his bag and two rackets, Norman Brookes had to crush into the train and stand all the way to Wimbledon Station. Then there was no hope of getting a cab. He had to walk all the way to the tennis courts. It was a good match, but Doherty beat him in straight sets.

The story was different in 1907. Again he had to pay his own way, but this time he won the Wimbledon singles, doubles and mixed doubles and, playing with the handsome young Anthony Wilding of New Zealand, he won the Davis Cup for Australasia. At first this did not cause much stir in Australia. Few people had heard of the Davis Cup. Since its beginning in 1900 only the United States and Britain had won the trophy, and Australian newspapers have never enthused over recording other nations' sporting victories. Our win received three cabled paragraphs in the Argus. Said the Argus: 'The news that Australasia has 'lifted' the Davis International Cup will be received with utmost possible satisfaction in Australia and especially in Victoria.'

These days the Davis Cup is moved in a special crate and insured for thousands of dollars. Norman Brookes brought it home in his luggage at his own expense. It cost him £7 10s. Dame Mabel told me:

We had it on our sideboard for years. You know the size of the thing. Big as a bath. It looked awful. I was glad when they took it away. Though the last time we had it here—I think we had the Frank Clarkes to dinner—I filled the Cup with red peonies and it looked very nice.

Having won the Davis Cup, Australia discovered it. Part of the discovery was the exciting news that the Challenge Round would take place in Australia, and in honour of Norman Brookes the event would be in Melbourne. The Lawn Tennis Association of Australia had no headquarters of its own (the Kooyong courts did not exist), so the match took place on the historic Warehousemen's Cricket Club's ground in St Kilda Road. The L.T.A.A. put up temporary grandstand accommodation which went right out across the footpath.

The great event took place a week after the Melbourne Cup and devoted racegoers from all over Australia stayed on for the big tennis. It was a classic, perhaps the most exciting Davis Cup battle ever staged. One racegoer said of the doubles contest on the Saturday: 'It was as if the Melbourne Cup was run neck and neck for half an hour.'

The temporary grandstands held between three and four thousand people. Everyone was there, the Governor, the Chief Justice, the Premier, Sir Thomas Bent. The Argus noted that there was an extraordinary number of women in the audience. Clearly this was a game for ladies. And how well-mannered they were. They did not hoist their sunshades in the air to impede the view of others. If a lady could not rest the shade low on her shoulder she dispensed with it and put a piece of paper on the back of her hat. Furthermore there were very few smokers, and the Argus believed that this was due to 'the charitable thought that there were ladies present'.

The opposing nations were Australasia and the United States of America. At the end of the first day they were one-all. Norman Brookes beat Fred B. Alexander 5-7, 9-7, 6-2, 4-6, 6-3. Brookes was stolid. Not once did he ever take his hat off; here was pure determination. When he served, said the Argus, he gave an explosive pant almost like the 'Uh-h' of an axeman breaking through the last of his log. Beals Wright beat Tony Wilding 3-6, 7-5, 6-3, 6-1.

The real thriller was the doubles, Brookes and Wilding v. Wright and Alexander. The Australasians won 6-4, 6-2, 5-7, 1-6, 6-4. During the first two sets it all seemed easy. Brookes and Wilding were going so well, the crowd thought they had won it already. So there was much cheering and sympathetic applause for the Americans. It was something they were to regret, for in the third set Wright became inspired. The players, almost nose to nose across the net, were hammering each other with volleying reflexes that seemed almost beyond belief.

At four games all in the final set some spectators left the ground because they could no longer stand the excitement. Brookes and Wilding managed to hold on to win, and the cheers at the finish were louder than anything ever heard before at an Australian tennis court.

The agony continued. The Monday was a Melbourne summer special, with a north wind and a temperature of 101°F. in the shade. The first match was a cliff-hanger exactly like the doubles. Norman Brookes lost in five sets to Beals Wright. At the beginning there seemed nothing he could not achieve. He didn't lose a game in the first set and lost only three in the second. Wright's game then began to improve and the strain appeared to tell on Brookes. He lost the third set 5-7, the fourth 2-6 and the final was a marathon 10-12. This match had taken three hours; it was already late in the afternoon and the score was two rubbers each.

Tony Wilding was what would be described now as a physical fitness fanatic. In this pre-Harry Hopman era he used to run and do physical training to keep in shape. He believed, too, in the value of relaxation and he thought it unwise to put himself through the strain of watching the Brookes-Wright match. He went away and lay down in a cool, shady place and in this pre-radio time he had no idea what was happening until the match was all over.

This was a different style of match. Brookes and Wright fought a net-rushing, serve and volleying battle, the type that would have been completely acceptable at Wimbledon sixty years later. Wilding and Alexander played an old-time classic, long-range artillery launched from the baseline, elegant ground play, faultlessly accurate drives, long rallies. Alexander could not handle the power in Tony Wilding's 'twist serve' and this was the easiest win in the three days—6-3, 6-4, 6-1.

Perhaps the cat nap principle worked, because the smooth Tony Wilding, never a hair out of place, was unconcerned throughout. At the finish the crowd streamed onto the court, picked up Tony Wilding and chaired him into the pavilion. In its leading article the Argus said it was all magnificent. Such a display of tennis, probably, had not been surpassed in any part of the world.

So the long era began. Alison Danzig, the great tennis writer of the New York Times, wrote that the Davis Cup win of Australasia in 1907 was the greatest boon tennis ever had. No other country had such a widespread interest in tennis as Australasia. Australasia won the Davis Cup in 1907, 1908, 1909, 1911 and 1914, always with Brookes and Wilding in the side. But 1914 was the last for Tony Wilding, he was killed in action by a German shell on 9 May 1915.

Norman Brookes was not young when he started his international career. Even when he made his first assault on the Cup in 1907 he was thirty years old, and he was still playing Davis Cup tennis at forty-three when he took the great Bill Tilden to four sets.

So he was the first of the Australian tennis heroes and the proportions of the worship he commanded can be gauged from his wedding. In 1911 he married Mabel Balcombe Emmerton, and it was one of the greatest weddings Melbourne has known. There were barricades along Swanston Street to keep back the crowds. Dame Mabel Brookes wrote that Buzzards the cake-makers sent from England 'a monster wedding cake with silver nets, tennis rackets and tennis balls on it.' There were ten bridesmaids and her mother had the lace for her veil made in Venice.

The Very Reverend Archbishop of Melbourne, Dr Clarke, assisted by the Reverend Evelyn Snodgrass and the Precentor of the Cathedral, Reverend H. Kelly, conducted the wedding ceremony. The 'wedding tea' took place at Raveloe in a large marquee decorated with white poppies and wheat ears. The society journal Table Talk said it was useless to list all the guests, because all society was represented. Actually Table Talk gave one a clear idea who was there, because it listed all the wedding presents and who gave them. So did the Herald and the list filled two entire columns.

The bridegroom gave the bride a diamond marquise ring, a diamond star, a pair of brass candelabra and a vase. The bride gave the bridegroom a gold watch. The bride's mother gave the bride-groom a set of pearl cuff-links and to the bride, a diamond necklace, a diamond star, a silver jug and silver liqueur glasses. The father of the bride gave her a diamond sun burst, an antique Welsh dresser, an antique clock, Venetian chairs and a gold bag. The mother of the bridegroom gave a chest of silver, then there was a cheque and a set of hand-painted Venetian china cups and plate. His Excellency the Governor and Lady Carmichael gave an enamelled jar. And so it went on and on. Table Talk said the presents were arranged in a large billiard room at Raveloe, but there was enough silver there to fill every room in the house. It was intriguing to note that they received at least five sets of solid silver fruit knives and forks, five sets of fish knives and forks, plus an enormous variety of silver vases, claret jugs, decanters and silver fruit dishes. An Edwardian society wedding was both unnerving and damaging to the bank account. When one knew that the size and quality of the gift would be thoroughly announced in both the newspapers and society journals one could not afford to give a mere set of coffee spoons.

So Norman Brookes was the first and perhaps the greatest of the tennis heroes. He was president of the Lawn Tennis Association of Australia from 1926 to 1955 and he was the acknowledged 'czar of tennis'. For his services to the game he received our first sporting knighthood in 1939 and became our first sporting knight.

There have been other tennis heroes: Gerald Patterson, the Wimbledon champion of 1919 and 1922, the imperturbable Jack Crawford, who won in 1933. Jack Crawford received the highest testimonial ever paid to an Australian sporting hero—$23,866 handed over by the Lord Mayor of Sydney, Harry Jensen, in 1959.

But in the public hero stakes the man to rival Norman Brookes was Frank Sedgman. Already he was greatly celebrated in 1950 when he went to Forest Hills, New York, and won back the Davis Cup for the first time since John Bromwich and Adrian Quist won it in 1939. But his fame took on new dimensions after the Davis Cup win in 1951. The scene was Sydney's White City courts, just after Christmas. In the first rubber Mervyn Rose lost to Vic Seixas in straight sets, then Sedgman evened matters by beating Ted Schroeder 6-4, 6-3, 4-6, 6-4. Rose went down again in straight sets to Schroeder. So the score was two-all when Sedgman met Seixas. The Herald reported that meanwhile in Melbourne, hundreds of city shoppers and workers blocked footpaths and arcades, crowded hotel bars and shops, listening to Davis Cup broadcasts. Work almost ceased in offices throughout the city. One man sat down on the edge of a gutter in Collins Street to listen to a portable radio. Soon a small crowd had him hemmed in. 'One of the most magnificent exhibitions of tennis ever seen in any country,' . . . 'one of the best players who ever lived . . .' were two of the comments.

Now the big question was, 'When would Frank Sedgman turn professional?' Jack Kramer, coach of the U.S. Davis Cup team, gave some sharp advice: 'Sedgman should not consider leaving the amateur ranks until he has won Wimbledon. He's too good a player to sacrifice the chance of having his name inscribed in the hall of world champions.'

Of course he was right. The whole nation clearly breathed a sigh of relief when Sedgman returned to Melbourne still an amateur. But how was it possible to keep him that way? He was in love with Jean Spence, a local girl, and about to marry her, so the Sun News-Pictorial decided that Melbourne and Australia should show proper gratitude by giving the couple a cash wedding present.

The paper wrote: 'Ping, Ping, Ping, Ping—Everyone who listened to the Davis Cup matches must remember these sounds. And the final ping was always Sedgman's. All Australia is grateful to him.'

Donations flowed in. The Lord Mayor, Sir Thomas Nettlefold; Gladys Moncrieff, the singer; Edgar Coles of Coles Stores; John Wren, Harry Hopman, Bill Dooley, the bookmaker; all sent their cheques. All public tennis courts were challenged to give money. Finally the grand wedding present total came to £5473 10s 3d. And that was not all. The City of Brunswick staged its own special show at the Padua Theatre. Outside in the foyer there was a big placard. 'Local Boy Makes Good. Let's Buy Him a Wedding Present.' The present was a canteen of cutlery.

There were cries of pain from the United States. The New York World Telegram and Sun demanded that the International Lawn Tennis Federation publish an official ruling on Frank Sedgman's amateur status. The newspaper published a cartoon showing Sedgman, racket in hand, dancing in a shower of pound notes, and singing 'Let it snow, Let it snow.' The cartoon ran the lines:

Oh yez, Oh yez, we have our hassles over eligibility, expense accounts and 'what is an amateur anyway?' over here in the good old USA. But in the land of marsupials and the duckbill platypus, Down Under, things are more simple—for example, the case of Frank 'Stirling' Sedgman, the world's greatest amateur tennis player, pound for pound . . . Have you made your contribution to the Keep Frank an Amateur wedding?

Sir Norman Brookes thought the fuss was of no consequence and the ILTF would do nothing. He was correct.

There was a civic reception for Frank and Jean at the Town Hall with a joyful crowd of ten thousand, but the spectacular affair was the wedding at Toorak Presbyterian Church. The Argus reported that 2,500 hysterical onlookers 'mobbed' the bridal pair; the bride, formerly Miss Jean Spence, was jostled and knocked about in a tortuous struggle from the church to the car. A 75-year-old woman staggered out of the crowd, weeping, and two girls were badly shaken. The Argus added that it took five minutes for the Sedgmans to scramble into their car. About 90 per cent of the crowd were women 'who had just come to have a peep at the bride and groom'.

In July Sedgman won the Wimbledon title, beating Jaroslav Drobny, 4-6, 6-2, 6-3, 6-2. The awful spectre of the loss of Sedgman to the professionals arose once again. But he took over a modern service station in Hawthorn only half a mile from the Kooyong tennis courts. All seemed to be well and the Argus reported: 'Frank Sedgman will never turn professional' it never seriously considered it.

But it was as inevitable as the coming of summer that both Sedgman and Ken McGregor would turn professional immediately after the Davis Cup to be played in Adelaide. Later Sir Norman Brookes revealed that some determined efforts were made to keep them as amateurs, even more determined than wedding presents. First, on 11 October, with the aid of Sir Frank Beaurepaire, he offered them a chain of service stations which would operate under the name of 'Sedgman-McGregor'. Next, if they were prepared to remain amateurs, he offered them jobs with a life insurance company at £5,000 a year each. This did not cause much surprise, although the Sun News-Pictorial commented: 'Why were these inexperienced young men offered salaries which the great majority of workers for insurance companies cannot hope to reach in a life-time? Not for sticking close to their desks, but to play tennis.' Bill Tipping, columnist on the Herald, commented, 'That job still going, Sir Norman. Our backhand's not so hot, but maybe we can get Harry Hopman to work on it and we'll practise every day for any job that will net us up to £5,000 a year.'

Meanwhile Sedgman and McGregor went to Adelaide for the Davis Cup; their expenses were the standard rate for an amateur, £4 4s. a day. As soon as the Cup was over, the story came out in the open. Sedgman would make $100,000 (£44,600) in his first five months in the United States playing against Kramer and Pancho Segura. McGregor would receive £11,200. Sedgman's was the greatest offer ever made to an amateur tennis player—£44,600 for five months' work! Even Mr Churchill, Prime Minister of Great Britain, received only £10,000 a year, commented the Herald.

So Frank Sedgman became a professional. Was he still loved? One newspaper conducted a street poll to find out about that wedding present. Was he under a moral obligation to his admirers

to remain an amateur? All those interviewed said, 'No'. Mr George Millar of Bellevue Avenue, Greenwich, N.S.W., said:

I think Sedgman and McGregor are quite right. If you or I were in the same position would we knock back the chance of economic security? Why should these two great fellows continue being good fellows to a public which has yet to know the meaning of an honest day's work for an honest day's pay.

Now all seemed to be lost. But it is remarkable how quickly sporting heroes can be found in Australia. In 1953 two Sydney eighteen-year-olds, Lew Hoad, the son of a fitter and turner, and Ken Rosewall, the son of a grocer, won almost all the major tennis titles in Australia. Times had changed from the days when tennis was mainly a game for the public school boys, like Norman Brookes. Lew Hoad won four titles and Ken Rosewall won two, including the national singles. Between them they won the national doubles. In the hero department they seemed in the nursery stage, but they were the best and the logical choice to defend the Davis Cup at Kooyong that Christmas. Now they were both nineteen, Hoad the younger by three weeks.

The Challenge Round took place at Kooyong before eighteen thousand people. Early it looked hopeless. Hoad beat Seixas savagely and quickly, 6-4, 6-2, 6-3, but Rosewall went down easily to Tony Trabert, and Hoad and Rex Hartwig lost the doubles, a match they were expected to win. So, down one rubber to two, Lew Hoad had to face the highly skilled, experienced, in-form Tony Trabert. The match was a marathon fought in the rain and Hoad won 13-11, 6-3, 2-6, 3-6, 7-5. The Sun reported: 'The wildest scenes ever at Kooyong followed Hoad's winning stroke. Hats, cushions, newspapers and umbrellas were thrown in the air. The Governor, Sir Dallas Brooks, was one who stood and applauded for some minutes after the match.' Colin Long, former Australian Davis Cup player, described him as 'Hoad the Mighty' and suggested that a monument should be erected at Kooyong in his honour. Rosewall then beat Seixas 6-2, 2-6, 6-3, 6-4.

The following year came the big surprise. Trabert and Seixas beat their old-young enemies with ease. In sports-conscious Australia it is a crime to be a champion and not deliver the goods. The crime was to win the Davis Cup at nineteen and lose it at twenty. No longer was Lew Hoad the golden boy of tennis and Harry Hopman, in his rating of players, dropped him from first to seventh.

Tennis writer David Burke said:

What has gone wrong with Hoad? I believe it is simply that we are expecting too much of boys who are expected to be men once they take up a racket. So great upon them is the pressure of our tennis system, which plays them non-stop around the world year after year, that we're in danger of choking out all their potential long before they reach their peak.

Harry Hopman, the famed captain-manager, received much publicity on this, particularly from London and New York newspapers. They accused him of being the tennis Svengali, or running a conveyor-belt system for producing infant tennis players. The New York World Telegram and Sun ran a sports cartoon depicting Harry Hopman with a long black whip standing over a cowed Lew Hoad. Hoad was kneeling with his hands covering his face, saying, 'Don't whip me, no mo', massah. Ah' tarred'.

The newspapers told of the soulless tennis machine producing automatons who had to lead monastic lives. The Daily Express tennis writer said:

Few fathers would want their sons to sacrifice the fun of life for the fame of a Hoad or a Rosewall if they appreciated the iron discipline that has to be endured under a boss like the Australian team manager, Harry Hopman.

In the Wimbledon of 1954, Jaroslav Drobny beat Ken Rosewall in the longest final ever, 13-11, 4-6, 6-2, 9-7. The Express man said:

Old Drob is so fallible—and so very human. That sad smile to the heavens when he occasionally misses an easy smash, is more appealing than the dead-pan determination of Rosewall.

There were stories too that the Australian players were restless, angry, fed up with the Hopman discipline. He could impose fines of five shillings for going without a shave, five shillings for banging one's tennis racket in temper, ten shillings for not being in bed on time.

Lew Hoad wrote later that they did not mind this fining; they rather enjoyed the joke. He only became angry with Harry Hopman once. It was the night before the final of the Pacific South-west doubles in Los Angeles. It was almost the end of tour, and he and Rosewall came in an hour after curfew.

'I was in the bathroom washing my teeth, when Hopman strolled in,' wrote Lew Hoad.

'Did you have a good time?' asked Hopman.

'Terrific,' I replied, trying to be brave.

'Do you think it was worth ten dollars apiece?' he said sharply and walked out.

Hoad said this was a mighty bite out of their finances, but he cut the fine by half when they won the match the next day. But the disgraced 'tennis twins' found form again and were restored to glory to win the Davis Cup back in August 1955. Robert Macdonald in one of the classics of euphoric sports reporting, wrote a story in the form of a letter to Mrs Rosewall and Mrs Hoad. It appeared on the front pages of newspapers all around Australia. It began:

Forest Hills, New York.

Dear Mrs Rosewall and Mrs Hoad,

You would have been proud of your sons today. They were tremendous. They were terrific. To 12,000 stunned Americans who saw it happen, they were unbelievable.

They went out on to the green and brown mottled centre court here as the enemy, and ran the American boys to a frazzle. You can clear the mantelpiece. Barring hurricanes, earthquakes, or the greatest upset since Humpty Dumpty, the Davis Cup's coming home.

There were only about 200 of us Australians there—a tiny island of cheers in the vast concrete amphitheatre of groans, moans and disillusionment. But when Lew crashed down with the last big one in the gathering dusk, and a weary Trabert flipped it weakly into the net with the 12,000 Americans knew we were there.

Hoad, Rosewall and Rex Hartwig returned to a civic reception and ticker-tape parade in Sydney. Thousands lined the side of the road on the way from the airport. There was a further civic reception for the team in Melbourne and the Lord Mayor, Cr Selleck, launched a testimonial fund for Harry Hopman. Two former Lord Mayors, Cr O. J. Nilsen and Sir Harold Gengoult-Smith refused to attend. Sir Harold said: 'It is the first time a civic reception has been turned into a begging party. I disapprove most strongly.'

But the money came in, with many distinguished contributors ranging from the Prime Minister, Mr Menzies, to Walter Lindrum and John Landy. Then there was £100 from another noted name, Frank Sedgman. Harry Hopman received a cheque for £7,000.

Lew beat Ken in the all-Australian Wimbledon final of 1956 and now the fear of their turning professional was a continuing serial with almost daily episodes. Already it had been going on for two years. When Kramer first announced that he was ready to take Hoad, Sir Norman Brookes said bitterly: 'I thought it wouldn't be long before Kramer would be butting in'.

Now he wanted them both together. The professional ranks were ageing—Sedgman was ageing and a little thin. The serial had particularly passionate episodes all through October 1956. Reporters covered every movement of the saga, and there were mystery meetings between the Hoads and Jack Kramer, and there were mystery pictures of Kramer's representative, Frank Sedgman, talking through a car window to Lew. Is this the last chapter asked the Herald? The stakes went up from £20,000 to £25,000 to £30,000 for a year's tennis. It was a marvellous cliff-hanger. On 27 October there were front page stories that Lew would turn professional the next day. Hoad's parents had said that Rosewall and Hoad had both signed their contracts. On the 28th Hoad announced he would not turn professional, so that he could play in the Davis Cup once more and try amateur again for the Wimbledon title with perhaps the grand slam of the major titles. So Kramer returned to the United States with no Australians. There were relieved newspaper editorials praising the tennis twins.

Rosewall turned professional alone for £30,000, immediately after the Davis Cup. Lew proved his shrewdness, waited another year, and won another Wimbledon title. He knew his value and he turned professional for £55,795. Norman Brookes was upset. 'It's a poor sort of a show,' he said, and claimed that Hoad should be sued for breach of contract with the Lawn Tennis Association. Hoad went away on an agreement that he would not turn professional until 31 January, after the Davis Cup.

The champions seemed to come off the production line endlessly, like Holden cars. Rod Laver had no trouble with Orlando Sirola of Italy in the Davis Cup Challenge Round of 1961. The score 6-1, 6-4, 6-3.

Lew wasn't worried. When told of Sir Norman's comments he said: 'Well, good on him'. Lew did not have many critics. One newspaper even printed a picture of his little daughter with the line, 'That little girl you've seen photographed recently waiting for Dad to come home—her name's Jane Hoad and she's only 18 months old—may well be worth all the criticism about a broken contract.'

Tennis in Australia reached its heroic peak with the Hoad-Rosewall juniors. When the pair failed in their attempt to hold the Cup in December 1954, there was a world record crowd of 25,578 spectators at White City in Sydney. More than nine thousand applications for seats were returned with a profit of £65,188.

There were many more champions to come—Wimbledon champions, Ashley Cooper, Neale Fraser, Rod Laver, Roy Emerson, John Newcombe and Margaret Court. Australia's mastery of tennis was so complete that the public even became bored.

It did not matter any more when great players turned professional, there was always another champion waiting in the queue. Rod Laver returned to win Wimbledon for the third time when open tennis was introduced in 1968.

So there was a general decline. The 1969 National championships, the first of the National Opens, was a $20,000 flop. Even the big match between the local Brisbane heroes, Rod Laver and Roy Emerson, could draw only four thousand people.

Yet the old fascination was still there, and undoubtedly this was due to the females, and particularly one female, Evonne Goolagong. Margaret Court won the title in 1963, 1965, 1970 and again in 1972, but Margaret was a little too cold, efficient and determined. Evonne was a much better story. She was part-Aboriginal, from poor parents from the little town of Barellan in New South Wales. It was an even better story than the rise of Lew Hoad. At nineteen she beat Margaret Court, 6-4, 6-1, to become Wimbledon champion; an Aboriginal girl champion of the world.

There was one headline THE QUEEN IS DEAD—LONG LIVE THE QUEEN, plus the ecstatic words:

The tiny Riverina township of Barellan is bleary-eyed today. Few of the 900 population moved from their TV sets until well after midnight. And most of them were up until the early hours of the morning toasting their local sweetheart.

How could one analyse the reasons for the burst of national joy over Evonne Goolagong? She was young and pretty, cuddly as some people called her, not like the possibly over-athletic Australian super-power, Margaret Court. This was a new burst of Australian magic when after a series of Davis Cup failures there was a fear that perhaps the old magic was beginning to disappear. This helped overcome racist White Australia feelings of guilt, knowledge of neglect and ill treatment of black Australians.

She came home to the typical 'triumph' now always accorded to an Australian sporting hero. In Sydney the Lord Mayor, Alderman McDermott, gave her the key to the city, 'The city is yours Evonne'. She drove through the town in an open sports car and it was reported that 'thousands in the streets went wild', showered her with ticker-tape, ripped up telephone books and sprinkled the bits out the windows as she drove past.

She had her handprint preserved in wet concrete outside McEwans department store in Bourke Street, Melbourne. The Australia Day Council declared her the 'Australian of the Year' and she received an M.B.E. in the New Year honours.

Margaret Court won back the Australian Wimbledon title in 1972, and beat her again for the Australian title in January 1973, but it was interesting to note from the cheers of the crowd that Evonne was still the heroine; a young lady who is moving progressively towards a fortune. She could even become a millionaire like the successful Rod Laver; and while that sort of money is available there are sure to be more tennis heroes and heroines.
