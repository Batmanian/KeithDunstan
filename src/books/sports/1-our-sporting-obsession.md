title: Our Sporting Obsession
date: 1973-11-11

John Snow, the English fast bowler, has been eloquent on our sporting madness. He wrote: 'In my opinion Melburnians are like piranha fish when it comes to sport. They will devour anything that will satisfy their appetite for competition.' Vividly he told what it was like to be out in the middle of the vast Melbourne Cricket Ground with sixty thousand hostile pairs of eyes, glaring: 'When you are a gladiator at that colosseum—and the villain of the piece—you can almost feel the jaws of the Melbourne mob snapping at you.'

He told a revealing story about the Melbourne Test Match of January 1971. 'I became very annoyed when a ten-year-old boy kept calling out to me "Snow, you bastard". His father sat beside him apparently approving of such behaviour. Having taken a stomachful of this treble voice's chirping, my school teacher's instincts came out. I strode across to him and said: "You have too much to say for such a small bloke." Whereupon his father spoke up for the first time: "Give the kid your autograph you mug," he ordered, between sips from his can.'

Undoubtedly the child can be forgiven, for he is showing a correct and manly will-to-win attitude. Sport is the ultimate Australian super-religion, the one thing every Australian believes in passionately. Sport is wholesome. It can do no wrong, and, best of all, it spreads the fame of Australians overseas.

Not to be keen on sport is, therefore, unclean, unmanly, even homosexual, and definitely contrary to the ethics and super-religion of the nation. In 1967 I started an Anti-Football League, a fun thing more than anything else, to counter the overwhelming drenching of the Melbourne winter season by football ballyhoo.

The hostility of many of the letters was interesting. One letter said: 'If you and other fairies like you don't like football then why don't you go and live in Sydney, go and live anywhere, and leave the place to decent people who like it.' But particularly intriguing was the number of people who accused me of being a Communist. The Anti-Football League had a square red badge of protest, symbolic of something that would not bounce. That red colour was indicative. Anyone who did not believe in something as true and as fine as Melbourne football had to be anti-Australian and therefore a Communist.

The situation has changed very little in the past hundred years. Here is an extract from a Sunday night sermon given by the Reverend Dr Bevan in the Collins Street Independent Church, Melbourne. Dr Bevan was renowned for his courage:

No one can fail to observe the large part of public attention which sport and athletics and games take up at the present time. If you listen to the conversation in public places, in railway carriages or elsewhere some reference is sure to be heard to an event, a race, a game, matches of all sorts between colonies, between cities, between nations.

See how large a space is devoted to sport in the newspapers. A king or a general or a statesman might die and the matter is dismissed in a few lines, but if two gentlemen who belong to the prize ring meet, it occupies half a column. There is not the slightest doubt that your pugilist is a much more important member of society than any monarch, and they, of course, are unerring and certain guides of all human thought in modern life.

Henry Lawson put it better:

<em>In a Land where sport is sacred,<br>
Where the Laborer is God,<br>
You must pander to the people,<br>
Make a hero of a clod,</em>

But, Dr Bevan made those remarks on 12 June 1892, and what is different now? Well, the fight would get much more than half a column, it would get five columns, and, even though it had been covered live on television the night before. Then the pugilist, when he died, unquestionably would have a finer, better publicized funeral than the politician. A king, if we could find one, would run him close, but who rates obituaries any more, only the super rich, prime ministers and sportsmen.

As for sporting news coverage, there is a difference from Dr Bevan's day. There is much more of it, and the so-called more sober newspapers have a bigger coverage even than the tabloids. During the football season of 1972 both the *Age* and the *Australian* had weekly, four-page, drop-in sporting supplements, in addition to their already vast coverage of other sports.

In 1962 Professor Henry Mayer made an analytical survey of the contents of Australian newspapers. He found that the quality newspapers covered sport in more detail than the popular journals. But this was the national average: take away the advertisements and there was actually 41.4 per cent news space; of this foreign news took 7.4 per cent, political-social-economic news 8.2 per cent, sport 15 per cent and other news 10.8 per cent.

This is the impression one gets on suddenly returning to Australia—the overwhelming coverage received by sport. The wife of the former American ambassador, Mrs Ed Clark commented: 'Living in Australia is like living in a gymnasium—there's always somebody practising something.'

But that is not all. Dr Bevan did not have to endure television and radio. The non sport-lover should not attempt to turn on his radio on a Saturday—there is a mish-mash of football-racing-dogs on four stations. Television is even worse—here there is no escape. As I write there will be fourteen hours fifty-five minutes of football on television this week, plus ten hours fifty-five minutes of other sports.

Ralph Broom, a television critic, recently gave a rebellious cry of pain when he listed all the sport on during one week—horse-racing, boxing, wrestling, soccer, tennis, cricket, and on one terrible afternoon four and a half hours of car racing—and he made the plea:

Is it possible that someone along the exclusive mahogany rows of televisionland could believe there are people like me—quite a lot of them—who are not interested in sport? You could just possibly be driving us to the brink of self-destruction.

Undoubtedly radio and television help to create the sporting heroes and heroines, but it has to be a mutual thing, after all a crow needs a carcass to feed on, and our sporting heroes are close to being more important than any other class in the community. Each year to mark 26 January, the Australia Day Council announces the 'Australian of the Year', the Australian who has done most to bring honour to Australia. From 1960 to 1972 these Australians have received the title: Sir Macfarlane Burnet, Joan Sutherland, Jock Sturrock, Sir John Eccles, Dawn Fraser, Sir Robert Helpmann, Jack Brabham, Lord Casey, The Seekers, Lionel Rose, His Eminence Sir Norman Thomas Cardinal Gilroy, Evonne Goolagong and Shane Gould.

Note the breakdown—two scientists, an opera singer, a popular musical group, a cleric, a statesman, and six sportsmen and women. The sports outnumbered all the others put together.

In 1970 the *Australian* ran a poll to find who were the men and women Australians most admired. Sir Robert Menzies topped the poll, Rolf Harris, the television entertainer, was second, Sir Reginald Ansett was third, and Ron Clarke, the athlete, was fourth. Perhaps this had an influence on the Government, because almost at the same time the Liberals made Clarke a film censor. When the Customs Minister, Mr Chipp, appointed him to the Commonwealth Film Board of Review Clarke admitted frankly, 'I know nothing about films', but Mr Chipp was unworried. He said Clarke represented the man on the street, and that he had had contact with youth. Few people questioned the idea. If a man is marvellous at running the 10,000 metres he must be a fine protector of public morals.

The women 'most admired' of the people polled could not think of one woman to admire, but finally they selected Dame Mabel Brookes first, and Margaret Court, the tennis player, second. The men (those who could bring themselves to admit that they admired any woman) voted for sporting ladies—Margaret Court, Evonne Goolagong, Dawn Fraser, Karen Moras (swimming) and Heather Mackay (squash). This was particularly interesting. Women are male-dominated in Australia. They rarely succeed in politics, or fill top executive positions, but sport is one way they can break through the barrier and achieve national admiration.

Then the *Australian* had another idea. They listed the names of twenty-six well-known Australians and those polled were asked to identify them. The best known was Jack Brabham, the racing car driver—96 per cent knew him. Then came Graham Kennedy, the T.V. compere, with 91 per cent; Sir Reginald Ansett, 91 per cent; Bobby Limb, 90 per cent; Rolf Harris, 89 per cent and Ron Clarke, 85 per cent.

Dr H. C. Coombs was way down the line with 43 per cent, and Patrick White, the best known author on the list, rated only 16 per cent.

Sport is so useful for winning public esteem that it is impossible to ignore it. Sir Robert Menzies was never known to be particularly devoted to sport before he entered politics, but then he became the nation's number one cricket watcher. It was interesting how missions to London and crises at Downing Street invariably coincided with Test Matches at Lords. If Sir Robert was number one, then Dr Evatt, leader of the opposition, was number two, and he once even wrote a lengthy article for *Wisden*.

But Alan Trengove reported it was Sir Robert who cornered the literary market, by writing forewords for our Test cricketers' books. Anyone who was on good terms with Richie Benaud and Co. could obviously be trusted.

Sir Robert's successor, the late Harold Holt, as soon as he became Prime Minister had himself photographed in a spear-fishing skin-diving outfit. And, after he died, and the struggle was on to find a new Prime Minister, most of the candidates set out to prove their sport-loving capacities. Mr Gorton was shown beside various swimming pools.

Mr McMahon had a squash racket in his hand. There was no doubt about his capacities, his youthful vigour. There were frequent stories that his skill at squash was so great he could even compete with the great Geoff Hunt. Mr Snedden gave an interview on the tennis court—it was hard not to like a tennis player.

But it was always important to associate oneself with a football club. Sir Robert Menzies was number one at Carlton, Mr Arthur Calwell was number one at North Melbourne and Sir John McEwen was a Fitzroy man.

For many years there were ugly doubts about the way Mr Gough Whitlam was heading. There was no evidence that he had even been to an Australian Rules football match. His entry in *Who's Who in Australia* disclosed no sporting interest beyond the Cabramatta Bowling Club. However, in 1967 he had himself photographed being fitted for a Collingwood football guernsey. Then, upon becoming Prime Minister he acquired the extraordinary modern practice of switching faiths. He became a 'ten-year man' for he became number one guernsey man at Geelong. He even had himself photographed with the immortal Polly Farmer. One wondered what had happened during the six intervening seasons. Had the mighty Magpies failed to push him to power?

Representatives of the Queen are also well aware of the necessity of being associated with a football team. Sir Dallas Brooks, formerly Governor of Victoria, was number one at Richmond for many years. His successor, Major-General Sir Rohan Delacombe, arrived in Melbourne straight from an assignment as British Commander in Berlin. He had barely heard of the Richmond Football Club, and had never seen them play, but he was well advised, for without hesitation he succeeded Sir Dallas Brooks as number one member.

A sportsman can do no harm in Australian eyes. The famous John Wren, who rose to riches through the underworld and made a fortune through the Collingwood Tote in defiance of the law, endeared himself and even acquired public devotion through generosity to sport. Always he was helping sportsmen—footballers, boxers, cricketers, billiard players—but more important, he was the benefactor of the Collingwood Football Club and he used to give £1,000 a season to be divided up among the best and fairest players.

His biographer, Niall Brennan, gives an interesting insight:

When I asked a random group in a Collingwood hotel bar what the name of John Wren meant to them, the answer was simple: he was a life member of the Club and a Club benefactor. His other activities were of little importance compared to this.

Sir Robert Menzies, when he was Prime Minister, had an aloofness which inspired fear among many of his colleagues. Menzies seemed happier and more relaxed when surrounded by cricketers or footballers, men below him in intellectual stature. Whenever a touring international cricket side came to Australia there was always the match to be played against the Prime Minister's XI in Canberra. Sir Robert would call on his old favourites to play for him—Lindsay Hassett, Keith Miller, Ray Lindwall.

Indeed sport is a very useful aid to the politician. In the United States it is different. There the show business star is the idol and a man has crooned his way to twenty million dollars, this somehow puts him in a position to give advice on politics. Ronald Reagan became Governor of California on his record as a successful Hollywood movie star. Presidential candidates in recent years have all looked to the show business celebrities to back them in their campaigns. Sinatra and Dean Martin were for Kennedy, Paul Newman and Joanne Woodward were for McGovern. Sammy Davis junior was for Nixon, and even Lainie Kazan came out for Nixon, thus winning the derisive comment, 'Tits for Nixon'.

But in Australia the footballer, the cricketer or even the cyclist is the man to be revered, and frequently he moves into politics. We have yet to find a footballer or a cricketer capable of becoming Prime Minister, but many have become ministers. In 1949, in a tough, vitriolic campaign in Corio, the electors preferred to put their trust in an ex-world champion cyclist, Hubert Opperman. He became Minister for Shipping and Transport and Minister for Immigration.

Just to mention some of the others: in Tasmania there is Darrel Baldock, M.L.A., former captain of St Kilda football team; in Adelaide there is Gil Langley, M.L.A., former Test cricketer; in Melbourne there is Brian Dixon, M.L.A., former Test star wingman; Sam Loxton, M.L.A., former Test cricket all-rounder, and Geelong has the former Geelong football star, Neil Trezise, M.L.A.

Mac Holten, Minister for Repatriation in the McMahon Government, was a Collingwood football player; Don Chipp, former Minister for Customs, played football for Fitzroy and was a professional sprinter; Francis Stewart, Federal Minister for Tourism and Sport, is famed as a front row forward for the Sydney Rugby League team of Canterbury-Bankstown.

A sporting background does not automatically get one elected but it is an excellent start. It helped Jack 'Captain Blood' Dyer, the mighty Richmond ruckman, gain pre-selection for a Victorian Labor seat, and when Ron Barassi, the football rover, says that his next adventure may be politics, few people see anything illogical in the idea. They say, 'Why not? Look how well he coached Carlton and North Melbourne.'

Australians have always been passionate about sport. In the 1870s and 1880s when the young nation first began to produce its world champion scullers, when its young men first beat the English cricketers, when boxers like Larry Foley proved themselves in world class, there was a belief that perhaps our isolation, clean living, good food and superior climate, were producing a race of supermen. That belief exists still, for unquestionably per head of population Australia has provided more world champions than any people on earth: world champions in boxing, running, cycling, swimming, tennis, football, cricket, rowing, sailing, squash, surfing, horse-riding, golf—perhaps only skiing misses out, yet even here the enthusiasts are working with a proper, chilling determination. The Australian Malcolm Milne is among the best half dozen skiers in the world.

It is important to examine the reasons why this sporting obsession came about. The first and most obvious reason is this: the early settlers came from the most sports conscious nation on earth, Britain. There was a cricket match in Sydney as early as 1803; there was an organized race meeting at Parramatta in 1810 and rowing events started with the First Fleet.

The climate, of course, was ideal. Sports could be played all the year around. The big cities grew in temperate zones where long warm summers were guaranteed. There was never a shortage of playing space, never a shortage of ground. There were extraordinary figures and it was discovered that it was possible to play cricket and football on the same turf, something that is still unheard of in England. Every year they witnessed this miracle of nature, growth so lush it was possible to roll out a turf cricket pitch two to three weeks after the football Grand Final.

John A. Daley, senior lecturer at Adelaide Teachers College, has written that a distinctive Australian 'type' developed during the nineteenth century, who greatly influenced the social attitudes of early colonists. The bush and the outback became the inspiration for a national outlook. There was almost a mystique about the Australian pioneer—here was toughness, manliness, a never-give-in attitude—here he was tall, sun-tanned, immensely resourceful and above all independent. The legend was broadened and enlarged by C. J. Dennis, Henry Lawson, Joseph Furphy and others. Daley wrote that this bush ethos caught the Australian imagination, it became the expression of zeitgeist—the intellectual, moral, social and cultural climate of that time.

Then, of course, there was the preponderance of males in the pioneer society, men who were forced to get together for companionship, for amusement. There was the feeling of mateship as described by Sidney J. Baker, which was more than half being friends, buddies or pals, but a mutual dependence, a sticking together in times of crisis whether it be in a war or a drought; ideal also for team activities.

Some have even suggested that the super-male-ego of the Australian was heavily charged with homosexuality, but whether this was true, old-fashioned headmasters of the Dr Arnold variety always had the answer: 'Get out there boy and run—get rid of those surplus energies'. The urge for sport has always been supported as a method for easing Australian masculine aggressions. This theory is supported by editorial in early Australian newspapers; the idea being that sport is essential for the making and toughening of a nation.

*Bell's Life in Sydney* in 1845 urged the young men of the country to get out and boat race, play cricket, skittles, quoits, to throw the discus and indulge in bowling and archery . . .

Let the youth, the patriot, the moralist, the philosopher combine to set foot on such designs and we shall presently see the lank and sickly native lad merging into the robust and manly man—nor will philosophy be checked, morality weakened, patriotism bluntened, nor youth rendered less attractive or adventurous.

The *Sporting Times*, three years later, introduced the favourite theme—the awful menace—non-masculinity.

History informs us that the moment Capua was taken the walls of Carthage trembled, for catching the infection, her citizens became soft and effeminate, and an easy prey for assailants. Looking forward into history, with what disgust do we read of the reign of eunuchs over the Roman world . . . we shall find the powers of every dynasty marked the decline of the dynasty in which they prevailed.

So the *Sporting Times* continued:

It is the habitual contempt of death which ennobles the profession of a soldier, and one of the greatest inducements to the encouragement of our manly sports is this, that the contempt of danger which they beget. Men who voluntarily expose themselves to danger as in the 'glorious tumult of the chase' acquire a coolness when in it, if not an indifference to get out of it.

A World War I recruiting poster read: 'Join up and shoot Germans.' It was sport on a new and more exciting level.

In 1864 *Bell's Life in Victoria* told us how we acquired this splendid heritage from the Englishman.

He does not sit down to a small marble table, sip sugared water like the Frenchman, nor plant his back against the wall, and smoke innumerable cigars, like a Spaniard; nor bask dreamily in the sunshine and surrender himself to the *dolce far niente*, like a Neapolitan. He demands motion and exercise. He engages in out-of-door pastimes which sometimes necessitate scarcely less physical effort than the ordinary vocations of daily life . . . rowing, racing, cricket and campaigning.

The mateship-sporting-man idea extended even into war-time. During the dark days after Gallipoli, when the recruiting drive reached its passionate heights, the Army raised a unit called the *Sportsman's 1000*. The La Trobe Library in Melbourne still has one of the original Troedel posters used in the campaign. It shows a digger, arm up high, calling on the footballers, cricketers, athletes and such, to join him as a mate in the greatest sporting adventure of all—killing Germans.

The early interests were above all horse-racing, hunting, prize-fighting, rowing and cricket. It was not until the 1870s that Australians became skilled at team sports, and began to exert some power. It was a country divided into colonies, a famed area for remittance men, and well used to the gibe about being the land of convicts or the sons of felons.

There had been the great migrations from Ireland, Scotland, Cornwall, and many families had migrated because of poverty or political suppression of various kinds. The nation gained an uneasy, underlying feeling of inferiority from which it never recovered. Anything produced overseas, or made overseas, was automatically rated superior.

So Australia's first successes at sport had an immense significance. Not only did they have an effect in unifying the nation, of making Sydney one with Melbourne, and Brisbane and Adelaide and Perth and Hobart, but they gave the feeling that this country's climate and soil was capable of producing extraordinary human beings, superior even to those of Britain and Europe.

R. E. N. Twopeny, who in 1882 wrote a very critical book on Australia, was particularly interesting on the importance of cricket to Australia. He claimed that Australia's most sporting nation in the world and that cricket was the national passion. Every little Australian that is 'born alive' is a little cricketer, a bat, a bowler, or field. Cricket is the colonial *carrière ouverte aux talents*. As Napoleon's soldiers remembered that they carried a marshal's baton in their knapsacks, so the young Australians all remember that they have a chance of becoming successors to that illustrious band of heroes who have recently conquered the mother-country and looted her into the bargain.

Who cares about Federation? According to *Melbourne Punch*: 'He can only watch one game at a time.'

And Twopeny became fearful. He realized that Murdoch's triumphant 1882 side was picked from say, ten thousand men and boys who played the game. What would happen by 1890 when they could choose, perhaps, from a hundred thousand.

The first successes Australia ever had were in sport, with the result that sport became the god. Edward Trickett won the sculling championship of the world in 1876. The same year a combined New South Wales and Victorian XI beat an all-England XI on the Melbourne Cricket Ground. The national euphoria that accompanied these victories is described in later chapters.

The grateful populace always gave their victorious sportsmen a triumph upon their returning home. Such a triumph welcomed Murdoch's 1882 'Ashes' touring side, and this tradition has gone on until the present day. Lionel Rose, Johnny Famechon, Dawn Fraser, Betty Cuthbert, Frank Sedgman, Lew Hoad, Evonne Goolagong—they have all had their 'triumphs'.

Other nations which do this perhaps have a similar sense of national insecurity. The Pakistanis, the Indians and the West Indians are similarly aggressive. Their national satisfaction through thrashing England is immense. Their victories release all sorts of chauvinistic jealousies and tensions.

Melbourne became the sporting centre, and it is curious how time and time again, when looking at sporting mania one comes back to Melbourne. Before 1890 Melbourne was the largest and richest city, and this enabled the building of luxurious sporting centres. In 1861, upon the visit of Mr H. H. Stephenson's XI from England, Mr Stephenson remarked then that the Melbourne Cricket Ground was the best he had seen anywhere. It continued to expand and has always remained one of the best cricket grounds in the world.

Flemington, under the inspired guidance of Mr R. C. Bagot, became a beautiful racetrack and by the 1870s one of the best in the world.

Visitors who came to Australia were astonished to see racetracks like Flemington and Randwick. Anthony Trollope in 1871 said that sport in Australia was clearly a national necessity, to judge by all the cricket, athletics, rowing, hunting, flat-racing, and steeplechasing. Every town had a racecourse and there were many racecourses where there were no towns. As soon as he arrived in Melbourne it was impressed on him that he must go to Flemington.

Nat Gould in 1889 said: 'The lawn at Flemington far surpasses anything I have seen in England, Ascot and Goodwood not excepted, and it is far in advance of those aristocratic club courses, Sandown and Kempton Park.' And he made the point that colonial governors, if they wished to be popular with Australians, simply have to be good sportsmen. They still do.

The sport mania literally possessed the whole nation, and it covered a remarkable variety of activities, of which from the mid-eighties until the turn of the century cycling was the national craze on two wheels. Coursing was another craze which reached its peak in Victoria. Well-to-do families like the Austins, the Clarkes and the Chirnsides specialized in raising special quality hares, and the slaughter of kangaroos, deer and bird-life occupied every weekend.

The big successes, which began with cricket and rowing throughout the seventies, were followed by a golden era in the nineties and the Edwardian era. The tennis boom began with the Davis Cup triumph of 1908, and about the same time along came the world champion swimmers, Beaurepaire, Fanny Durack, Annette Kellerman, followed later by Boy Charlton. The cricketers continued to improve, living up to the awesome prediction of R. E. N. Twopeny.

In 1960, the American magazine *Sports Illustrated* sent a man to Australia and he wrote in the wondrous tones of one who had just discovered the place:

When you consider what Australians have managed to do in the intensely competitive field of international sport against nations with huge populations, it simply staggers your comprehension . . . To be in Australia and not to watch their top athletes perform is as unthinkable as to journey to Chartres and skip the cathedral.

It seems facile to explain all this success by the obvious fact that Australia has a lovely sunny climate. So do Spain and Italy, but these countries are not possessed by sport mania, nor do they have this aggressive desire to succeed, to beat many international triumphs. Both New South Wales and Victoria have ministries of sport. The Queensland Government has appointed a director of sport. Many promises have been made and hopefully spectatorship will decrease and general participation will increase.

Nothing ever stays static and there have been remarkable changes in Australia's sporting passion. Melbourne is still the spectator capital of Australia, just as it was a hundred years ago, and the changes there have not been quite so marked as elsewhere in the country. Melbourne still gets the biggest crowds for football, cricket, tennis, boxing, racing, almost any spectator sport. All this came sharply under notice during the New Year of 1972. The 1973 Lawn Tennis Association had to decide where to hold the national titles. The 1972 titles had just taken place in Melbourne, where they had had an audience of forty-five thousand. Over the same period there had been 98,864 over three days to watch the Rest of the World side play Australia at the Melbourne Cricket Ground. The crowd of 38,179 on the Tuesday was only eighty-two short of the total attendances at the first two international matches in Brisbane and Perth. The vote amongst L.T.A.A. delegates for Melbourne was overwhelming. The Queensland president, Ron Leahy said: 'You are assured of a crowd in Melbourne. It is the home of sport.'

Whether the crowd watchers be for cricket, tennis, football or anything else, Melbourne's neighbourly critics say the reason for this is beautifully obvious—the city is so incomparably dull there is nothing else to do. There are other reasons. The sport has always been administered with remarkable skill. The sporting venues are amongst the best in the world, the press, radio and television give sporting events as respectful a news coverage as if they were reporting the arrival of the Pope, and, most important of all, Melbourne has the best public transport system in Australia. Melbourne is not split in half by a harbour, and its Melbourne Cricket Ground is the best positioned sporting ground in the world.

One talks of a religious passion for racing and football. This is very great indeed, but it must be understood that the passion comes from a section of the community, not all. If one takes football attendance in the archdiocese of Melbourne, then relates it to population, the result is most interesting. In the 1920s few grounds had proper turnstiles and writers would give their estimate of how many spectators were present. These estimates tended to be vague, however I have collected some of them. For the first eight rounds in 1928, a year when there were only nine teams in the League, there was an average attendance of 79,250. Relate that to Melbourne's population of 852,850 and it means 12 per cent were watching football on Saturday afternoon. Good reliable figures came from 1930 on.

The average for the eighteen rounds of home games in 1930 was 93,570 and the population was 1,032,500. The percentage at the game was 9.1.

Just ponder how things were at that time. There was no television and radio was making its timid beginnings. There were no big-name commentators like Lou Richards and Harry Beitzel. And the newspaper coverage was comparatively slight. On Saturday there was half a column of football, which could be considered now an insultingly brief rundown for matches, and on the Monday there was a whole page—about ninety column inches. But from Monday to Saturday the football loving public presumably thought of other things, for the newspapers provided zero information; no stories of stormy tribunals, no tales of high prices being paid for seven-foot tall recruits and States drifting close to war over failure to grant inter-state clearances.

Ninety column inches in the 1930s existed on its own passion. It was the Monday coverage rarely goes below 440 column inches, just for a normal non-finals Monday, and this spread comes after the whole tale has been exhaustively explored on Saturday and Sunday by radio and television. Even by 7 p.m. on Saturday one would have to be sealed down a mine not to know who won the matches during the afternoon.

So football in the 1930s existed on its own passion. Enthusiasm kept cheap entertainment in the industrial suburbs through most of the 'thirties. There was a big slump during World War II, and under cries of 'Why aren't those healthy men in uniform?' the patronage fell to 3.3 per cent in 1942. That was the worst year.

After the war all was well again. In 1946 the great euphoric love was away with quite. Yet things were never quite the same again. Attendance drifted with the rise of the affluent society and by 1971–2 only 5.3 per cent of the population were going to the football every Saturday. During 1972 the average weekly parade of football devotees was 133,405. If Melbourne had maintained its percentage rate of 1946 it would have been 227,500. In terms of numbers alone the best year was 1963, when the average attendance was 152,187.

But even these figures may be misleading. One should not underestimate the extraordinary effect football does have on Melbourne. In 1971 the Gallup Poll organization did a special survey on how many people over eighteen actually 'watch' a football match every week; that is, go to the ground or look at the replay on television. They found that 360,000 people or 23 per cent of people in Melbourne and Geelong see all twenty-two matches. The breakdown in ages was interesting—16 per cent of those under thirty-five saw all matches and this rose to 29 per cent for those over thirty-five. Those figures, of course, cater for total dedication. 11.2 per cent watch one to five matches, and 56.5 per cent watch at least one. These are extraordinary figures and they are accurate surely, they could not be beaten anywhere.

So football has a huge T.V. audience and a large, but static, ground attendance which is declining in proportion to population.

Australia is following a world-wide trend; spectator sport is on the decline, with the possible exception of night activities like trotting and dog-racing, if these can be called sports. Horse-racing everywhere in Australia has lost 25 per cent of spectators since World War II. Nowadays a good average metropolitan meeting is twenty thousand. Twenty years ago a good crowd was thirty thousand.

Tennis has gone through a period of crisis. The National Championships of 1972 and 1973 saw a big revival, but White City in Sydney has often been called the White Elephant.

Cricket still has its devotees; people love to buy books about it, talk about it, read about it and look at it on television, but the crowds have dwindled. In the 1930s several thousand would turn up for a District cricket match. Now the crowd would consist entirely of girl friends, aunts and mothers. Exciting personalities will still draw the crowds. On 11 February 1961, on the Saturday of the Test Match against the West Indies there was a crowd of 90,800. For the same match at the M.C.G. for the West Indies Test of 1969 the crowd was 43,464. Of course, in 1969 there was still the promise of seeing the world's most exciting cricketer, Garfield Sobers.

The reason for the decline in spectator sport is obvious. An aerial photograph of Victoria Park, taken in the 1930s, shows a big crowd at Collingwood, the home ground of Collingwood, but there are only twenty or thirty cars parked outside, but there could not afford them. It is a different tale now—we are living in an affluent society. The young people have jobs, are well-paid, and can pick up a second-hand Holden sedan for $250. This is the answer.

The great Australian hedonism now takes different forms. We have new types of world champions, like Nat Young and Midget Farrelly. The surfie cult has its greatest power in Sydney where for forty miles there is an unbroken chain of superb surfing beaches. The Australian Surfriders Association ran a census on the number of board riders in Australia and it came up with a remarkable half to three-quarters of a million. Frank Tyson, the fast-bowler, has lamented the potential Bradmans, Hoads and Elliotts who are now 'bronzing their pots' on the beaches. But it is a cult to be reckoned with and the enthusiasts are there around the beaches summer and winter. They set out at dawn, surfboards on the roofs of their Holdens, their radios tuned in to those swinging stations which give half-hourly reports on the state of the coast.

It is not only the surfie who is carried away by the sensuous attractions of sun and water. At least 75 per cent of Australians live within fifty miles of the sea, a sea which is rarely unpleasant, rarely over-cold. In Victoria alone there are forty thousand registered power boats and the number is going up at the rate of 14 per cent a year. And when driving off on a summer holiday it often seems that every one of those forty thousand boats is on a trailer and hogging the road immediately in front.

A fascinating thing is to fly over the Hawkesbury River near Palm Beach, N.S.W., or over the coastline of Port Phillip Bay. There the parallel lines left by the water skiers go off forever into the distance. The beaches have all lost their old-time peace. When lying face down on the sand it is impossible to sleep for the eternal racket of the speedboats.

Nor is the noise of the speedboats heard only along the coastline, there are even more inland. Eildon reservoir in Victoria is used by more than three thousand boats, and those who believe that travelling by boat is a method for getting away from it all should realize that boat building in Australia has become a fifty million dollar industry.

Perhaps the yachting fever is even greater. How many yachts are out on the water in Australia during any one weekend? The number could be nigh on two hundred thousand. One of the pleasant occupations is to sit in the security of one's apartment, say high up somewhere around Point Piper, and watch the thousands of yachts manoeuvring out on Sydney Harbour, taking part in the Sydney-Hobart afternoon races. The best day is the opening of the yacht race and the sight of the big fellows setting out through the heads is one of the most majestic sights on earth.

Golf is another game which has grown with the affluent society. In the 1930s the young man in an industrial suburb had no hope of doing anything else but watch football on Saturday afternoon; now he puts his golf buggy in the back of his sports car.

One in every twenty-four Australians, man, woman, or child, plays golf at some stage of the year. There are 1,235 golf courses in the nation, with 203,940 registered male golfers and a hundred thousand women golfers, euphemistically called associates. Just in one year Australians buy four and a half million golf balls and 390,000 new golf clubs. It is simply the most golf conscious country in the world, and even despite the number of golf courses space is so desperate that summer golfers queue at 4 a.m. at the first tee on public courses, waiting for first light.

The urge to get away, the loathing for staying at home, eternally drives the Australian. He likes to camp, drives his caravan alone. At least one in ten of the population fish regularly, and according to the fishing writer, Geoff Brooke, Australians spend more on fishing than all other sports combined. They range from the devoted who catch eels in the Yarra at Melbourne, to those who go to Cairns, to catch the world's largest marlin.

Fishing, of course, is a family exercise. The crafty parent buys a speedboat with potent twin outboard motors. This is good for his guilty conscience. It means that he can spend Sunday, Monday and Tuesday satiating the enthusiasm of the young for water-skiing, then they are prepared to tolerate his absence on Saturday looking for snapper, bream, flathead or whatever. It is big business. Fly fishermen will pay up to $150 just for two pieces of split cane, and when they turn to big game fishing there is no limit. It is no trouble to pay $500 for a fancy big game fishing reel.

So while the spectator sports go into decline, the sports of the participant, whether it be sailing, flying, golfing or even archery, are booming. One of the greatest boomers for the pocket, and bone specialists, is skiing, and even though it is disastrous for the pocket, and bone specialists, it increases in popularity at the rate of 20 per cent a year. Outsiders do not normally expect to find skiers in the sunburnt country, but tourist bureaux are always anxious to quote the line that the mountains in the Australian Alps have a greater area of snow than all Switzerland. Then when Australian skiers go off to Europe they are sometimes a little eager to push back home that Australia had the world's first ski club. This was the Kiandra Ski Club, set up in 1864.

The old hands who used to go up into the mountains with pack-horses say that the sport has gone hideously soft. In the 1930s the ski season had six thousand skiers on the mountain.

The passionate skier is always a sad case. He works, or just looks wistfully out of the window during the week. He leaves his office at 5 p.m. on Friday, drives at high speed through the night, covering two hundred miles or more, to arrive up on the mountain at 1 a.m. or 2 a.m. Saturday. He skis all day Saturday and all day Sunday, indulging in 'après ski' during the night hours. After dark on Sunday he drives off home to arrive in the city at 2 a.m. on Monday, with the object of working a little and gaining enough strength to do it again next weekend.

The newest boom sport is squash, and the fascinating sight on the morning train is to see the business men carrying their brief cases and their squash rackets, looking forward to a lightning sweat during the lunch hour. The number of public squash courts has multiplied ten times in ten years. No active club would dare be without its courts. The squash court has replaced the billiard table as the prime recreational asset. Here again we have a new type of world champion. Australian men and women dominate world champion squash.

Probably nowhere else is there so much opportunity for recreational activity, whether it be running, jumping, kicking, hitting, boating, surfing or just putting a line in the water.

The curious contradiction is this, that the bronzed hero of the sunburnt country, the man with salt and sand in his hair, a tennis racket in one hand, a surfboard in the other, may not be a fine physical specimen after all. Most people associated with physical fitness believe he is not.

John Snow, the English fast-bowler who claims that Australian spectators have all the rapacity of piranha fish, says this about us: 'I think your national reputation for physical fitness is a myth. I'd say the formula man—which, of course, I'm not—I'd say most Australians tend to do more lip work than leg work.'

It is heresy in Australia not to think in terms of champion excellence, but Ellery Hamilton-Smith, who has acted as a consultant to youth organizations, city and shire councils, was brave enough to say this:

It is our emphasis on competition which enables us to produce world champions like Ron Clarke, Rod Laver and Shane Gould, and I say it is not really important that we produce such individual champions. What is extremely important is that we eliminate our present proportion of people who don't run, jump, or whatever, don't get to play tennis, cannot swim or who have no regular recreation.

Actually few surveys have been made on how much Australians run, jump, or whatever they do with their spare time. Inevitably as affluence increases, humans show a greater desire not to use their limbs. In 1948 a Gallup poll showed that three out of every ten Australians spent their weekends playing or watching sport. The proportion does not sound high, but even at that it was almost certainly above that of any other people on earth. The question was: 'Do you play any sport regularly?'—32 per cent said they did; 68 per cent said they did not.

The man who has done the most research on this subject is Dr A. W. Willee, Director of the Physical Education Department at the University of Melbourne. He started a lecture to the Eighteenth Australian Dental Congress with these words:

THE SEVEN AGES OF THE AUSTRALIAN MALE

(With apologies to Wm Shakespeare)

At first the youngster,<br>
Nourished and cared for with his mother's love,<br>
Active from dawn to dusk in thoughtless health,<br>
And then the youth—a surfie with his surfboard,<br>
Or, say, a football hero, stern in training,<br>
Eating lean steaks, eschewing wild excess,<br>
Still vigorous and strong. And then the lover,<br>
Wedded at last, who thinks his heart is only<br>
The seat of his affections. Soon he merges<br>
Into the husband, loafing around at night,<br>
Watching his action on a TV screen.<br>
And then the family man—his little woman<br>
Delights in setting lavish meals before him,<br>
And so he plays his part. The sixth age comes,<br>
When, far from lean, this sandalled swelling goon,<br>

The Y.M.C.A. in Melbourne conducted tests on businessmen and gave a pass to only one in twenty for being 'average' fit. Most of the tests have been done only on groups here and there, but all come up with the same story that as soon as they leave school Australians take little exercise. Dr Willee conducted a survey on dentists in South Australia and found that 65 per cent of them had one hour or less of vigorous activity a week and 94 per cent had too little for healthy living.

University students were little better. He sent questionnaires to students at Melbourne University and other universities around Australia and found that undergraduates were little better than dentists. Fifty per cent of students had one hour or less of vigorous physical activity a week. Only 5 per cent had more than five hours. Considering the facilities available at the universities it would have been reasonable to presume that the students would be more active than the rest of Australians.

The students' most popular activities were squash, football, tennis, swimming and basket-ball. As for the dentists their favourite exercise was golf; which Dr Willee considers a waste of time; a pleasant enough activity, but with not enough vigour involved to set the heart pumping.

He points out that in 1968, 41,312 Australians died from heart disease and for many of them it was suicide—they could have saved themselves with proper exercise.

He cites all the elaborate measures Australians take to escape proper exercise, like the latest moving pedestrian platforms at airports, or the latest domestic motor mowers, which not only do not have to be pushed, but will actually carry the driver.

His favourite story is about a Housing Commission strike. Eighteen men refused to work on a high-rise block in Fitzroy because there was no lift beyond the eighth floor. The *Herald* quoted a union official as saying: 'The long climb upstairs is affecting the health of the competent tradesmen and driving them out of the industry.'

'Affecting their health was quite correct,' said Dr Willee, 'it was saving them from a heart attack.'

Now it is useful to speculate on the future of the sporting passion. As the nation becomes older and more sophisticated, the desire to express its ego on the sporting fields should become less desperate; the need to show our physical greatness should be able to gain public approval without having to pull on football sweaters.

The nation will discover equally rewarding—touring orchestras, writers and poets, perhaps even roving scientists. Both New South Wales and Victoria have ministries of sport. The Queensland Government has appointed a director of sport. Many promises have been made and hopefully spectatorship will decrease and general participation will increase.

It is doubtful whether the gambling passion will ever decline. It is important to keep a number of sports, like horse-racing, dog-racing, football and even professional foot-running, healthy, in order to give gambling respectability. We are said to be the world's champion gamblers, $160 a head a year, compared with $90 for the Americans, $50 for New Zealanders and $30 for the British.

As an illustration of the passion this surely was the most revealing story of the day. Igloo, the racehorse of the day, was in Perth for the Cup carnival of 1972; this almost priceless creature had an injured leg and was in danger of being destroyed. A Perth nun sent holy water from the Shrine at Lourdes to rub on that injured leg.
