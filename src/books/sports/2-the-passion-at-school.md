When I was at school it was a toss-up as to who was the senior god—the captain of boats or the football captain. They wore their prized special blazer in the school colours. One had to be a regular member of a senior team to win colours, and we looked upon them in wonder like altar boys gazing at the robes of an archbishop. There were no colours to be had—and few honours—for triumphing at the examination table.

Cricket, rowing, football, athletics—these were the vital games. One could represent the school at tennis, but it was considered to be slightly effete, a game for the weak, and it wasn't worth a school blazer.

The choice was cricket or rowing—everyone had to play football and everyone had to compete on the track. There was a system of qualifications—if one ran the 100 yards, in say eleven seconds, jumped five feet, ran the mile in five minutes, did the 440 in sixty or sixty-five seconds, then there were points to be had for the House. The names of all were on the noticeboard, which listed for all to see whether one had qualified for each event. Failure not to have jumped high enough, far enough, tossed a lead ball sufficient distance, was a terrible humiliation.

Not only was one required to play sports daily, it was also compulsory to watch school teams in action and show 'school spirit' in times of sporting crisis. Even to question these ideals could be dangerous. One time with a friend I decided not to watch the annual football match against Melbourne Grammar. I had the idea that this was a perverted form of nationalism—how much better to spend the day in an educational and healthy manner, ride a bicycle into a factory ten miles off, and learn something of its manufacturing processes.

When we returned there was a team of sixty waiting to toss us into an icy pond, and swim us round and round until we learned the correct qualities of life. It is doubtful whether that fervour exists to such a degree any more. In the 1950s schools began to diversify their sports into different codes of football, lacrosse and even golf. Geelong Grammar developed its mountain retreat, Timbertop, where for a year boys played no sport at all.

Before this there was a belief that a boy at boarding school had to be kept active at all times. He must be kept healthy, his mind on wholesome things and away from the horror of homosexuality. Then there was something perhaps even worse; the awful spectre of masturbation. Mr Henry Varley, a Temperance preacher in Melbourne, produced a revealing pamphlet in 1893. He dared not use the word 'masturbation' and he called it 'personal pollution', and he wrote:

A short time since a youth wrote me saying that in a large school numbering nearly 1,000 scholars, to which he was sent, he was at once taught this hideous sin; another writing of a superior school in a principal city added, 'I do not believe a single boy who entered that school escaped'.

Mr Varley made it clear that unless one stopped this sin loss of memory and insanity could result and he advised:

Cold-water bathing, water as a beverage, simple and wholesome food, regularity of sleep, plenty of exercise, sports such as cricket, tennis, boating, football and bicycling are amongst the best preventatives against these youthful lusts.

Independent schools tended to reflect their community. There was always a suspicious belief that if a school never succeeded at football, cricket or on the river, there was something wrong. Where was the 'moral fibre', or wherever there was the 'school spirit'? It might be better for junior to go to Kings, Wesley or wherever, which had won premierships for the past three years. This could be a grave matter, for schools frequently were short of pupils. They did not have waiting lists as now, and a full roll was needed to pay the bills. So headmasters encouraged athletic boys to stay at school until they were twenty or even twenty-one. Sometimes, nefariously, they even brought in outsiders to play in important matches.

Some schools had ferocious school songs written, not around scholarship, but around a desire to slaughter the opposition. Melbourne Grammar had, and still has, a rabble-rouser sung to the tune of *The Men of Harlech*. The chorus:

<em>None our ranks shall sunder<br>
Who will shirk or blunder<br>
If all are true<br>
To our dark blue<br>
Our foeman shall go under . . .</em>

The 'under' was then repeated and shouted UNDAH, as if the rivals at Scotch, Wesley or Xavier were being ultimately crushed under foot. Sport was always this vital.

For a good example of the sporting passion it is best to turn to L. A. Adamson, headmaster of Wesley College. He had been at Rugby School in the 1870s, and he came to the post charged with all the appropriate adoration and he expected all his boys to feel the same. Of course, he had great success; the period 1902–13 is looked back upon as the golden age when Wesley won almost everything in sight.

The history of Wesley College, *The First 100 Years*, tells how the top sportsmen became the 'bloods' of the school. Adamson introduced a 'colours' system, similar to that of a university. The captains of teams had first choice of lockers, vice-captains the next choice, then boys who had won colours in three sports, two sports and so on, down the line.

The sporting champions would be lauded by name in assembly and quadrangle. Adamson loved them. The Wesley history contains even a song written about his great young men. L. A. Adamson composed a song about Harry Prout, who dominated public school football from 1903 to 1907. He called the song 'Harry Rover'.

<em>There's a chap that Wesley's known for many a day,<br>
We mostly call him 'Harry' just for short—<br>
He can run a bit and jump a longish way,<br>
But at football, he's a quint-essential sort!<br>
He's a ducky, he's a daisy, he's a trick,<br>
He's an injia-rubber idiot on the spree,<br>
It would take a double eighteen for to lick<br>
Little 'Harry' when he's going strong and free!<br>
Then there's to you 'Harry Rover'—it's your loss that we deplore,<br>
You have stayed with us your longest and you can't stay any more.<br>
Now that playing time is over and we've got to part with you,<br>
Here's a health to 'Wesley' too—and a health to 'Harry Rover'!</em>

Note the line 'you have stayed with us your longest and you can't stay any more'. This would indicate that Harry had reached twenty-one and was beyond the legal age for a schoolboy football match. In 1912 Scotch actually had won the football premiership when it was discovered they had a boy over age, so the team was disqualified.

Passions were strong in these matches and almost always there was a fight. A classic battle was the Melbourne Grammar-Wesley match of 1905 played on the St Kilda Cricket Ground. The traditional rivalry had always been between Melbourne Grammar and Scotch, but as Wesley had won so often, the battleground had shifted. The *bête noir*, the enemy, was now Wesley.

Naturally there was a great crowd of supporters from both schools. They had cheer squads, they had ribbons, flags and school banners. Everything was carefully organized. The headmasters, Mr L. A. Adamson, M.A., of Melbourne Grammar and Mr G. E. Blanch, M.A., of Wesley, called in appropriate police protection—five constables and two mounted troopers.

Then there was a careful apartheid between the two schools. Wesley was behind its goal and Melbourne Grammar behind its goal. At the end of each quarter they changed ends, moving clockwise, so that they never met. Perhaps nothing would have happened, but at half-time, the Melbourne Grammar boys went into the centre of the ground and grouped around a large dark blue banner for a photograph.

Wesley, noting that the centre of the field had been occupied by the enemy, decided to go that way also. Just as the Wesley column reached the Melbourne Grammar group, a Wesley boy reached out and tore off a Grammar cap. The *Argus* reported:

The photographic group broke up, and the battle became general. Caps were torn off, and their owners knocked down when they tried to recover them. Some boys, shouting with delight, rushed round from group to group, hitting every hostile face they saw. Others picked out foemen worthy of their steel, and in a few minutes the field was dotted all over in a series of hand-to-hand encounters. A well-known barrister's son, who made a star appearance as a boxer some time ago, was prominent in the thick of the fight—a modern Hector. As soon as the combat settled down into seriousness five constables and two mounted policemen appeared on the ground, and sought to separate the boys. Their task was hopeless and it was only the necessity of finishing the football match that stopped the battle.

During the second half the situation was reasonably calm—the battle eased to 'vigorous compliments' shouted from either side. At the end of the match both teams filed off the ground, and here again there would have been a struggle, except that there was only one exit from the ground. Melbourne Grammar moved, Wesley moved, and then they both began to sprint to get there first. And here battle was joined. The main object was to secure battle trophies—rosettes, caps and straw boaters, something to souvenir, like Romans looting Carthage. The captain of the Wesley team, Boynton, came out of the dressing-room to see what was going on, and very smartly received a crack in the eye, so he retaliated.

By this time the police were back again. They bundled him out of the ground, and pushed the difficult ones through the gate like pigs into a sausage machine. As for who won, *Punch* gave the honours to Wesley. 'The young Methodist gentlemen went home with enough caps to set them up for life in the college headgear business'.

Neither of the headmasters sounded over-distressed after the match. Mr Adamson said it was the same sort of thing that always went on at the Eton-Harrow match: 'I suppose it's the nature of boys'. But there were sour leading articles in the newspapers, and the Letters to the Editor columns were filled for days.

'Materfamilias' began to shake when she saw the 'shocking conflict', the 'ruffianly behaviour'. Why didn't the masters of both schools rush out onto the ground in the interests of humanity and the ladies amongst the spectators?

An ex-Wesley committee man said: 'As the father of four boys I have cherished the desire and hope that they should in turn pass through one of these institutions. Yesterday's record has banished this desire for ever.'

But it was interesting that for every shocked letter there was one which thought the battle was not only normal but character-building. 'Thoughtful parents and those who fear fatal injuries to their little darlings in school scraps ought to put frills on their limbs and send them to ladies' seminaries.' 'Ex-schoolboy' said: 'It will be a poor look-out for the British Empire when its younger schoolboys do not behave roughly and even rudely at times. Nations are not made and sustained by milksops.' Incidentally, if Wesley won the battle, Melbourne Grammar actually won the football.

But football over the years was insignificant compared with rowing, and the story was the same both in Sydney and Melbourne. I remember the Head of the River on the Yarra, back in the 'thirties, as one of the great social events of the year. It was as good as the Grand Final and almost as good as the Melbourne Cup. Rowing writers had all the verbosity of Neville Cardus, and they would discuss prospects, form, qualities of individual crew members, for weeks beforehand, until one knew all the rowers like footballers. Just take these lines from the *Argus* rowing writer of 1926:

The title of 'Head of the River' is more than a name; it means the glorification of young athleticism, and while the fact that a boy is in the crew is enough to single him out for special notice, the possibility of him being one of the victors is something to quicken the pulse of all connected with him.

Quicken the pulse—there was no question about that. The crew member inevitably was on a special diet of steak, eggs and milk, while the rest of the hungry boarding-school looked on enviously.

The event was covered, complete with sepia pictures, in society journals like *Table Talk*. The newspapers would list all the important people there—Governor, Chief Justice, Premier and all the great. It was important to form. Both boys and girls went in school uniform. It was important to have thick dark stockings and a friend from a girls' school. She wore three feet of ribbon. If one's parents had large property on the Riverina, then the young man took his lady to lunch at Simport's, the old basement café on the Block. Otherwise she was given a milk and ice-cream parfait. If she was starving she received a banana split with banana, chocolate, ice-cream, nuts, whipped cream—'the works'.

It was important to wear military cadet boots, because there was much jumping to be done. Then, as soon as the race started, there was a roar which did not stop until the crews passed the judge's box. Then if your crew won it was *de rigueur* to rush to the boatshed and exult. One shouted: 'We want crew! We want crew!' Then as they came in everyone chanted: 'Well rowed bow, well rowed two, well rowed three, well rowed four . . .' Finally the joyous schoolboys picked up the cox and threw him in the river.

Coxes do not get ducked any more. After that there would be marches through the city. That is considered far too dangerous.

The Geelong crews would come down on special trains, all decorated. The school crests would be on the front of the engines. Then there would be special theatre nights. The whole school would go to the theatre, and the crew, those men of almost unapproachable distinction, would sit together in the place of honour in the dress circle.

The first year that there were really extensive complaints about all this was 1923. The crowd in 1904 was seven thousand and now it had risen to sixty thousand. As mentioned earlier, the only event in Australia that could do better was the Melbourne Cup. Melbourne Grammar won the River and the *Argus* devoted several pages to the event.

Mr Stanley Bruce, the Prime Minister, entertained the winning crew to dinner at his house in Walsh Street, South Yarra. Then he took them on to the great triumph at Her Majesty's Theatre. Just as every other year the whole theatre was decorated with River motifs and topical jokes. As soon as the official party entered there was a wild cry, 'Three cheers for the crew!'

In letters to the newspapers there were complaints about the manner in which the boys were being turned into heroes. You would see details of their lives on slides at picture theatres and they were even on hoardings at railway stations, complete with pictures of each crew member plus potted biographies. Mr Adamson of Wesley said only the coxes missed out on a biography. Presumably they were too young to have a past. One father, who appealed for a calming-down, said: 'After all, I have to live with these youths, and I have to buy their hats'; suggesting he couldn't get them on their heads any more. Another parent, complaining about the portraits at the railway stations, said: 'Had they been star actors or prominent politicians, more flattering publicity could not have been bestowed on these youths'.

Even Mr Adamson complained. He said: 'It cannot be good for boys of seventeen or eighteen, most of them quite ordinary boys, to have so much spot-light thrown on them, nor is it good for the schools, as faithful members of other teams grow restive.' He also complained of the behaviour of the young old boys. They went on the rampage in Collins Street and their behaviour was deplorable.

Sydney's year of reckoning was 1934, when Sydney Grammar won the Head of the River. It was a windy, difficult day and the crowds on the banks and on the Parramatta were huge. The top deck of a ferry launch collapsed near the finishing post and sixty people were thrown into the river. Many were trapped under the woodwork and thirty-eight had to be taken to hospital suffering from fractured limbs and shock. A high school four collided with a small craft and sank to the gunwales. A launch capsized taking with it a dinghy, which it had been towing. A woman in the dinghy was trapped underneath, and she was very lucky to escape death. The *Sydney Morning Herald* complained about all this and about the crowds.

The G.P.S. regatta is not free from danger of sensational publicity, which leads to a loss of proportion. In recent years the tremendous public interest it has aroused has not been entirely welcomed by the schools themselves . . . the headmasters think that school sport is becoming too much of a public stunt.

One of those who suggested that it was too much of a public stunt was Mr L. C. Robson, headmaster of Sydney Grammar. He said the regatta should be rowed only on a week day, and that this would get rid of all the unwanted spectators.

The unwanted spectators continued to increase. Some post-war crowds on the Yarra reached a reported ninety thousand people, and in 1946 the Headmasters' Association issued a statement deploring the boat-race glamour. Corrie Gardner, secretary of the association, said: 'Members of the association do not believe in boys being paraded and made into little tin gods'.

The race could not be held on the Swan Street Bridge for long, and a de-escalation programme was drawn up so that it would not become a convenient excuse for trouble; it has been held on the Yarra at Geelong ever since. Some schools voted for a change with one exception. There were brawls, flour bombs, tomatoes, eggs and even a refinement, the chocolate flour bomb mixed with cocoa. A Wesley old boy was hit over the head with a baseball bat and taken to hospital with a suspected fractured skull, so it was back to the Barwon in 1958. Meanwhile the Sydney Regatta switched from the Parramatta to the Nepean and boat races have never been the same since.

The Headmasters' Association's de-escalation programme extended to all sports, and in 1958 competition in Melbourne brought in six more schools, thus widening the competition and making some of the old rivalries a little less deadly. Now there were so many more sides to hate.

Even so, competition in sport is still a matter of immense interest to boys and their parents. I. V. Hansen, in his study of independent schools, wrote:

The headmaster of a well-known Sydney independent school once told me that the school's fund-raising tour among grazier old boys of western New South Wales would have been more successful if only the school had not lost an important rugby match that year.

He also gives an intriguing account of a headmasters' conference in a year when L. A. Adamson (now Sir James) was close to retirement, and J. R. Darling was a brash young headmaster. Adamson, as the high priest of manliness and adulation of the sporting hero, was arguing that school sporting results held together generations of old boys. Darling disagreed. 'But Darling,' scowled Adamson, 'it's the only thing that marks us from the state schools.' 'Oh, come, come,' said another headmaster, 'not the only thing, I hope.'

Sport at state schools has never been waged with the same passion as at the independent or church schools, because they have rarely had the facilities or the money. One has only to look at the prison-like asphalt squares of some of the metropolitan schools to see why. The good high school footballer, athlete or cricketer often performs for a public team.

So the amount of sport played at state schools varies from none at all to a great deal, some of the top high schools playing sport in the old muscle-mania tradition of the public schools.

The mania is widespread, as John McLaren points out in his book *Our Troubled Schools*. He describes how the gods of the school walk mightily in their athletic renown, and the students are driven weekly to 'make obeisance to the holy shrine'. Nor, he says, is the teacher unaffected by this frenzied cult.

Even in the state schools he will have to do his share of coaching teams, running a house and keeping the records. If he wishes to obtain money from the parents to supply books for the library or a projector for the geography room he is wise to first show enthusiasm for the completion of the school oval or the provision of a new tennis court. Few people realize just how much time is occupied by sporting activities. In a country high school which has to travel to play its matches this is unlikely to be less than ten days, or two teaching weeks, in a school year which is seldom as much as thirty weeks in effective length.

McLaren is brave enough to question the value of any school sport. At one time there were few facilities for public recreation, and the schools had to provide them. Now, if anything, there is an excess of facilities for public play. Let the students use these and the schools get on with the job of teaching.

In 1971 the New South Wales Education Department declared that it would drop compulsory sport from the beginning of the 1972 academic year. The New South Wales Director-General of Education, Mr D. J. A. Verco, said the move was unavoidable because of the expected shortage of secondary teachers. The announcement sent shock waves of horror around the country. 'It's going to be hard to get the rest of the world to believe,' said the *Australian*. 'That nation of sunburnt sportsmen can never look quite the same again.' There were stirring letters, including a plaintive cry from students at Murwillumbah that Australia's greatness, her sporting champions, all had their start on school playing fields. What would happen now?

But not all were so sure. Many debated the other way, asking what school sport had ever achieved, and saying that Dr Arnold's old talk about sport being character-building belonged to the nineteenth century; no evidence existed for it, and very likely the reverse was true. The way some of our teams behaved on the field was hardly conducive to good character.

Some were even tempted to recall the comments of Dr D. J. Drinkwater, senior lecturer in education at Queensland University, who said there was no evidence to support the theory that sport developed character. If one took the various sports then one could conclude that:

In Rugby or any other form of football, there is no question of all players getting together and having a jolly co-operative game of catching-in-a-circle, so that all get an equal share of whatever is of value about the ball. From Rugby one could then say the players learn selfishness and non-co-operation.

In cricket, also, one might say that players learn loitering, indolence and the irrevocable effect of a single mistake. Athletics—you learn there is only who is the best, and if the best is not you then you are some variant of a failure.

Of course, he could have enlarged on this. One of the primary skills of cricket is gamesmanship, and one of the first lessons of football is how to circumvent the rules. Dr A. W. Willee, in his *World Manifesto on Physical Education*, said:

It has been my 'pleasure' to sit next to a priest at a football match and hear him urge a player to kill another. Fortunately, the player could not hear the cries of the pugnacious priest, and when I queried his behaviour in relation to his Christian beliefs he was almost able to convince me that this was militant muscular Christianity. Student teachers have told me it is necessary to teach children to foul so they can protect themselves.

However, the pressure of the sport-lovers was too much, and compulsory sport did not disappear. New South Wales found that recruitment of teachers improved, and in 1972 it continued with the old system—three sports periods a week for high schools—all on the one afternoon. For primary schools the periods could be spread during the week.

Even then compulsory sport is not necessarily the answer. According to Ron Clarke, sport tends to be for the élite, and the sport system is actually governed by inter-school competitive sport, so that only 10 to 15 per cent of students actually take part. The other 90 per cent are inadequately supervised and receive little encouragement to play.

Again, surveys are few as to what sports Australians play and how many play them. However 1972 did see the publication of a remarkably thorough survey on the fitness of children of Government schools. It was a national survey conducted by Dr A. W. Willee of Melbourne University. He had the co-operation of the National Fitness Council, all the state Education Departments and the A.M.P. Society. They asked 7,439 school children, aged from thirteen to seventeen, to do a series of physical tests, which included sit-ups, throwing a softball, pull-ups on a horizontal bar, a run/walk of 600 yards, a standing broad jump and a 50-yard dash.

Information came out too about the schools themselves; for example, only one in four had a gymnasium and covered playing area. But the prime lesson was that Australian children, anyway, were disappointingly unfit. Dr Willee compared the Australian survey with a similar survey done in the United States in 1964. There the American scores for boys were better in pull-ups, sit-ups, sprinting, agility in standing broad jumps, softball-throw and endurance. The so-called 'soft' American was far ahead of the so-called 'tough' Australian.

As for girls, the Australian was superior only in the 600-yard run/walk. There were other remarkable features of the survey. The mean score for girls at seventeen showed a deterioration from thirteen years to seventeen; that is, the girls were fitter and stronger at thirteen than at seventeen. The boys did improve, except in performing sit-ups: the boys of thirteen were stronger in the abdomen than the seventeen-year-olds.

But the shocking results for the girls are interesting. It would indicate that sport is predominantly a male concern, that the adulation is directed towards males. Indeed the history of sport in Australia until recent times has been a male chauvinist affair. In most co-educational institutions male sports are the ones that predominate, receive all the cheers, and usually most of the finance. There are a number of spectacular exceptions to the male sporting dominance—female swimming champions have often been more successful than male. Female track and tennis stars have also shown their power in recent years. Their success has even been a matter of wonder, for the males have had the greater opportunities.

From these figures it would appear that those who can't play separate themselves very early. As with adults, the community is divided between the elite who play sport at all levels, and the rest who watch. Dr Willee's survey covered students outside rugby, athletics, swimming, cricket, or tennis, and found there were many opportunities for those who wanted them.

Here the interesting subject for study is not the child, but the ambitious parent who seeks identification with a future 'hero' in the family. One sees these parents at all the 'little sport' activities. The stop-watch parent, of course, is famous. This is the mother or the father who gets to the pool-side, stop-watch in hand, taking notes by the hour, as junior goes up and down, up and down, requiring the dedication of a religious order; self-control also, when the little blonde from next door shows obviously greater muscular power and endurance.

In New South Wales there are fifty-five thousand soccer players under twelve. Rugby Union has a thousand non-school league teams and there are fifty thousand registered junior league players under twelve.

Those who have watched the parents at junior league matches claim the passion of the cheering and even the language used could not be beaten, even by the coaches at senior league level. Marion McDonald in the *Bulletin* told what it was like in Centennial Park, Sydney, on a Saturday morning: she saw a young man with a battered face bent over a midget football team, giving a pep talk: 'I've got a dummy here for anyone that wants to cry and muck around. And you know what will happen if you run on the field with a dummy in your mouth; they'll laugh at you.'

The tots were dressed in perfect football gear and as they swarmed around the ball like bees the distracted parents shouted: 'Don't just run beside him, Brian, TACKLE! TACKLE! . . . Get 'im by the car and haul 'im down.'

In Victoria the infant brigade has a little league of under-elevens sponsored by the State Savings Bank, proudly known as the S.S.Bs. Every V.F.L. team has its own mini-Magpies, mini-Bulldogs or mini-Demons, mini-Swans. They play at half-time before crowds of anything up to a hundred thousand, and as the year progresses, the tension mounts with the final prospects becoming more and more desperate, so it does with the S.S.Bs. The spectators are there to shout, floggers and all, for here are the future champions.

For athletics there is the Little Athletics movement, which has more than a hundred thousand members in Australia and is escalating at a remarkable rate. Forty-five thousand are in Victoria alone. The events are superbly organized, with more skill and devotion than is given to the adult sport.

Undoubtedly here is a sport which does a great deal for general physical fitness, but the finals can be awe-inspiring, even frightening. In Melbourne they take place in mid-March and it is something to see more than two thousand mini-athletes, all under twelve, male and female, competing on the ground. The events require four hundred officials, who conduct matters with ruthless rules and timing. Here again it is important to watch the parents. I have watched scenes of excitement at the finish of a Melbourne Cup, but spectators at Flemington are the embodiment of serene calm compared with what takes place at Little Athletics events. Mothers scream, pull their husband's hair, throw their arms in anguish or joy to the heavens. Indeed there is no greater joy than seeing a beloved little one spread-eagling a large field, crushing the less gifted utterly, and coming home in front.

Then there is the grand moment. It is just like the Olympic Games. There is a proper stand or podium—number one is high up in the centre—two and three on either side. A bearer brings out the gold, silver and bronze medals on a blue velvet cushion, then the chairman of the Little Athletics puts the medals on a ribbon around the necks of the lucky winners. Only the band playing the national anthem is missing.

The range of events varies from the under-ten shot-put for girls, to the 400 metres and 1500 metres for under-twelves. In 1971, for example, there were many collapses and one girl spent forty-five minutes on a stretcher while first aid was applied.

The Little Athletics movement rouses an annual controversy on how dangerous it is to push small children into intense competition. Successes and failures for a ten-year-old can reach a level at which they become agonizingly hard to bear. Dr Henry Pang of Canberra has been doing some research on this. He made studies of children at the Pearce Sacred Heart Primary School. In August 1972 he recommended to the school that it phase out inter-school games for under-elevens and concentrate on a physical fitness programme instead. Dr Pang said the children could suffer not only physical injury but from the pressures of competition, at an age when they were too young to cope.

He said he first became aware of this problem when coaching the school's under-eleven Rugby Union team.

Towards the end of the term I noticed something was wrong. For a start only about two of the boys seemed to be doing any work—scoring the tries and so on—and the others were doing nothing. But there was a constant pressure on them—from the parents as much as anything—to do better than the other boys. I came to the conclusion that they were being forced to compete at far too early an age and fear that this could cause serious trouble in later life.

Yes, the will to conquer all, is forced into the young Australian at an unbelievably early age; to lose is the greatest crime of all. Just to prove this point let us conclude here with three remarkable news stories.

RUGBY TOTS BRIBED SAYS NUN

Sydney.—A leading Roman Catholic school has withdrawn its under-six team from the junior rugby league because of 'bribes' by parents.

The school is the Holy Cross Primary School in Woollahra, a wealthy eastern suburb.

The school principal, Sister Mary Julian, told parents in a statement yesterday: 'A dollar for a tackle or a try, and more after the game, is common. It is a sick atmosphere and it is not to be encouraged.'

Holy Cross plays in the Eastern Suburbs Rugby League competition which is held on Saturday mornings.

DAD THUMPS THE UMP

*The Herald*, 1 March 1973

Sydney.—The father of a nine-year-old footballer was fined $60 in Bankstown Court today for assaulting a junior football referee.

He is Charles B., 28, of Panania, an outer Sydney suburb. Mr K. Hammond, S.M., was told B. had been summonsed for unlawfully assaulting the referee, Terry Marshall, 21, on July 29. B. pleaded guilty.

Mr J. Pollard, for Marshall, said that on July 29 Marshall was refereeing an 'E' grade rugby league match between nine-year-olds at Smith Park, East Hills.

He said after the match B. followed the referee into the dressing room where he made some comments to the referee and later pulled him from the bench by the legs, Mr Pollard said.

He said when Marshall fell to the concrete floor his buttocks and an elbow were injured.

B. was then asked to leave, which he did.

Mr Pollard said Marshall was off work for two weeks and had to have medical treatment.

Mr A. Weaver for B. told the court B. felt very deeply about the incident and later attempted to apologize to Marshall. He said B. had not been to a junior football match since. B. was ordered to pay $46 compensation and $66 costs.

BARRED!—AND HE'S ONLY EIGHT

*The Herald*, 18 October 1972

Sydney.—Martin Fletcher, 8, loves rugby so much he takes his football to bed with him every night. But a League judiciary committee has banned him from playing Junior Rugby League.

The ban follows a tripping incident in an under-12s game between Cromer Youth Club and a Forestville side. Martin, playing hooker, was sent off and told to appear before the seven-man judiciary on Monday.

'Too silly for words,' Mrs Frances Fletcher, Martin's mother, said. 'We flatly refuse to let him appear before such a trifling thing. It would be a terrible experience for an eight-year-old boy.'

Sequel: Martin, banned for life from the Junior Rugby League, received an immediate offer to play from a Collingwood fan over Australian Rules.
