Has the horse taken over the role of god of the Australians? The Catholic writer John Francis Hogan thought so. He pointed out that other nations had great national festivals to commemorate a famous deed in history. Perhaps they did honour to some departed hero, perhaps they celebrated a noble victory in the battle for freedom or rejoiced anew at the overthrow of a hated despot. Australians were different. Their excuse for a national festival was the worship of the horse. The first week in November was devoted entirely to 'that interesting quadruped'. Hogan said that as we had decided to adopt the horse as our god we could hardly afford to belittle our 'dusky brethren in less civilized lands' who selected their gods from the animal kingdom.

Australians have not always been monotheists. We have had other idols, the football, the cricket ball, the surfboard and even the tennis racket, but our earliest god and the one that calls for the greatest reverence is the horse.

The size, variety, and spread of the equine worshipping congregations would give immense satisfaction to any Bishop of the Equine Church. Even though our population is only thirteen million we have more race-meetings and more racecourses than any other people on earth. Go to the United States, Britain, France, Asia, and there are perhaps one or two big racecourses near the main cities, but out in the provinces often there are none at all. Yet in Australia every provincial city, indeed any town of size has its own racetrack. Even the little bush meetings are conducted with extraordinary skill and efficiency. When exploring the history of small communities it is interesting to trace which came first—the hotel, the church or the racetrack. Usually the church came third, even more loved in the outback. It is eerie to fly by light aircraft over the Gulf Country, Western Queensland or even the Centre. In these parts, where often there is no evidence of civilization beyond a station homestead, one will spot the oval track standing out clearly from scrubland and gibber desert. It looks so strange standing there in all the loneliness; a visiting anthropologist or scientist might suggest it is a strange mark made by a meteorite, or a corroboree ground for aboriginal tribes.

Not so, the corroboree is for the local annual race-meeting. The situation there is the same as in Melbourne; it is the greatest social cultural event of the year. Except, of course, for a little town like Birdsville out on the 'corner' where the state lines of New South Wales, Queensland and South Australia meet, where it is almost the only cultural event. At the Melbourne Cup the champagne is served politely from the backs of Rolls-Royces and Mercedes in the members' car park. At Birdsville there is nothing of the gentility of glasses; the beer is drunk straight from the stubbies. A lonely publican needs a real thirst after a lonely twelve months in the dry inland, so a man gets stuck straight into it, and the three days of an annual bush race-meeting has a frenzied, never-go-to-bed quality about it.

Australia is now the most urbanized of countries, but it was not always so. The pioneers who pushed out from the cities depended totally on their horses. These horses were often a strange, mixed brew, but hard use sorted out the weaklings. Maurice Cavanough, the racing historian, writes that as the nineteenth century progressed Australia produced a distinct type of horse known as 'the waler'. Conditions were so hard that the waler had to be hard also, capable of carrying big loads and covering long distances in the heat with much courage. Cavanough says that not only did this horse take the explorers further and further into the undiscovered hinterland, but remained to prove the ideal stockhorse for the pastoralists who followed in the wake of the explorers.

The poets, the bush balladists, all honoured the horse and there was an affection for the animal we can hardly begin to understand in an era where the suburban gentleman goes down on his knees in daily devotion to another of his neighbours, the motor-car. The horse was everything both for transport and for recreation. The early settlers soon began to match their favourite horses against those of their neighbours. From these simple contests came the first organized race-meetings.

Hawkesbury River settlers had a racetrack in 1809, and there is even a legend about a goanna which outran a so-called racehorse when it was being exercised near Windsor in 1805. But the first official race-meeting was held on 15, 17 and 19 October, in Hyde Park, Sydney, by officers of the 73rd Regiment. Captain Ritchie's grey gelding Chase won the top prize, a plate worth £50.

As a good racing town, Melbourne was even more enthusiastic. It waited for only three years after the founding of the colony to hold its first race-meeting, on 6 March 1838. Garryowen tells us that the event took place on Batman's Hill, where Spencer Street station now stands, and the grandstand was a couple of bullock drays lashed together.

But the prizes were quite handsome for an opening meeting—the Town Plates, twenty-five sovereigns; the Ladies' Purse, twenty sovereigns; and the Hunter Stakes, fifteen sovereigns. The course was over two miles and the settlers came in from all around for the big event. Two publicans, Messrs J. P. Fawkner and Michael Carr, set up refreshment booths and you could get Jamaica Rum, Cognac, Bass, or their inferior counterfeits, for a shilling a tot, though the drink most in demand was 'a spider', a mixture of brandy and ginger beer for fifteen pence.

But the most curious event of the day was the grinning match. Garryowen reports that this was an obsolete amusement derived from eighteenth-century England. In a grinning match a contestant has to perform with his face through a horse collar. The crowd contributed forty shillings for a prize and this attracted four or five of the ugliest men in the small community. It appears to have been a purely male sport. The easy winner was a fifty-year-old carpenter named Thomas Curnew.

The bones, muscles, sinews and tissues of Curnew's head seemed as if composed of whalebone and India-rubber. At one time his tongue looked as if jumping out of his mouth, his lips and palate would be drawn in as if about to be swallowed, whilst the chin and forehead approached as if to meet.

Mr Curnew received immense applause, whereupon he immediately set about converting his forty shillings win into fire water. After the second and final day of the meeting there was a great celebratory dinner at Mr Fawkner's hotel, where the horse-lovers cleaned out his entire stock of liquor, plus his 'limited stock of bad champagne'.

The only unpleasant incident during the three days was the detecting of a pickpocket, so an interesting tradition began at Victoria's very first meeting.

The real excitement was caused by individual clashes between champion horses. The excitement was improved if the clash was against a horse from a totally unloved neighbouring colony.

The first of the great Victorian champions was Petrel, a horse of very doubtful origins. Garryowen says his sire could have been a Sydney racehorse named Steeltrap, but he is not even sure about that. In 1841, a man travelling overland from Sydney to Adelaide with two fine mares stayed briefly at a property in the Grampians owned by a man named Riley. The mares were supposed to have been stolen and both had gone missing by the time the man moved on. Such a background was most regrettable. Hero-worship was banished and horse-worship had taken its place. The name of Petrel was a household word. Everyone was asking who was Petrel? Where did he come from? Who was his sire?

The big Melbourne meeting was to begin on 24 March 1846. For weeks beforehand the Port Phillipians talked of nothing else. At first the champion of Van Diemen's Land, Pagini, was to come for the clash, but just before the race he was switched for another, Paul Jones.

Petrel, a dark chestnut, sixteen hands one inch, with a fine head but a little over-powerful in appearance, was the favourite. There were three heats for the Town Plate, each over two miles plus 'a distance'. Both Petrel and Paul Jones took part in all three heats. That was the custom at the time and racegoers thought it quite normal to run a horse in three mighty heats all in the one afternoon, then race it again the next day and the next.

A bush jockey named Muff rode Petrel in the first heat. Muff by name and Muff by nature, according to the news reports. Even though Muff flogged Petrel unmercifully, Paul Jones still managed to win by a head.

Van Diemonians in the audience went beserk. Garryowen wrote: 'They ranted and roared, and jumped and swore, and one fellow in his delirium made for the river, in which he leaped, with some difficulty saved from drowning.'

There was a time lapse of just fifty-five minutes between heats for the Town Plate. Petrel's owner, James Austin, sacked Muff on the spot and replaced him with another jockey known as 'Sandy the Butcher'. It was a long fifty-five minutes, for excitement on the track was at 'white heat'. The citizens of Van Diemen's Land had few doubts, they thought it all over. Melbournians just wouldn't believe Petrel could be beaten. There was an excellent start and Paul Jones immediately went to the front, holding the lead for all of the first mile, but Petrel then steadily overhauled him, showing much more stamina, and went away to win by four lengths.

In the third heat Paul Jones again had an early lead, but this time Petrel's win was even easier. Paul Jones made no show at all. Petrel had a long and distinguished career. He raced until he was fourteen and lived until he was twenty-five. Unquestionably they bred tough horses in those times.

The classic of all challenger races took place in 1857. Victoria had been a colony in her own right only seven years and there was a great yearning to prove her superior to mother New South Wales in all forms of enterprise, particularly sport. The Victorian champion, the winner of the Australia sweepstake, was a mare called Alice Hawthorne. Alice, like Petrel, had been bred by the rich Western District squatter Andrew Chirnside. She was by an imported Arab mare, was eight years old, having been sired by an imported horse.

She did not race until comparatively late in her career. Her status fell so low that Chirnside gave her to an aboriginal boy, who used to carry rations from one station to another. Eventually, reported *Bell's Life*, 'even the darky refused to honour her back with his presence.'

One day Mr Chirnside was anxious to try out a very good mare, Miss Campbell. For the training run he needed some competition and did not think Alice good enough to gallop with Miss Campbell, let alone pace her over one and a quarter miles. 'To the surprise, and at the time disgust, of Mr Chirnside' Alice beat Miss Campbell by twenty lengths. Yet he was shrewd enough to readjust his thinking very quickly. Alice began her career as a five-year-old and won four races at her first meeting. She then proceeded to beat all the best horses in the colony.

Consequently, by 1857, Andrew Chirnside had become more ambitious. The only way to test the quality of local horses was to spread one's ambitions over a broader canvas. The Sydney champion was Veno, reportedly a fine, slashing horse with a great raking stride, a good heart coupled with high speed and strong endurance. He was a rich, gold chestnut, an eight-year-old, and he had won twenty-eight races.

Chirnside and a group of backers sent this challenge to George T. Rowe, owner of Veno:

Victoria is open to run a horse bred in that colony, three miles, weight for age, against any horse bred in New South Wales, for 1,000 sovereigns a side, the race to be run over the Melbourne or Camden course, a toss to be made for the choice . . . The loser of the toss to receive £100 expenses.

There was no overland telegraph between Melbourne and Sydney, no fast mail service, so it was four weeks before the Alice Hawthorne party even received a reply. George T. Rowe did agree to a match in Melbourne, and after some haggling they settled his expenses at £250.

This was the biggest event in the history of the turf in Australia, the biggest thing in sport of any kind, and little else was talked about in Melbourne or Sydney.

The race was set for 3 October and Veno travelled by the steamer *London* from Sydney in the care of Johnny Higgerson, his trainer-jockey. According to one report, a large and enthusiastic crowd gathered at the wharf to say 'goodbye' to the New South Wales champion. Johnny Higgerson was a family man and he was determined that his wife and children should not starve while he was gone. He converted three or four fat pigs into bacon and pork and stored loads of other edibles. Veno arrived on 17 July and went to the private stables of Mr E. Row at South Yarra.

Meanwhile Alice Hawthorne was put in the charge of trainers Antony Green and George Watson. They were so terrified that the mare would be nobbled that they built a special box in the middle of their yard. They hired police to watch the box by day and slept there themselves by night.

The Alice Hawthorne group took great care to use their influence through the Melbourne Jockey Club to stop Veno training at Flemington.

George T. Rowe wrote to the Melbourne Jockey Club pointing out that he had put up with every disadvantage—change of water, different climate—so would there be any objection to giving Veno a few gallops on the course? 'Will my friends believe it?' he wrote. 'The request was refused by a large majority and Higgerson threatened with prosecution if he attempted to go on the course.' *Bell's Life* described the refusal as both impertinent and unsportsmanlike.

Excitement built up and up. Visitors flowed into Melbourne from Adelaide, Hobart and particularly Sydney. Price of a passage from Sydney was £16 return by boat. In the midst of all this, despite the careful provisions made for his family, Johnny Higgerson's sixteen-year-old daughter had been kicked by a cow and she was dead. Mr Higgerson, however, did not allow grief to interfere with his important preparations.

*Bell's Life*, the sporting paper, devoted column after column to statistics and predictions. The writer 'Observer' pointed out that the quality of racing was better in Victoria than in Sydney, Alice had beaten many better horses than Veno. It was her favourite distance and whether the ground was light or heavy and regardless of weather, ALICE HAWTHORNE WILL WIN, said he. 'Melville' backed up 'Observer', but 'Nestor' was of a different opinion. He had no doubts that Veno would be the winner: 'Veno's stride is long, is grasping, and he goes through dirt as a feather on a puddle'.

On race day all the newspapers ran stirring editorials. The *Herald* was particularly eloquent:

This is not a day on which politics have much chance of being attended to, in a community of Englishmen. True to their instincts they have brought their love of horses and racing to the antipodes, and—what is equally satisfactory—they have imported the noble animal himself. The passion for horses may be ridiculed, by persons of narrow minds and sedentary lives; but the feeling has ever been characteristic of the most intellectual and powerful races of mankind, and the highest order of literature and art has been inspired by the contemplation of this useful and admirable gift of the Creator.

The *Herald* made it clear that rightly all normal activities had halted in Melbourne, and that this was the greatest event in the history of the colony.

That morning at least half Melbourne was on the road to Flemington and the newspapers gave various crowd estimates from sixteen to twenty thousand. Mr Rowe was remarkably determined. Not only did he wage heavily on Veno, but he accepted another race for his horse to take place two hours after the contest with Alice Hawthorne. He bet £500 to £300 that Veno could beat Tromp, the champion of Geelong. The odds on the day were 5/4 on for Veno to beat Alice Hawthorne and just before the race they rose to 6/4 on.

The Melbourne camp could not decide on a jockey, and right until the last moment jockeys Steven Mahon and Bob Mitchell did not know who would be chosen. Finally the ride went to Mahon. Alice Hawthorne was the first into the saddling paddock. Veno was saddled away from the crowd. He did not come on to the course until just before the start at one o'clock. *Bell's Life* reported that thousands of hearts palpitated audibly within thousands of waistcoats as the starter dropped the flag and the two horses bounded away.

Veno started at a great pace and led to the first turn, and Higgerson, realizing he had the faster sprinter, pulled his horse back and Alice went to the lead for a quarter of a mile. Before they passed the stand for the first time Veno was in front again. It was obvious that Alice was overmatched and outpaced and Veno was never extended.

George T. Rowe went crazy with excitement. As Veno galloped past the post he threw his hat in the air, gave a hilarious jump, then fainted. Many thought he had died of a heart attack, but he recovered in time for the next race against Van Tromp. Veno won by two lengths. That was hardly more difficult than the first race and 6 minutes 12 seconds for the first race and 6 minutes 5 seconds for the second.

There was much grief in the editorials. *Bell's Life* called it 'a humiliation' and 'a disgrace'. Victoria had proved superior to New South Wales in every form of human enterprise, so we must not rest until 'we gain for ourselves that supremacy on the race ground and the cricket field'.

Of course, the classic example of Australia's sporting passion is the Melbourne Cup. No sporting event anywhere in the world so completely consumes an entire nation. In the classic days of the starting-price bookmakers back in the 1930s it was said that it wouldn't matter whether you were on top of Mt Kosciusko or five hundred feet down a mine, you'd still be able to place your bet on the Cup. Even if you were wandering lost way out in the great Simpson's Desert you could still get yourself fixed.

At 2.40 p.m. on the first Tuesday in November the whole nation stops dead—courts, parliament, stock exchange, everything. In a city like Brisbane, a thousand miles from where the Cup is run, it is eerie. At Cup time the main streets are deserted and the atmosphere is death-like, like some wretched place waiting for an atomic attack. But wander down, say, Queen Street, and it is possible in business premise after premise to hear the sound of radios, all tuned in to the nation's most vital piece of business.

Only rarely has anyone protested. Mr Paul Hasluck, the Federal Member for Curtin, was brave enough to do so on Cup Day, 7 November 1950. At 11.8 p.m. he complained to the House that both the Senate and the House of Representatives had delayed their normal starting times so that members could hear the race on the radio. He said:

When we reflect on what happened in the National Parliament, formally demonstrated and declared to the people that anything we might have done between 2.30 and 3.15 p.m. was far less important than finding out whether one horse could get its nose in front of another horse in a certain race.

The Leader of the Opposition, Mr Chifley, said that he had not objected to the adjournment; after all, the highest courts in the land stopped for it. The Prime Minister, Mr Menzies, also had a sense for the religious feelings of the people. He said:

As the most notorious non-racegoer of the House, I accept full responsibility . . . I am perfectly certain that none of the four million people who have listened in to the broadcast of the race this afternoon will have any bitter complaint to make about the fact that honourable members also listened in to it, although, no doubt, about three and a half million of them will have bitter complaints to make on an entirely different ground.

Mr Hasluck eventually got things in correct perspective. Nineteen years later the same gentleman, now Sir Paul Hasluck, was Governor-General of Australia, and on 4 November 1969, he found that the most vital thing for him to do was to be present at the Melbourne Cup and present the trophy. What's more he performed the same duty in 1970, 1971 and 1972, announcing on each occasion that it was a privilege for him to do so.

The State Government of Victoria proclaims a holiday for the Melbourne Cup. If horse-racing is actually a religion then no doubt it is proper that the day should be declared a holy day, but surely Melbourne is the only city in the world that has a state holiday for a horse-race.

True enthusiasts can chant all the Melbourne Cup winners right back to 1861 in the way a schoolboy might chant *Paradise Lost*. It has always been customary to refer to years by the name of the horse that won the Cup, rather than by its actual date.

For example, you would say the Second World War started in Rivette's year and finished in Rainbird's year. Maurice Cavanough, giving the toast to Racing at the Carbine Club in 1972, said he grew up in this intense racing atmosphere. His father never said that he was born in 1890. He was born in Carbine's year. As for his marriage, that didn't come about in 1916, it took place in Sasanof's year. Mr Cavanough said he even had to refer to racing fixtures to remember his own wedding anniversary.

There are many reasons for the extraordinary success of the Melbourne Cup. In Queen Victoria's day Melbourne was the biggest and richest city in the colony. Partly, of course, it was a simple fact of geography—Melbourne was in the middle. Most horses came by ship, so it was much more difficult for Adelaide and Hobart owners to get their horses all the way to Sydney. And for the prime reason, there was the inspired Mr R. C. Bagot. He had a hand in our two most deeply revered institutions, Flemington Racecourse and the Melbourne Cricket Ground.

He was the first secretary of the Victoria Racing Club, which began in 1864. He was an Irishman, and he had the unique idea that racing was for the cash customers, better than the timber affair which had been provided for the members. Furthermore, he drained the marsh in the centre of the course and threw open the Flat for 'no charge'. All Melbourne could come there for a picnic, a picnic that was a real carnival. That is barely an exaggeration; there was never an entertainment for the masses like this. In the pre-turnstile era one is inclined to be skeptical of crowd figures, yet the official crowd figure for 1880 was only 282,000. When you consider that the entire population at that time was only ninety thousand, better than one in every three people were at the races on that day. Over ninety years later we still struggle to get a crowd of a hundred thousand.

There was yet another reason for the adoration of the Melbourne Cup, and racing in general. The classic races in England have been weight-for-age affairs; for, say, three-year-olds, which all carry the same weight. Therefore the colt that wins the Derby and the filly that takes the Oaks are clearly the finest in the field. So after these races they are retired to preserve the line, to propagate further champions. This was no good at all to the working man. In the 1860s Melbourne was full of the Irish and the Cornstalks who had sampled life on the goldfields. They knew what poverty was like and everywhere there was the hunger to get rich quick. What odds could you get for a weight-for-age race? You just had to look for the best horse and the bookmakers knew this better than anybody.

Ah, but the Melbourne Cup, spread over an agonizing two miles, was a completely unpredictable race. It still is an unpredictable race. We can quote you colts and fillies, three-year-olds, four-year-olds, seven-year-olds no end, who have won the classic. The favourite has come home less than one in four and the odds are splendid.

So Australians, who are known to have a humble interest in gambling, adore it. We are the only people who put a handicap race on a pedestal above all others, and, if you must have a greatest, we rate the Melbourne Cup as the greatest handicap horse-race in the world.

The idea of a handicap race was not always popular. Just before the first Melbourne Cup in 1861 one racing writer commented: 'To make a handicap of the principal turf event of the year is to make a farce of everything that racing stands for. Its effect would be to make any brumby bought out of a mob for a shilling, the equal to the finest in the land. It's a mad idea, doomed to failure.'

But even by 1862 Melbourne was going berserk over the Melbourne Cup. As *Bell's Life* ecstatically wrote about Archer after the Sydney horse had scored its second consecutive victory: 'Now and then the world gives us a great poet, anon a mighty general who electrifies his fellow men with marvellous victories, and in like manner now and again, a wonder in the shape of horse flesh visits the world.'

In the 1880s the mutual loathing of Sydney for Melbourne and Melbourne for Sydney was working towards its eloquent climax. So the quality of the success of the Melbourne Cup could be gauged by the regular assaults in the Sydney press. Just two days before the Melbourne Cup of 1884, the Sydney *Echo* painted the Melbourne Cup as a sinful event bordering on the horrors of Sodom and Gomorrah, the cause of 'murder, suicide, crime, despair and disgrace'. The *Echo* said:

Let those who derive pleasure from the sport, and have no desire to make a profit, open their eyes and ears; let them listen to the voices of ministers from the pulpit, of judges from the bench, let them see with their own eyes if they will, the countless cases of trouble, disaster, ruin, wrought by this sport.

The following year the *Sydney Morning Herald* thundered in like vein over this national cesspool of avarice:

It is the worst occasion and cause for a national gathering, that is naturally allied to more that makes directly for human degradation than any other public sport or pastime that could be named. The spirit and incentive of gambling hangs upon its skirts and penetrates to its heart. Artless and glaring extravagance fattens upon its countless forms of trickery, and knavery finds encouragement in it.

The *Herald* then became eloquent about how the festivals of Europe were devoted to all that was fine and noble, representing the purest sentiment. What was our festival, the Melbourne Cup, devoted to?—all that was rotten!

The Melbourne press naturally responded to these thoughts: 'Let us think a moment. Is there any racing in New South Wales? Yes, there is; something on a level with our "Wallaby Flat" or "Narracan Valley" meetings . . . Even the joys of Sydney Harbour cannot keep the Sydney people away from the cabbage garden at Carnival time. Hence these strongly worded lamentations. Poor Sydney.'

Almost certainly the Melbourne Cup reached its peak in 1890, Carbine's year. By November, Melbourne was already well into the worst depression it had ever known and finance firms were collapsing one after another. The prize money for the Melbourne Cup was £10,000, an immense sum for that time. The working man earned about £2 a week, so the equivalent for the 1970s would have to be at least $200,000.

*Melbourne Punch* said sourly: 'A veil of gloom painted with RUIN on it in lurid letters covers the face of the land. Now with one sweep and final burst of the dismal drop-curtain has been drawn up once more at Flemington. We might say that £10,000 is a large premium for success in hippoculture. We are not absurd enough to suppose that a mere man could have an immortal soul, is as good as a horse in this country.'

Of course Carbine was very nearly a god. As the *Age* pointed out: he carried ten stone five pounds, a weight never previously borne to victory in a Melbourne Cup (nor has it been carried since). What's more he ran it in record time for the race (3 minutes 28½ seconds), and he beat a record number of starters.

Current newspaper coverage of the Spring Racing Carnival is something to behold, but in 1890 it could hardly have been less impressive. *The Argus* on 5 November 1890 had sixteen huge columns, almost the same amount of space as that devoted to the outbreak of the First World War.

Fashions were minutely detailed, and of course, the weekly *Table Talk* went on for page after page. Everyone and what they wore was listed down the page in alphabetical order starting: 'Miss Armytage, blue and white pongee silk with large white hat. Mrs Ashley, petunia brocade and velvet with bonnet to match. Mrs W. Andrews, black faille and jet, with lace bonnet.'

After all this there was the report of the Government House Ball with all twelve hundred guests listed. Not to have had your name on that list must have been social ruin.

The race was described in extraordinary detail by the regular writers, but also by the staff super-writer. For *The Argus* he went under the by-line 'Telemachus'. The first two columns he devoted to philosophy, quoting Virgil, Robert Burns, Swinburne and even Sophocles in the original Greek. He wrote: 'Ay, the horse is, and ever will be, the friend beloved of gods and men. Sport and supreme consolation also.'

But this great eloquence came as he described the finish: '"Where's Carbine?" "Where's Carbine?" shouted a score of backers. It was only when the turn was reached that everybody knew where Carbine was. He shot through his field like a thunderbolt; then he closed in on the rails; he placed himself as the pilot fish before the shark, clear away from all his frantic pursuers . . . before the thunder broke from all the clouds of humanity which had settled down on Flemington.

The Hill roared to the Flat, and the leaves rent by a September gale. Men leapt and shouted and women screamed. It went flying through the air like flood waves across a barrier, full of delight. Up in the wake of the horses flowed the people, all shouting, all cheering, all jubilation over the greatest victory ever known on the Australian turf. And out then from the stewards' stand came Mr Donald Wallace, his face like the rising sun, barred with the silvery line of his white moustache. A happy man. Aye doubtless, it must be a strange sensation to march down the turf to greet him by the hoof, or to fall upon his neck, to embrace, or to weep over him, or to do something in some way remarkable or outrageous . . .'

In 1895 Carbine left forever to go to England. Mr Donald Wallace sold him for £13,000 to the Duke of Portland, to go to stud. The grief in Australia at this event was enormous. Mr John Murray, in the Victorian House of Assembly, even suggested that considering Carbine was an idol, even a god, could he not be made the Victorian Agent-General in London?

There were crafty moves to divert the public from Carbine's departure. The *Herald* reported that Carbine would not go aboard the *Orizaba* until the Friday. However this did not stop a large crowd turning up regardless. The *Age* reported that no departure of a distinguished citizen had ever attracted such a large crowd to the Port Melbourne Railway Pier. Not a governor, an alderman, nor an admiral would have drawn such a crowd—two thousand people were there, wrote Nat Gould, the racing writer, in his book *On and Off the Turf in Australia*:

I never saw a more determined mass of people than Carbine's admirers. They crushed up the gangway and jammed in front of his box, regardless of torn clothes and pickpockets, and there were plenty of the latter about, or what looked like them. Hundreds of people caught a passing glimpse of Carbine as he stood quietly eating in his box . . . When we cast off from Sandridge there was a mighty burst of cheering and cries of 'Carbine', rent the air. I was near the horse's box and Old Jack pricked up his ears at the sound, as though he fancied there was another race to be run. A beautiful wreath in the shape of a horseshoe was sent on board for Carbine.

Henry Varley, the Temperance preacher, was horrified at all this idolatry. In the name of the Anti-Gambling League he brought out a tract accusing the leading daily journal of a disgraceful misuse of the functions of the Press. He headed his page:

MELBOURNE'S IDOL, LORD CARBINE.
HORSE WORSHIP AT FLEMINGTON.
'O CARBINE, SAVE US!'

No time to lose. The Press hath given
Golden applause; *Argus* and *Age*
Bend low in house of Donald Wallace
And horseflesh passes human sage.
Go forth, ye citizens, to meet him
Victoria's God await in line;
Ramage rides Carbine to glory
Worship your idol horse Carbine.

There was much verse written about Carbine, but undoubtedly the most touching was that titled 'Carbine's Good-bye', written by John Sandes who wrote under the name 'Oriel' in *The Argus*.

Place your palm upon my shoulder
Stroke my smooth and glossy mane,
Let me lay my honest muzzle
In my owner's well-loved hand,
I shall never feel my dear old master's
Proud caress again,
Beyond the cruel ocean in a strange and distant land.

No more your ruddy face will smile, your kindly lips no more
Will call me truest, staunchest steed, your horse without a peer;
And yet I somehow know that fancy can the past restore,
And when I am in England, ah, my heart will still be there.

And now good-bye. To each and all a last and fond farewell.
Yes, pat me, Hickenbotham, trusty trainer, truest friend,
And unbuckle this old headstall, while I turn my head to tell
The people of Australia that I'll love them to the end.

Throw open all the windows and throw wide the stable doors,
And let me see my public in this hour of parting dire;
Perhaps some day a colt may come from England to these shores,
And in my son they'll recognize the image of his sire.

This was a great era for verse and ballads. Every noble deed had to be recorded in rhyme and naturally the gods of the community, the sportsmen, did very well indeed. One of the gods was Tommy Corrigan, an amiable little Irishman, born in County Meath in 1854. He came to Australia in 1866 and had his first ride at Flemington on Melbourne Cup Day when he was thirteen. There were few big races in New South Wales, Victoria and South Australia that he did not win. He was the nation's most celebrated jockey. Everybody knew Tommy Corrigan in the manner that everyone knew Don Bradman in the 1930s. He was fearless, and in twenty-eight years of riding he had an extraordinary safety record, never a broken bone. When warned of a horse's reputation for throwing jockeys he would say that only badly-handled animals remained bad-tempered. Banjo Paterson wrote of him:

When any slip means sudden death—with wife and child to keep—
It needs some nerve to draw the whip and flog him at the leap—
But Corrigan would ride them, by danger undismayed,
He never flinched at fence or wall, he never was afraid;
With easy seat and nerve of steel, light hand and smiling face,
He held the sluggard horses neck, and made the sluggards race.

Tommy rode a New Zealand bred horse, Waiter, in the Grand National Steeplechase of 1894 at Caulfield. Before the race even the Governor, the Earl of Hopetoun, wished him luck. 'Wouldn't you like to be coming with us, Sir?' said Tommy.

At the fourth hurdle Waiter fell. Tommy landed on his head and the horse rolled on top of him. He was carried off the track in an ambulance. Dr Thomas Fitzgerald, Melbourne's most celebrated surgeon, better known as the original owner of Young and Jackson's 'Chloe', was in attendance. He looked at Corrigan's brain injuries, said there was no hope for him, and suggested that all they could do was take him home. Corrigan lived nearby in Caulfield. He died there at 7 a.m. on the Monday morning.

The funeral was on the Wednesday, and the *Sportsman* wrote that never in the history of racing anywhere in the world had there been such a tribute to a jockey. It surpassed all other public funerals in Melbourne, except that of Burke and Wills. It started from Corrigan's house in Kambrook Road, Caulfield, at 2 p.m. There were two hundred and forty vehicles in the procession plus a 'vast procession on foot'.

When the cortege reached Princes Bridge in the city a hundred and fifty jockeys and trainers joined it and marched in front of the hearse to the Melbourne Cemetery. The whole procession took forty-two minutes to pass.

As it went down into the grave the coffin carried Corrigan's colours, his riding boots and a wreath sent by the V.A.T.C. The number of wreaths was astonishing. There were more than seventy of them. They came from the Governor, the Earl of Hopetoun, the Victorian Club, the VRC, and Tattersall's Club in Sydney sent, according to the *Australasian*, one of the finest wreaths ever made in this country.

There were many poems and ballads, almost every newspaper had one. The best of them was on sale in the streets a week after Corrigan died. These were the closing verses:

The flag was dropped and off they sailed, with Dondi in the lead,
Till Corrigan on Waiter came, with onward mighty speed;
He was fast approaching Dondi, and rounding on the turn,
When Waiter came a fearful crash and seemed to overturn.
Poor Tommy was beneath him, with a deep wound in his head,
And everyone upon the course made sure that he was dead;
And as they bore him from the scene into the doctor's room,
Alas, poor Tom, they knew the truth, that he had met his doom.
'We have lost the bravest jockey that ever held a rein,
For Tommy Corrigan must die, he'll never speak again.'
Now all you who have loved him, and followed him through life,
I hope you won't forget to help his sorrow-stricken wife,
She has lost a faithful husband, let us offer to console,
And may the Great Almighty God have mercy on his soul.

After such names as Carbine and Corrigan, one must go on to Phar Lap. If 1890 was Carbine's Melbourne Cup year, 1930 was Phar Lap's. In her biography of Phar Lap, Isobel Carter wrote: 'Phar Lap, a taxidermist's triumph, a tourist triumph infinitely greater than Cleopatra's feast.'

In popular speech his name has become synonym for speed as Ned Kelly's is for gameness. He has been celebrated in song and verse; his portrait has been painted by famous artists; the National Museum in Melbourne, where his stuffed and mounted hide is on display, has become a place of pilgrimage.

And a place of pilgrimage it was. An overseas visitor made a complaint in a long letter to the *Herald*. She wrote: 'I decided the best way to see your city was on a tourist bus offering an afternoon viewing the city sights. Imagine my disappointment and disgust when this tour started with a five minute stop at the National Museum to see a horse.'

At least until the building of the Arts Centre in St Kilda Road, Melbourne's two greatest tourist attractions were the nude painting of 'Chloe' in the saloon bar at Young and Jackson's Hotel, and the stuffed hide of Phar Lap at the museum. While 'Chloe' inspired genuine devotion from all visiting males, Phar Lap was the greater drawcard because of the more religious nature of the worship. Although both had the touch of the martyr about them—the original model for 'Chloe' is said to have died by suicide back in 1876, having boiled the heads of some matches and drunk the water. Some still insist that Phar Lap died of poison, so the two big drawcards had that in common.

Until 1968 the National Gallery was in the same building as the Museum. One attendant didn't even bother to give directions to the art gallery, he would automatically point the way to Phar Lap, and only occasionally would he have to complete the direction with 'and the gallery's through there too'. An assistant-director of the National Gallery, Mr R. H. G. Haines, complained that art treasures were merely landmarks on the way to Phar Lap. He said: 'Phar Lap's body should be placed in the centre of an intricate maze, through which people must pass the whole gallery of paintings, to find it. It is disappointing to be working in a gallery and to find so many people come here only to see a horse.'

Actually it was fascinating to watch the tourist buses arrive. They did not get as far as the gallery. The bus would stop outside the Russell Street entrance, the passengers would troop inside. They would give little attention to the vertebrae of whales, the relics of dinosaurs, or the weapons of Aborigines. They would march straight to Phar Lap, pause for five minutes in wondrous admiration before the big horse, march out again, and so on to the next attraction, usually Captain Cook's cottage in the Fitzroy Gardens.

Phar Lap's remains however are spread far afield. His fourteen-pound heart is displayed together with the normal heart of an Army remount, a mere six pounds, under glass at the Australian Institute of Anatomy in Canberra. The American sporting writer, John Lardner, could hardly believe the tiny heart beside the huge one was that of a horse. 'For all I know, it belonged to a marsupial mouse and they rang it in just to build up the tragedy.' But that is not all. The Phar Lap skeleton is in the Dominion Museum, Wellington. Phar Lap started fifty-one times for thirty-seven wins, three seconds and two thirds. He won £66,738 in stakes and his supporters used to claim that when fit and well he could give any horse of his time a hundred yards over a mile and beat him.

The great Australian legend is that both Les Darcy the boxer, and Phar Lap were 'murdered' by the Americans. The truth is that Phar Lap was remarkably fortunate to escape the Australians. On Saturday morning, 1 November 1930, Tommy Woodcock was walking Phar Lap back to his Glenhuntly stables after a workout at Caulfield. He was 11/8 on, the shortest priced Cup favourite in history, and that afternoon, just to complete his Cup preparations, he was running in the 10-furlong Melbourne Stakes.

Woodcock was riding a grey pony and leading Phar Lap. He noticed a large blue sedan parked at the side of the road. They both concealed their faces behind newspapers. Then they started their car again and came up behind the horse. Woodcock looked at the car again and to his horror a double-barrelled shotgun was pointing out the rear window. Desperately he dug his heels into the pony's side and tried to get between the gun and Phar Lap.

The shotgun went off, but neither man nor horse was hurt. The blue car raced off down the road, escaping identification. How the blast failed to hit Phar Lap at such point blank range is not easy to understand. Tommy Woodcock must take the credit for putting the gunman off his aim and saving the champion's life.

Phar Lap was not upset by all this. He won his race easily that afternoon. But his trainer Harry Telford was alarmed. Immediately after the race he took him away to a secret hide-out and for the next three days the best newspaper story was the Mystery of Phar Lap. Where was he? Actually he was locked in a padlocked stable at Werribee, twenty-five miles from Melbourne, under armed guard, and only on Tuesday morning, under special escort, was he taken to Flemington for the Melbourne Cup.

He didn't give his opponents a chance. He won his race easily by two doubt. He cleared out from the field and won going away. The Cup of 1931 was a different tale. He was a sick horse, wearied by the years of hard racing, and the handicappers gave him the appalling task of carrying ten stone ten pounds, nineteen pounds more than any other horse in the race, and he had to concede half a hundredweight to the winner, White Nose.

Phar Lap's jockey Jimmy Pike did not torture his horse. He let him run his natural race and did not try to achieve the impossible. His American owner, D. J. Davis, wanted to show him off in his own country and collect the far larger stakes available over there. Phar Lap arrived at San Francisco on 1 January 1932, trained for several weeks, then went five hundred miles south to Caliente in Mexico where he caused a sensation by winning the $50,505 Agua Caliente handicap. His trainer Harry Telford had remained home in Australia and the training job had now fallen to his strapper, Tommy Woodcock.

Phar Lap wasn't given much chance in the big Mexican race. H. A. Wolfe, Australia's top racing writer, who was there covering the Phar Lap tour, later wrote: 'If every man at Agua Caliente who had made sneering remarks about Australian methods and wisecracks about Tommy Woodcock and his apologies for the line of repenting ones would have extended for five hundred miles.' Phar Lap won that race by two lengths in 2 minutes 2¾ seconds, a track record for one and three-quarter miles.

After this Phar Lap was demanded for races all over the United States, but in training at Caliente the horse split a hoof and probably should never have raced. So Davis sent him to Menlo Park in California to recuperate.

Several days later Phar Lap began to run a temperature and on the morning of 5 April Bill Nielsen, veterinary surgeon with the party, treated him for colic. Soon the horse was in great pain and Woodcock walked him round and round the yard. Phar Lap was in agony, and by early afternoon he couldn't walk and he had to be taken to his stall.

In Woodcock's own words:

Nielsen told me to keep Phar Lap on the move until he returned with assistance. On no account must I allow him to stop, but when Phar Lap had swollen to half his size again and was groaning in pain, I took him to the barn. He whimmied, he groaned dementedly, I rushed about to make him more comfortable. Coming toward me he nosed me affectionately under my arm. Then something inside him burst, he drenched me in blood and fell dead at my feet.

The news of Phar Lap's death left Australia in an extraordinary state of bereavement. One moment Phar Lap was the mighty conqueror, going to conquer all America, the next moment he was dead.

The first cables told of Woodcock's misery. Woodcock threw his arms around the horse's neck and wept unrestrainedly. Mrs D. J. Davis, wife of the part-owner, was hurriedly summoned and tried to comfort the trainer. Finally he was dragged away from the horse. Woodcock said: 'My friends in Australia know how I loved the horse. He was almost human and he could almost speak. At home they will realize what his death means to me.'

Something like disbelief was the first reaction. *Truth* reported: 'Every sportsman and non-sportsman was staggered . . . It cast a gloom over the whole Continent and in the Dominion across the Tasman. Every man who had wagered on the champion whether it was five shillings or £100 felt pangs of remorse steal chokingly at his throat.'

The artist Daryl Lindsay said: 'It's like the Prince of Wales is dead. It was a long time before I could take in the significance of it.' The artist bought a paper. People were standing about the store with long, stunned faces. Phar Lap was dead.

Harry Telford, the trainer who stayed behind, suffered the greatest grief. The *Sporting Globe* reported: 'Poor Telford. He is quite distracted. He walks around Braeside like one in a trance. The tragedy has been a terrible blow to him. Listlessly he wanders about the place—into the huge stables, out again into the exercise yard and down on the track, following a phantom, or perhaps listening for some spirit voice that will tell him Phar Lap is not dead.' Telford said: 'I have had some sad days and some reverses, but this is the worst I have known. He was almost human, an angel almost. I've never practised idolatry but I loved that horse. He was to come back and end his days in these paddocks. I wish to God he was coming back.'

It was almost as if the King had died. In Sydney flags at many public and private buildings were flown at half mast in mourning for Phar Lap. In Melbourne a city pavement artist produced a picture of the horse draped with an Australian flag and the inscription 'The Late Phar Lap. R.I.P.' At the City Court there was an offensive behaviour case. Two men had come to blows over which was the greatest horse, Phar Lap or Carbine. The Magistrate commented that it was an argument unlikely to be settled, not even in fifty years.

Naturally the newspapers burst into verse. The *Sun News-Pictorial* wrote:

Phar Lap farewell; your race is run.
And 'tis not in defeat that Death has won.
Your victory is that extinction came
While yet the world was ringing with your name.
To swell your country's fame you've done your part—
Your name is graven on Australia's heart.

*The Argus* said: 'Those who are content to take life as they find it will find it realize that the Carbines and Phar Laps are something more than horses that run in races. They are national figures, sometimes symbols of physical power, courage and pride in performance.'

The Sydney *Sun* in an editorial on the front page saw Phar Lap as one who stood serene above all others, and in the enthusiasm with which the crowds followed his performances, even though it was unthinkable for most of them to back the horse, he was an inspiration for all that sport in its best sense, should mean to everybody.

But why did Phar Lap die? Even the first stories tossed out suggestions of foul play. Yankee gangsters or international greed were at the bottom of it all, said some—'Ah, yes, another Les Darcy'. In threatening tones *Truth* said: 'America has a hoodoo on Australian champions. Les Darcy died there. Funny rumours got about that. Phar Lap died from some irritant poison. It was given to him in this way—in his stall were two windows and the poison was placed in balls of clay and thrown through.'

Bert Wolfe says that after Phar Lap's death there was a great flurry. County, City, State police, even the F.B.I. were rushed in. He said some of these clay balls were found in Phar Lap's stall and he tried to steal away with one of them to have it analysed. He hid it in his shoe, but he wasn't clever enough. As he rushed out 'hot-foot for San Francisco', he had it taken from him. Summing up he said: 'I saw the autopsy on Phar Lap. His stomach was highly inflamed and perforated and there's no doubt that it was due to a severe poison. It was not caused by the alfalfa or barley covered with dew in the stableyard which had been eaten by the horse. This alfalfa had been sprayed with a zinc concentrate, strong enough to have the effect which the stomach showed. If Phar Lap died from this why hadn't every other horse on the place died also?'

Isobel Carter in her book *Phar Lap* went exhaustively into the causes of Phar Lap's death. She did not go along with Bert Wolfe's clay ball theory. Certainly there were traces of arsenic. But Tommy Woodcock said that Phar Lap was in the habit of picking up bits of mud, rolling them into balls with his tongue and dropping them.

She concludes that Phar Lap almost certainly died of intestinal tympany—swelling of the abdomen by air in the intestine and other organs. It occurs when horses are fed highly fermentable green pasture. She believes Phar Lap emerged from his stable on the morning before his death and grazed on some succulent but lethal lucerne dampened by a San Francisco fog. The huge accumulation of gas in the horse's intestines could have been relieved, perhaps, by the insertion of a hollow tapping instrument through the stomach wall.

But the legend of the poisoning lived on. Damon Runyon lived on Broadway during the Second World War when he received a letter from an American soldier in Australia, who said: 'Mr Runyon, when us fellows go into a bar over here and get to talking sports to one of them is sure to say, "Ho, you Yanks murdered Phar Lap . . ." I wish you would write and tell me something about that horse and why and how the Australians seem to think there was some crime in connection with him.' Runyon replied that the idea that Americans would poison Phar Lap just because they feared he would make suckers out of local champions was just plain daffy. 'No, Sonny,' wrote he, 'no-one in this country would dream of murdering a horse like that.'

John Lardner, another famous American sports writer, also suffered from the Phar Lap legend. When Murray Rose went to the United States, Lardner was delighted. Murray Rose still managed to set world swimming records, although a vegetarian, thus disproving the age-old Australian theory that athletes had to build muscle on steak. So that to have the grass-eating Rose walking around the U.S. without a bodyguard was clear indication that all was forgiven.

But was all forgiven? John Lardner reported how he called in at Jimmy Carruthers' Bells Hotel in Sydney. He was being crowded in at a scrimmage at the bar when he shouted, 'Take it easy, cobber, I'm not Avery Flaming Brundage.' 'Stone the . . . crows you're not,' the cobber replied. 'All the same, you blokes, because you're not, I'll buy you a drink, Yank.' Lardner wrote: 'Later over the beer he began to brood. "All the same, my word it was." So there's no end to that. How will we ever prove to the Australians that the big horse died naturally of the old "put-and-take"—meaning the stomach-ache?'

But the Phar Lap legend goes on and on. For years on the anniversary of Phar Lap's death, some anonymous Phar Lap lover used to nail a wreath on the main gate at Randwick racecourse. The wreath always was inscribed: 'In memory of Phar Lap, Australia's greatest racehorse.'

Racing euphoria reached its peak with Phar Lap, but there were many other greats beloved by the public—Peter Pan, Bernborough, Shannon, Tobin Bronze, Tulloch, and Gunsynd who was the most recent proof that four-footed creatures are more valuable than humans.

Gunsynd won $280,455 in prize money, and in September 1972 his owners sold him to a syndicate of breeders for another $270,618. Soon after this came the announcement of Gunsynd's retirement, and it was like the goodbye to a great hero who had achieved extraordinary glory for Australia.

The *Sun News-Pictorial* ran a series of articles minutely examining the life story of Gunsynd. Even Sir Henry Bolte, who had retired a little earlier after a record period as Premier of Victoria, hardly received better cover. Gunsynd received a series of emotional farewells all around the country. Gunsynd's farewell was on 17 March 1973 at Flemington, more in the manner of the last goodbyes to Dame Nellie Melba. Gunsynd had to stand there in the mounting yard and listen to all sorts of laudatory speeches.

Mr A. R. Creswick, the chairman of the V.R.C., likened him to the idols of old—Carbine, Phar Lap and Tulloch. The *Herald* reported: 'The crowd clapped and cheered as Gunsynd entered the mounting yard . . . Never has there been such cheering, handclapping and waving at Flemington.'

He won his race at Flemington and he won his farewell race at Randwick, Sydney, on 21 April. 'As jockey Kevin Langby brought the champion back to scale there was pandemonium,' recorded the press. The crowd of 55,022 went wild.

There were Gunsynd T-shirts, Gunsynd ball-point pens and Gunsynd cocktails. Four songs were written about him and recorded. One was titled *The Grey Who Grew* and another was called *Goondiwindi Grey*. Here's an excerpt from *Goondiwindi Grey*:

When Roy Higgins rode him in the Cup in Melbourne
He raced the champion all the way
But the great weight cost a win for this mighty horse Gunsynd,
Who is known as the Goondiwindi Grey.

Chorus:
For his winnings, he's Australia's greatest racehorse
And you can bet he's a champion all the way,
He's a safe bet, place or win, this mighty horse Gunsynd,
Who is known as the Goondiwindi Grey.

As one newspaper put it, Gunsynd collected rugs like Elizabeth Taylor collected fur coats. They were all handed over with great ceremony and they were all in the champion's colours—purple and white. There was one from the V.R.C. in Melbourne, and another from the Australian Jockey Club in Sydney. There was a third from the Sydney Turf Club and a fourth from the Amateur Turf Club in Brisbane.

Gunsynd went to Queensland for his Doomben farewell in May. The horse was met at the Queensland border by motor-cycle police and then guarded continually by security men. Gunsynd's stall was be-ribboned in purple and white, and the stallion was attended by two mini-skirted girls also in purple and white. Then the presentation rug had the words 'Queensland's Gunsynd'. The Goondiwindi Grey is very nearly Queensland's most beloved son.

In Australia, as in England, it is a good thing to be associated with racing. It is most prestigious to be on the committee of a racing club, and committeemen tend to have impeccable backgrounds. Mr Creswick, for example, chairman of the V.R.C., was an Olympic equestrian, a polo player, and educated at Melbourne Grammar School and Oxford. Also it is good to be a Western District man of wealth like a previous chairman, Sir Chester Manifold, who attended Geelong Grammar and Jesus College, Cambridge.

The elections are normally a most gentlemanly affair, as those of a very exclusive club should be. When Sir Henry Bolte retired from politics it almost seemed that the highest honour that could be bestowed upon him was this—election unopposed as a committeeman of the V.R.C.

Just occasionally a battle leaks out into the open. There was a shock, even horror, when four candidates fought for one vacancy on the committee of the V.A.T.C. in May 1970. The candidates brought in public relations men to push their chances, they lobbied by telephone, and one candidate even charged that ten days before the election someone broke into his printing works and burned a thousand of his campaign letters. Some of the older members thought even having campaign letters at all was something that wasn't quite done.

Victoria Racing Club attendances reached their peak in 1949 with 702,106 and an average attendance of 41,300. From then on there was a steady decline, falling to 470,039 in 1969, average attendance 27,943. However, the V.R.C. claims attendances for race clubs all over Australia are now rising again. Attendances for 1971 were 511,318, and for 1972, 527,509. The V.R.C. was particularly pleased with its crowd for the 1972 Melbourne Cup, 102,906. Not as good as Spearfelt's year (118,877 in 1926), but the Flat does not exist any more, so the V.R.C. is able to claim 1972 as an all-time high for a crowd in the stand enclosure and on the Lawn with 66,848. In Spearfelt's year they had only 36,000.

Yes, the Melbourne Cup prospers. In 1971 the V.R.C. raised the stake to $100,000, in terms of real money the best stake since the 1890s. It was just as well, for on the following 1 January the West Australia Turf Club ran its $100,000 Perth Cup. Melburnian punters thought it a sacrilege that a junior affair in the West should be of equal value. The humiliation was too much. The V.R.C. raised the stakes for the 1973 Melbourne Cup to $150,000, and in Sydney the stakes for the Caulfield Cup went up to $112,500 and the 1974 Golden Slipper Stakes rose to $100,000.

If gambling can be described as sport then interest is far from flagging. In 1970–71 the national betting bill on horse-racing, trotting and greyhound-racing was $1,628 million, and that bill continues to go up at a rate of 15 per cent a year. So this flow of cash is contributing to an all round racing boom. Racehorses are even more important to the economy than stud bulls. The record price for a bull in Australia was $56,000 paid for a Brahman at Rockhampton in 1969. But this was nothing on 24 July in Lexington, Kentucky, when Sydney trainer Tommy Smith paid $280,000 for a yearling colt. Indeed 1973 may not have been a boom year on the Stock Exchange, but it certainly was at the yearling sales. The score for yearlings sold by May in Australia and New Zealand was over $12,000,000.

Yes, racing is very important. If one is interested in listening to the radio on Saturdays or even on week day afternoons, it is almost impossible to avoid the high-pressure jabber of races that can be heard on almost every city and country radio station.

In 1973 the *Australian* reported that there was an 'all-out war' between Melbourne radio stations 3UZ and 3DB to win racing listener ratings. Said the *Australian*: 'Both stations now travel far and wide to cover even the smallest TAB race meetings and both give a 10-race coverage of the greyhound meetings in the city and some provincial fixtures . . . punters have never had it so good.'

Australians insist that their sporting commentators, like their sportsmen, are the best in the world. Some of them are even revered. Commentators like Bill Collins travel the world to meetings in America, Britain and Japan, so that Australians can have the quality of an Australian voice for the big occasions. Bill Collins is so loved as a racing commentator that he also appears as a compere for popular television variety programmes.

So one need not fear for the health of racing. There are still six hundred race clubs in Australia and 464 racecourses. As pointed out earlier we have more racecourses than any nation on earth. By comparison France, a very good racehorse country, has 265 racecourses and England has 64, the same number as Victoria.

Our racehorse population in 1971 was nineteen thousand and we had two thousand nine hundred days of racing. But whether we can do without them is a matter for debate. The racing men consider themselves the most highly taxed in the community. In Victoria they pay a 30 per cent tax into the course. Every time the punter makes a bet, the Government takes a slice. Then in the tension of it all the racegoer smokes a little more, drinks a little more, and so the Treasury benefits again.

Oh, the racegoer is a great help.
