Billiards, like the piano, had problems in competing with the electronic entertainments of the twentieth century. Its decline started with radio, it received no help at all from the gramophone record, and with the advent of television the patient became desperately ill.

No longer did a hotel of eminence have to provide a billiard table for its patrons. How much easier, how much less space-consuming to put a television set in the bar. And, besides, whoever heard of a billiard table at that most anti-social of all modern institutions, the motel.

So many of the grand old billiard tables which had endured for a century went to the graveyard, simply because their owners were not prepared to pay the money to keep them in playing condition. Space was another factor. The nice people of Toorak, even the squatters of the Western District, no longer could afford a special room for a gargantuan billiard table. The nadir came around 1958, 1959. I remember a billiard table restorer, who told me, almost with tears in his eyes, of one lovely table that had been broken down and now the slates were being used as a urinal in a city hotel.

Yet it is doubtful whether, man for man, any country has ever gone as crazy over billiards as Australia. Billiards had its vogue in England but there was always a slight aroma of infamy, or caddishness about it. Was a good billiardist somebody you really wanted to know? He was a little too sharp; it was a sign of a mis-spent youth. Frank Richards, when he produced his incredible range of comics including the *Magnet* and the *Gem*, always made the cads good at billiards. For example, there was the 'bounder of the Remove', Vernon-Smith. He did terrible things. He smoked, he gambled, and when he was in real trouble he went off and played billiards at the local pub, the Three Fishers.

In Australia it was different. Not only were there large and popular billiard houses, but no good club, no hotel of quality could afford to be without its tables. And a gentleman of affluence or quality, whether he was in the city or out on his station property, found it absolutely necessary to have a billiard table for the entertainment of his guests.

The game was so popular that Australia produced a series of world champions, and a super-champion in Walter Lindrum. Indeed, Walter Lindrum was in the legend class, like Don Bradman, so superior to his fellows that he virtually destroyed the system.

The remarkable growth of billiards in Australia was largely due to one man of genius, Henry Upton Alcock. Born in Dublin in 1823, he studied cabinet making for seven years and went to London to learn the billiard table trade. Then in 1852, like another Irishman, he heard of the magic of gold and emigrated to Australia. But he did not follow the march up the Ballarat Road. In 1853 he set up a business with four men in Fitzroy and started making his famous billiard tables.

At first he was desperate for materials, but cleverly he made use of beautiful Tasmanian and local timbers, which had been spurned by Australians. How could they be as good as cedar and oak and the fine timbers of home? He was told they would crack, they would warp and it was impossible for an antipodean timber ever to hold its polish. He defied all these ideas and went straight ahead. He used blackwood, tulip, maple, Huon pine, and musk from Dandenong.

Finding slate seemed an impossible problem, but he discovered that some of the early settlers in Collingwood had built houses made entirely of slate, all imported from England. So he pulled down houses and converted them to billiard tables.<sup>2</sup> Later he imported slate by the ship load and he even started his own slate quarry at Castlemaine.

Rapidly his business expanded. He moved to the city and within ten years he had a vast establishment which covered almost an entire block in Russell Street, Melbourne, between Little Bourke Street and Lonsdale Street. Alcock & Co. was one of the wonders of Melbourne, something that had to be seen like the Botanic Gardens, the Theatre Royal or the Zoo.

In 1862 he was making three billiard tables a week and the average price for a full-size Exhibition table was £100. There was the 'Exhibition Prize' with the well-filed curvy legs and carved flowers and ferns, as richly decorated as a Corinthian column. There was the 'Roberts' in figured blackwood, 'The Squatter's Favourite' also in blackwood but a little more austere, and the standard model in cedar with blackwood cushions. This cost £90, or ten pieces, and so constructed that it could be carried on pack horses to places where no one dreamed it would be possible to take billiard tables. So Alcock tables even went to the gold diggings.

Henry Alcock organised a series of tours by English players and his first real breakthrough came in 1864 with the visit of John Roberts Senior, champion billiard player of the world. Alcock wrote:

> He at a bound, so to speak, exalted billiards to the rank of a science, even in the estimation of men who had previously regarded it as puerile, if not dangerous. Hence the billiard table is regarded as much a necessity as the grand piano in the mansions of the affluent.<sup>4</sup>

Mr Roberts, so the story goes, was stunned when first he played on one of Mr Alcock's tables. He could hardly credit such accuracy and he told the crowd that was watching: 'I never played on a better table in my life.'

Henry Alcock described these words as significant as the Duke of Wellington's 'Up, Guards and at them!' on the field at Waterloo. Mr Roberts was so impressed that as soon as he returned to London he ordered an Alcock table for his saloon. When he came to the colonies in 1867, the young Duke of Edinburgh, although perhaps not quite such an authority, was equally impressed, and he took home a table plus an assortment of beautiful Alcock & Co. cues. After this event one could buy a 'Duke of Edinburgh' table, as selected for H.R.H.', of handsome figured blackwood or tulip wood. One of the first customers for a 'Duke of Edinburgh' was the Melbourne Club.

After this Alcock & Co. had arrived. Before, like all other colonial companies, it had suffered. Local citizens refused to believe anything produced in their own town could ever be as good as anything that came from overseas, but now with the blessing of a world champion and a son of Queen Victoria Alcock & Co. could not go wrong, and the import of English billiard tables almost ceased. Mr Alcock sold a table to Government House and another table for the recreation of members of Parliament. The members made use of a remarkable new patent, an Alcock table that would convert quickly from a dining to a billiard table. Alcock tables went to all the best hotels — Menzies, Scotts, the Port Phillip, the Bull & Mouth — to the best clubs, the Melbourne, Athenaeum, the University, the Yorick; then to Craig's Hotel in Ballarat, the Shamrock in Bendigo and to all the best clubs and hotels in Sydney, Adelaide, Hobart, Perth, Tasmania and New Zealand.

They went all over the world, to England, Malaya, Java, even to Foote's Club in Yokohama, Japan, and Alcock won medals for his tables in London, Dublin, Paris, Vienna, Philadelphia and many other cities.

He developed all sorts of patents and inventions. *Town and Country* journal reported that at the extreme end of the polishing room was a sanctuary which was 'unpolluted by the tread of the general workman'. Here all the secrets were preserved under lock and key. One trusted workman looked after them all. He alone looked after the secret that had struck the illustrious London firm of Thurston & Co. 'with dismay and misfortune'.<sup>5</sup>

Alcock & Co. had devised a method for retaining elasticity in cushions regardless of heat and cold. Lesser tables which did not make use of Mr Alcock's invention had to be heated in front of fires or have tubes of hot water run around the cushions.

There were also secret ingenious methods for making billiard balls. The workmen at Alcock & Co. made them out of elephant tusks and a good deal of tusk was necessary to make the perfect ball. According to Mr Alcock it required one elephant for a game of billiards, two for a game of pool and a grown up family for a game of pyramids.

However, little ivory was wasted. Mr Alcock took home the shavings from the billiard balls and dug them into the ground around his orange trees. He said they helped make beautiful oranges.

Alcock made furniture. He made billiard accessories including a remarkable electric marker which the player could operate from buttons under the table. He also operated a timber business and opened branches in all the capital cities in Australia.

He continued to bring out champions, including John Roberts Junior, the new world champion, and certainly the greatest player of his generation. Even by the time he was nineteen John was already beating his illustrious father.

John played against all the top Australian amateurs and professionals, including the professional Charlie Memmott. Roberts gave him 4,000 start in 18,000 and still won by 1,234 points. He scored at the rate of 535 an hour, which was considered a world record at the time.<sup>6</sup>

Henry Upton Alcock died of bronchitis on 6 August 1912, in his ninetieth year. He was the father of Australian billiards; the many thousands of tables he made spread the popularity of the game, and he even wrote books on how to play billiards. Oddly enough, he never learnt to play it himself.

After the turn of the century came the family that was to dominate the game as it had never been dominated before — Fred Lindrum senior, Fred junior, Walter, Horace and Walter's sister Violet. The greatest of them all was Walter. John Wren used to call him the Phar Lap of billiards.

Walter Lindrum was born in Kalgoorlie on 29 August 1898. His father, Fred Lindrum, the Australian champion, was running a billiard saloon there at the Palace Hotel. He celebrated the birth of his new son over a bottle of champagne with the tenor, Wallace Brownlow.

'What'll we call him?' asked Fred.

'Well, he's the only Lindrum so far born in Western Australia,' said Brownlow, 'so his initials will have to be W.A.' That is how he came to be named Walter Albert.<sup>8</sup>

His father described him as the finest nugget ever found in Kalgoorlie. But the interesting part of the Lindrum story is the determination and dedication which goes into the making of a world champion.

Billiards was big time in Kalgoorlie. Fred Lindrum paid £33 a week rent for a lone table at the Palace, yet he still cleared £100 a week. One day when the hotel caught fire the miners rushed for the billiard saloon, carried out the billiard table and let the hotel burn. The game and the gambling that went with it was so important.

From Kalgoorlie the Lindrum family went to Donnybrook, where Fred Lindrum had a room with two tables and at the age of four little Walter would sit in the corner and watch brother Fred junior being coached. Invariably he would be thrown out for making a nuisance of himself.<sup>7</sup> They went back to Kalgoorlie, then on to another mining town, Broad Arrow. Here Fred junior received the finishing touches before he went out to become Australian champion. Walter would work for hours taking balls out of pockets while his brother strung together enormous breaks.

Walter thought how marvellous it would be if he could be as brilliant as young Fred, but his father thought billiards a hard life and there were already enough players in the family, so he would not let him play. One day he stole the key to the billiard room out of the bar and went in for a long secret session.

In 1938 he recalled:

'I was having the time of my life and I attempted a shot I had seen Fred playing. I shudder even now when I think of it. I ripped a priceless cloth almost a yard square. I was panic stricken. I hid myself behind a bedroom door and cried myself to sleep. Police and black trackers were out looking for me when I awakened and the family heard me crying. I got a good spanking for ripping that cloth but it was worth it as the family saw the uselessness of trying to keep me out of that room.'<sup>8</sup>

So the great training began. He was taught how to hold a cue, how to handle a bridge, and his father drew chalk marks on the floor to show him where to position his feet. Fred Lindrum would watch secretly through the window and if Walter did not use those chalk marks he would take away the balls for the rest of the day. When Walter was eleven the Lindrums moved to Sydney and his father took over the billiard room at Bellfield's Hotel. It was here, at the noble age of twelve that Walter scored his first 100 break and perhaps it was appropriate that he scored his century against Dudley Nourse, the captain of the touring South African cricket team. Fred Lindrum was so pleased that he gave Walter a gold watch to remember the occasion.

Soon after this Mr Fred Lindrum took Fred junior on a tour of England, but before he left he gave Walter some instructions. All the time he was away Walter was to be on his honour to train four hours daily with only two balls and he was to practise the spot shot, the run through shot along the cushion and nothing else. Walter was dumbfounded and aghast at this order, but later he realised what a smart move it had been. It helped him acquire an extraordinarily sensitive touch and when his father returned and he was allowed the third ball again, it was pure joy.

At the same time up went the training schedule. He had to train from 10 a.m. until noon, 2 p.m. until 5 p.m. and 8 p.m. until 10 p.m., six days a week for a year. When he was beginning to look tired his father would say:

'Well, son, is your back aching?'

'Yes, Dad.'

'Then, son, it's time you did a little more.'

One day when he had almost scored a break of 200 his father told him he would not be allowed to leave the room until he had made 250. So starting at 2 p.m. he battled on until 8.30 p.m. until he made it. Several times he wanted to give up but his father said: 'If you want to make a champion you must have pluck and stamina.'<sup>9</sup>

Walter Lindrum recalled that his back felt broken as he struggled home for a very late dinner. When he was fourteen he went on tour giving exhibition matches with his father. In their first match his father crushed him. He gave Walter a start, but in his first break he scored 380 and followed with an unfinished break of 250.

Young Walter was very upset, but his father said that the opponents he would meet in life would treat him that way so it was wise to learn how to handle a beating early.

Walter Lindrum did not mature as a player, and was not certain of his own greatness, until he was nearly thirty. He has recalled how Willie Smith, the English champion, thrashed his brother Fred, by 18,000 in a series of matches in 1928. Fred Lindrum was making a come-back attempt and undoubtedly he took on Smith before he was ready.

Now Fred was Walter's idol and he was determined to get a family revenge, but he needed backing. He went to John Wren, the sporting man's patron. Wren had a billiard table in his large Melbourne mansion at Studley Park, so he asked Lindrum if he would demonstrate a few shots. Walter wanted to do more than that and he asked Wren to time him while he made a break of 1,000.

Wren did not believe anyone could make a break of 1,000 and he looked on in silent incredulity as Lindrum made his 1,000, then proceeded to make another 1,000 in a brilliant, un-ending display of dazzling high-speed scoring.

'All I want to do is see you miss,' said Wren.

'My game doesn't allow for misses,' replied Lindrum.

So Wren was captivated: 'There is no limit to the amount I will back you in any match in which you tell me you can win,' he said.<sup>10</sup>

So Walter prepared to meet Willie Smith. When Smith was playing Fred Lindrum at Alcock's tables in Melbourne he watched secretly by standing on a bag of firewood and looking through a ventilator. He conceded that Smith made a beautiful break of 460, but he had no doubts as to who was the greater. Smith could make 500 in an hour, which was good scoring, but he had trained himself to race to 1,000 in twenty-nine minutes. He was convinced he could give Smith one third start and still win with ease.

So in 1929 Walter went to England to meet Smith and the idea was to take things very calmly, not to show his hand. John Wren had said he would give him unlimited backing in wagers, even £100,000.

But the excitement of it all was too much. Walter Lindrum wanted to show the British public his skill; he couldn't restrain himself. At Newcastle-on-Tyne in their first match Lindrum beat Smith by several thousands, then in London he really let the cat out of the bag, he set a world record of 3,262.

One famous punter, Charlie Hannam, said to him: 'Sonny, until seeing you tonight I would have laid £20,000 to £2,000 on Smith trouncing you. It's money saved.'<sup>11</sup> The series lasted two weeks and Lindrum won by 22,000.

Perhaps the greatest world record was the break of 4,137 that he scored against the reigning world champion, Joe Davis, on 20 January 1932. The London representative of the *Herald* in Melbourne reported:

> As he approached the record, he appeared to become somewhat anxious, wiping his hands and cue. At 3,799 he asked the marker for the score. Apparently he thought he was 100 higher. When within a few points of the record the crowd became so nervously excited that it ceased smoking. Lindrum was then playing nursery cannons. With two to go he stopped, and smiled, chalked his cue, and executed the shot which broke the record.
>
> A great roar of applause followed, interspersed with coo-ees and shouts of 'Good old Walter', such as might have been heard in a crowded stadium. Hats were thrown in the air. The demonstration lasted for five minutes. Lindrum, in response, made a graceful speech. He then continued his play unperturbed, still making the game look absurdly easy.<sup>12</sup>

At 4,137 he missed a cushion cannon by a 'butterfly's whisker' and it was all over. The *Herald* reported that Lindrum was disappointed, he had set his heart on scoring 5,000 and would have kicked the white ball had it been big enough. His record break took two hours fifty-six minutes and it included fifteen sequences of nursery cannons which accounted for 2,578.

It was the story of Don Bradman all over again. Something had to be done to stop this man and the only way to do it was to alter the rules. The controllers of championship billiards put a brake on nursery cannons. They decided first that the player must make the cue ball cross the backline at least once every 100 points,<sup>13</sup> then they relented to make it once every 200 points.<sup>14</sup>

There was much controversy over this. The *News Chronicle* commented: 'Why should they want to stop him scoring? He makes modern billiards, yet the reward of the authorities is to stop him. Nobody wants to stop Kreisler and Lindrum is the Kreisler of billiards.'

But the new rule did not stop him; or shall we say the brake was very gentle. During the 1930s he travelled millions of miles playing billiards in almost every country that had a billiard table and his scores ran to thousands made at extraordinary speed with many breaks of 1,000, 2,000 and more. His world record score under the new rules gradually began to build and in 1940, playing against his brother Fred in Melbourne, he established another world record of 3,361.<sup>18</sup>

He was an easy winner in a world title contest in 1933 and he kept on winning. His personal tragedy was that he virtually ran out of opponents and a Lindrum match became mostly an exhibition of Lindrum. So he devoted much of his energy to charity and his friends claim that in his lifetime he raised over six million dollars.<sup>17</sup>

He died suddenly while holidaying at Surfers Paradise on 30 July 1960, aged 62. The funeral service was at St Paul's Cathedral in Melbourne, and despite the building's great size, it was so filled with mourners that police had to hold back the crowds outside. Then there were so many flowers and so many wreaths that the funeral directors had to lay them out all over the lawns outside. The corner of Flinders and Collins Streets was a sea of flowers.

When the cortège moved off, crowds two and three deep lined either side of Flinders Street all the way to Spring Street. Later the Sportsmen's Association of Australia built a spectacular memorial over his grave at the Melbourne General Cemetery. The top was a billiard table in marble complete with pockets. Resting on the top were three bronze billiard balls and a bronze cue.<sup>14</sup>

Lindrum once said: 'There's been only one really first class player in a generation for the last 100 years',<sup>15</sup> and certainly there has been no one like him since. Perhaps it will take nigh on a hundred years to produce another Walter, but his nephew Horace did become world champion when Joe Davis retired in 1954. Horace is a son of Walter's sister Violet, who was also a great billiard player. Violet married Walter's manager, H. Morell, and Horace used the Lindrum name for professional purposes.

The next snooker star was Eddie Charlton, a former coal miner from New South Wales. Eddie failed in the final of the world snooker championship in May 1973. Ray Reardon of England, a former policeman, beat him at Manchester's City Exhibition Hall 38–32 in a 75 frame contest. Charlton said sadly: 'I have beaten all the top players in the world and have held more titles than any other player. But I just can't win the elusive world title.'

Undoubtedly his day will come and the Australian billiards passion will produce more world champions, for the boom in the production of billiard tables has returned. No longer are they being converted into urinals. One would hesitate to say that the era has returned when a gentleman should possess both a billiard table and a grand piano, but most hotels now have at least a pool table.

And once again it is not uncommon to see a billiard table in the homes of the affluent, and once again architects are being asked to design houses around billiard tables. Henry Upton Alcock would have approved; at last his game was beginning to conquer the tedious monster of television.
