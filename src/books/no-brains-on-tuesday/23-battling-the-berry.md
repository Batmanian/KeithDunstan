title: Battling the Berry
date: 1991-01-01
summary: A lament over blackberries engulfing Keith Dunstan's farmlet, relief at the blackberry rust being tested at the Werribee research station, and a counter-proposal that Von Mueller's bust in the Botanic Gardens be joined by busts to the men who introduced mynas, sparrows, rabbits, foxes and cane toads.

For my sins I have a little farmlet. It doesn't produce much but it is fantastic at making blackberries.

Of the two acres almost half has been covered with blackberry.

There's just nothing to the problem of the blackberry. According to my handbook one bush can spread up to six metres in a year.

It can spread through seeds, through cuttings or it can run right over the ground and come up somewhere again.

The only way to get rid of blackberry is to dig it out root by root. There is also a spray with connotations of John Wayne and Ronald Reagan. It is called 'Roundup'. The stuff comes in a tin container which sells for $15. Apparently it does no harm to the soil.

The tall purple spot has started to appear on some of my blackberries, and despite its piratical behaviour, I have a feeling we could learn to be friends.

Elegio Bruzzese at the Keith Turnbull Research Station in Werribee, the expert in this field, says there are eight different varieties of blackberry. The rust is effective on six of them and very virulent on three. It has been available for some time in France and South America but it takes five years or more to push a disease through all the tests to make sure it doesn't wipe out everything in sight. So far he has tested 108 different anti-blackberry species, which has taken four and a half years. He says that they have applied for Government approval, but it all takes time. In Tasmania there is opposition because there are farmers down there who grow blackberries commercially.

What better than the blackberry. Legend has it that sweet blackberries were originally introduced by men who settled in Victoria. According to Eric Rolls in his excellent book *See How They Run* the blackberry was first imported from England and was first grown in the 1840s.

He quotes the sheer delight of an 1843 Tasmanian blackberry planting: 'I watered them! I watched them! I weeded them with all the tender care of a nursery man and had the unspeakable pleasure of seeing them push forth in all their majestic luxuriance, waving their arms, throwing out long arms in every direction.'

I appreciate exactly what he meant, but I bet his long arms didn't catch my scratch marks.

All of which reminds me of one of the grand gestures of Victoria's 150th celebrations. Baron Ferdinand Von Mueller had to be remembered. So we erected a bronze bust to him in Melbourne's Botanic Gardens.

Now Von Mueller was a great scientist, explorer, world-renowned botanist, founder of the Melbourne Botanic Gardens and a superb eccentric. He had an uncommon disregard for the passing of time, never carried a watch and gave remarkably long speeches.

But as I burn my funeral pyre of blackberry, I think about others.

The Acclimatisation Society started importing the Indian mynas and sparrows for market gardeners. In Melbourne you can hardly see anything else but mynas, sparrows and starlings. Eric Rolls thinks they might have been imported by Mr Brown the bird dealer in 1857. O.K., let's have a bust to Mr Brown.

We could have a bust to Thomas Austin of Barwon Park who nurtured 24 English rabbits in 1859 and did so, one gathers, with quite some success.

There seems to be a little doubt as to who first imported foxes and deer, but Thomas Chirnside who built Werribee Park did marvellous things for the Melbourne Hunt, providing foxes, deer, hares and rabbits. He almost deserves several busts.

Then how about busts for the characters who introduced the cane toad, the prickly pear, Patterson's Curse, St John's Wort, Oxalis, Bathurst Burr, Boneseed and I am sure there are lots of others.

We could have a gallery of busts which could become one of Melbourne's intriguing tourist attractions.
