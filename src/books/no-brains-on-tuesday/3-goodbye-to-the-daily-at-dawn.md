Goodbye to the Daily at Dawn

The new creature, the *Herald Sun News Pictorial*, arrived on our doorstep yesterday morning.

There were mixed feelings. First sadness, an odor of death in the nostrils. Two newspapers not one had died in Flinders Street. Then there was irritation.

Where was the old chook on the masthead? Where was that famous rooster standing before the rising sun and the slogan 'Daily at Dawn'?

Gone, that's what. And that title HERALD SUN. Why not SUN HERALD? As long as I can remember the employees at the Sun Pic had a feeling of deprivation. The Herald considered itself the elder brother, the intellectual superior and *Herald* men received all the perks.

The *Sun* didn't even rate in the company title. It was always the Herald & Weekly Times Ltd, but almost from the time of its birth it was the lusty child, the *Sun News Pictorial*, who made all the money and supported the parent.

Now even in death the *Herald* is still lording it over the *Sun*.

Please forgive me for talking this way because I was practically born in that Flinders Street building. My father, William Dunstan, was General Manager of the Herald & Weekly Times Ltd. He adored the place, but not so much journalists; he loved the machine room and production staff.

His idea of heaven was to go down to the machine room just before noon when the first edition of the Herald was going to press. He would listen to the huge DRUM. . . .DRUM. . . .DRUM. . . .DRUM. . .DRUM as the presses began to roll. He didn't think much of Beethoven at all, but he thought the sound of a Goss printing press in full cry was the most perfect sound on earth.

From the age of 12, in school holidays I used to hang around Flinders Street pleading to be taken out with photographers or best of all, out on deliveries with Herald trucks. The Argus had brought out its new evening tabloid, the *Star*.

Sir Keith Murdoch, Rupert's father, had given orders that the *Star* must not be allowed to survive and it did not. One of the tactics was to make sure the *Herald* was always delivered ahead of its rival. Herald drivers were on bonuses if they won the race.

So the race down the Nepean Highway to Sorrento or the Geelong Road to Geelong was more exciting than anything that goes on at Calder. A kindly driver, Mr Milling, used to take me with him. At newsagents along the way there was no time to stop, we just hurled the bundles of *Heralds* out the back.

There were some very regrettable incidents and repercussions when ladies on the footpaths were hit by big bundles of City Extras.

I started as a cadet in 1946 and immediately I had to meet the extraordinary Sir Keith Murdoch. He insisted on interviewing all new cadets himself and furthermore he kept track of them and remembered their names.

One my assignments in the first month was to go through the *Sun*, the *Age* and the *Argus*, count the number of news stories in each, then list any that were not in the *Sun News Pictorial*. If the *Sun* was short on any good stories Sir Keith wanted to know why.

Bylines were almost unknown. Only the very great, Mr Harrison Owen, Mr Clive Turnbull, Mr John Hetherington, Mr Allan Dawes or Mr Rohan Rivett received bylines. We called our seniors Mister, no bandying round of Christian names in those days. Furthermore Sir Keith insisted every reporter had to wear a good suit, tie and a hat. How could a reporter be taken seriously as a gentleman without a hat.

The *Sun's* leader writer was Mr Harrison Owen. Harrison Owen had a Max Beerbohm *fin de siecle* air about him. Legend had that he wrote plays and some of them had even been staged in London's West End. Mr Owen did not like the radio and I was told he didn't own one. Radio with all those dreadful serials only softened the brain.

Not long after I started Mr Owen retired and the *Sun* dropped its leading article. The theory was that its readers should be able to make up their own minds on political matters. From then on the *Sun* only ran leaders at times of dire emergency or on the eve of elections.

There was nothing politically complicated about those leaders, in their entire history the *Herald* and the *Sun* had backed only one side of the political spectrum.

When I started the *Sun* editor was Mr Jack Waters, or Promising Jack as they called him. Jack was always putting his hand on your shoulder and promising you a glorious future.

But it was Jack who helped push the formula that made the *Sun* such a success in the forties and fifties. Pictures, above all, were important. Jack loved to get a good picture and run it over the entire front page. Good photographers, like Bert Rodda, George Bugden, Frank Tolra and Lloyd Brown were more important than reporters.

As for reporters, we were told over and over, the *Sun* was a family paper, nothing horrid that might upset the fair story would ever appear. We were to use simple words and a *Sun* story should not go beyond 12 paragraphs. The Second Coming of our Lord might be worth 25 paragraphs but only for one or two days.

Above all, the *Sun* had to be warm and human. Mr Waters introduced a bonus system for the best human interest or funniest story of the week. That was cut out of the paper and put it on the office notice board. That was worth an extra five pounds in one's pay envelope, as good as a week's salary. Winning that was better than riding a Cup winner.

Yet what surprises me particularly about those old days was the speed of production. When we were out on Court stories it was still possible to get results of cases into the *Herald* at 4.30 p.m. Those were the days of old fashioned hot metal. But come the days of computerised production everything seemed to slow down. If anything happened after 11 in the morning you were lucky to get it into the paper. I am not sure this is one of the reasons for the *Herald's* demise. No news.

If I could just mention this in very small print. In the 1940s I used to double as a football writer and I reported top league matches. There was a twin miracle every Saturday afternoon. All the racing and football news used to come into the *Herald* around 5 p.m. The final *Herald* would be on the streets at 5.30. The *Sporting Globe* was the same, and that was a true marvel, an entire newspaper was produced in half an hour with all the results produced accurately.

You might get away with errors in some parts of the paper but not on sport. Those papers disappeared and you wonder if they could be repeated now with all our modern technology.

I wish the brand *Herald Sun Pictorial* every kind of success, but I don't believe this 24 hour stuff. I used to buy the *Herald* every day, I wouldn't miss it. But I certainly wouldn't go to the newsagent to purchase another edition of what I already had in the morning.

I give the afternoon edition six months, the mid-morning three months and then it will be back to being a morning paper like all the others. But Mr Murdoch, kindly put the rooster back on top.
