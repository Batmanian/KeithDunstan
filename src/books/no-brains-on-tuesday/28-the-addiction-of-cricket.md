Could I make an instant confession. I am a cricket addict. We live 50 metres from a cricket ground.

There are all sorts of bird calls, magpies, wattle birds, kookaburras and cockatoos, but the most evocative, beautiful sound is that every ten minutes there is that wonderful shriek, more staccato, even more aboriginal than that of the sulphur-crested cockatoo, the cry of 'OWZAT'.

Our cricket ground is lovely. It is surrounded by stringybarks, a few pines and a small pavilion. There are no advertisements, no blinking scoreboards. Watching cricket at our ground is bliss.

We have our own hazards, the wicked tendency to bang the pot-hole which lies beyond the long-on boundary, and a good book will put the ball into a pond beside the bowling green.

The passion for cricket is something not easily understood. It is like skateboarding, or appreciating trios and unions, or even learning the violin: you have to be introduced to it at a very early age. As the Jesuits used to say, put the religion into them at tender years and you have them for life.

It is no good coming suddenly to cricket in one's twenties or thirties. It is quite beyond the adult mind. There are too many strange subtleties and mysteries.

The disease is common in men but relatively rare in women. Not enough women get cricket. I believe that the female mind, so used to child bearing and elementary survival instinct, is too practical and pragmatic.

For example, the non-addict's tolerance of a cricket broadcast. My tolerance extends to the full length of a Test Match. I like to hear every ball bowled.

But occasionally, say every hour or so, you get a nuance, a subtlety towards a climax. Again, like a symphony. The Test Match never comes to a mundane end and that is the beauty of cricket: it doesn't matter if a Test Match does not provide a result. You adore it because it is there.

It is difficult for the rest of the house to live with addicts. Non-addicts find all things inconsiderate. There is nothing worse than talking to an addict who is tuned in and hears nothing but the game.

The ear plug is a useful device but somewhat uncomfortable in bed. When you turn over you are inclined to get throttled. It is also a danger. My most regrettable episode took place when Colin McDonald and Jim Burke were still batting.

It was 2.30 a.m. when I re-awakened after a lapse of more than three hours. The broadcast was still going. Australia, 4 for 100.

The most exciting time for me is the beginning of a Test Match, the very first over. This is so overpowering I get quite sick in the stomach. I like seeing the fast bowlers preparing for battle.

It was Ray Lindwall who invented psychological warfare. He did it to scare the hell out of Len Hutton. Ray before taking the ball would do little runs up and down the crease, do knee bends and on one occasion, I think, he even did push-ups.

Then he would take an unconscionable time measuring out his run-up, putting down a marker, finally eyeballing his victim. It was always a most impressive performance.

Fast bowlers exciting? Well yes, but for true excitement you have to go to leg-spinners. Australia was always the breeding ground for the greatest leg-spin bowlers.

Remember Grimmett bowling. His action recalls the ancient round-arm worthies, recalls cricket on the sands with a walking stick for a wicket. To watch Grimmett bowl something so beautifully slow is to witness an artistry which would not disgrace the most committed painter.

There have been ugly suggestions that Pyjama Cricket will eventually replace Test matches. We must fight against this. The National Trust must have Bradman classified and preserved.
