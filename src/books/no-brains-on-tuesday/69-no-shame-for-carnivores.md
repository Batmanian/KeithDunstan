Have you noticed, it is all over? The shame is gone. Quite all right, at least for several weeks, to be carnivorous.

I know what happens at our place. We believe all the scientific statements and all the advertising campaigns. We always have.

We are spellbound by that TV ad depicting the boneless leg of lamb. Out comes a carving knife and zap, zap, zap, a fine hunk of lamb is all sliced up before you can 'Baa'. The thought comes to mind that through a miracle of genetics, the accommodating sheep industry has produced a lamb for carnivore humans that possesses no bones at all.

Then we get an advertisement which shows a pair of scales. On one side there is a hunk of chicken, on the other a hunk of beef. The scales magically tip proving somehow that the much maligned beef is lower in fat, lower in cholesterol, much more filled with energy and succulent goodness than the chicken.

But the one we like best is the flirty young hussy with the telephone hanging on her ear. That telephone never stops ringing with admirers. But it is no problem. She has this menu box full of short cut recipes. Fast as you can bat a steak knife she can whip up a marvellous ragout, noisette, kebab or whatever, full of macho, male nourishing, heart-perpetuating goodness.

Oh, we have been through all the phases. Remember 20 years ago it was the fashion to buy a deep freeze bigger than a bath tub. They were so big you kept them on the back veranda or in the garage. We couldn't afford one, but they were all the go out our way. The theory was you made fantastic savings by buying your meat in bulk, half a sheep or half an ox at the one time.

You can imagine the dedication with which our neighbours chomped their way resolutely through almost an entire animal. There was the occasional panic when the deep freeze broke down and there were para-medic dashes with wing ribs, legs and various pieces of frozen sheep cadaver to other friends whose deep freezes were still healthy and throbbing. I often wonder what happened to all those deep freezes.

Then we had the full assault of Time, Reader's Digest, Vogue, Women's Weekly, New Idea; they all told us red meat would kill us. Naturally at our place we kept up with every campaign.

We were amongst the first to succumb to Pritikin. Everything went white, chicken, and all the oil-less salad, accompanied very carefully with one glass of white wine.

Dinner parties were hell. I mean you had to ring round tactfully and find out who was vegan, who was Pritikin, who was quiche-prone, who was free range, who ate only natural unsprayed fruit. As for red meat, that was not done, terribly untrendy. You could almost pause for two minutes silence in deep sorrow for all the marvellous executive gas powered Webers that were sitting out there untouched on exquisite enamelled patios, while mother sweated over her Chili Bean Casserole, Aubergine Parmesan, her Vegetable Pasty with the wholemeal pastry, her Spiced Lentil Flan or her home made Pasta Parmigiana.

Yes, we have been vegetarian, organic, macrobiotic, high fibre. We notice too that our local grocer even sells 'wholemeal eggs'. Presumably you get bits of grit even in the yokes.

But now I think it is all over. You are reasonably free. It is trendy once more to be a traditional Australian carnivore and all the butchers are going berserk. There was a time when a skilled gourmet could go out to dinner and instantly pick the recipes. Mmmm, very nice; Elizabeth David. Like that; Constance Spry. Delicious; Beverly Sutherland Smith. Mmmm, Peter Russell-Clarke, last Tuesday night on ABV2.

Now you pick your butcher. Mmm Mongolian Roast; Jim, Toorak. Ahh Sourabaya Satay; Alf, Vaucluse. That meat sauce on the pasta, wait a minute, Bruno in Carlton.

Our local butchers are delighted with the new mood, but they admit if everything is not prepared, ready to go. Jim, for example, says one time he sold acres of stewing steak. Not any more, it all has to be cut ready to go.

His pepper steak is all peppered, his schnitzel is all schnitzed, his stroganoff is strogged ready to heat, his Mongolian Lamb, if not straight from Mongolia, looks like it, and his Hungarian goulash looks superbly Magyar.

Reiner, who hails from Germany, is always racking his brain for new recipes. His marinades are inspired. He has lamb spare ribs, marinated spare ribs, marinated beef with a black bean sauce, his pork is utterly fatless, he has sweet and sour, and he has Chinese Chicken, chicken olives, meats delicious stuffed and wrapped in bacon.

Reiner says a butcher these times is a chef, a creative cook, everything. He says you wouldn't believe the work involved: the day starts at 4.30 a.m. with him and his wife, cutting, slicing, preparing, marinating, and they finish, cleaning the shop at 7 p.m. They also have to work on Saturdays.

He says you still sell old fashioned joints at the week-ends, but during the week with husbands and wives all working, the prepared meats outsell the others 10 to one.

So we are eating meat again this week. Tonight we might have the boneless lamb seasoned with the apricot, sultana and honey mix or the Javanese lamb satay with the crushed nuts peanut sauce.

I think we had better get into it quickly, because there is sure to be a terrible backlash from all this. Any time from now we will be told that the only thing that doesn't kill you is dried, free range, fibre-rich, salt-free, un-sugared, organic, un-sprayed prunes with fat reduced yoghurt.
