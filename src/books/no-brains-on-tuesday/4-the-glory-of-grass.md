The Glory of Grass

The truth is I am deeply fond of grass and this is a last ditch appeal in its favor.

Not the ghastly hash kind, but honest, lovely old fashioned green grass.

Now if you were watching Wimbledon or television you would have noticed the glory of the grass tennis courts. Whenever things got dull they gave us this panorama of grass activity going on for ever and ever.

You will have noticed further that Australians with their deep grass heritage always do better at Wimbledon. Frequently many of them get past the second round.

But with the advent of the National Tennis Stadium at Melbourne's Jolimont the tennis championships are played on a horrid surface called Plexipave which is heat absorbing and guaranteed on a hot day to barbecue players like a leg of lamb.

This means that grass tennis is virtually finished in this country, our grand slam tournament is plexipaved yet we still have an organisation which laughably calls itself the Lawn Tennis Association of Australia.

This, of course, is tragic. When I was a small boy, back in the days of Bromwich, Vivian McGrath and Adrian Quist - Good Grief - I can even remember Jack Crawford when he wore long whites - yes, back in those days there were grass courts scattered everywhere through the suburbs.

We had several well-off friends with grass courts, and we would assist with the mowing and rolling on Friday evenings. The smell of fresh cut grass was one of those divinely created odours perhaps second only to fresh ground coffee.

On hot days there was nothing to beat grass. You could play in your bare feet and feel the cool grass between your toes.

And there was a marvellous charm about grass. We had a wonky machine which laid down the chalk lines, so that with the passing of the years the lines got wider and wider. Also you were never precisely sure of the size of the court. As for a bad line call, a ball could pick up a flash of white for a bad line call. Over 20 years of line laying, a chalky line could be anything up to a yard wide.

Just after World War 2 the developers discovered that a tennis court was just space enough for erecting four units of Own Your Owns. So the lovely grass courts we knew became sinister blocks with names like Beverly Mews or Buckingham Close. Close always meant too damned close to your neighbors.

This murder of the domestic court was the beginning of the end for Australia as a world tennis power. *En tout cas* those courts which didn't go under brick and concrete went under gravel.

But the Americans wreaked even greater havoc. I think it was on the second or the third day that Jehovah created grass. But in the early 1970s the Americans decided he had done a bad design job.

Grass had to be watered, fertilised, weeded, nurtured. It was hideously slippery in the wet and tended to tear under running feet. So they invented hard wearing plastic grass which did not have to be watered and when damaged could be replaced precisely like carpet.

It took over in baseball stadiums, football stadiums and (shudder) in some areas they even used it for bowls. Synthetic grass, plastic, rubber, took over everywhere. Grass is still available in some parks but I have the feeling it is discredited and it may disappear altogether in the United States.

There are no major tennis tournaments on grass in the U.S., none in Europe and soon Wimbledon will be the last repository of grass left on earth, a curiosity, a sight to see like the Elgin Marbles or Stonehenge.

I think the LTAA is showing an incredible lack of foresight. We should be putting in more grass courts, not taking them away. At least when Davis Cup matches are played on grass we have a better chance of winning. I mean, the last time we won the Davis Cup was at Kooyong on grass and that curious Jeohovah made substance confound the Swedes.

I think the National Trust, the Historic Buildings Council, the Ministry for the Environment, even Bob Hawke, should step in now and declare grass a sacred surface, before it is too late.

We do have one or two small pockets where grass tennis is still played. Kooyong, nigh forgotten, battles on bravely with grass. Meantime, unless the LTAA is prepared to act sensibly with an eye to the future of Oz tennis, it should change its name to the Plexi Tennis Association of Australia. Or even the Perplexed.
