Now I wonder if there are many S.A.P. members who are enduring the same problems. Perhaps we could band together, and open a market. Now what would be the right name? I have looked up the opposite of 'Super' in my thesaurus and come up with mini, bantam, dainty, diminutive, dwarfish, lilliputian, micro, minuscule, minute, pygmy, tiny and wee.

How do you think The Wee Market would go? We would have our morning suited greeter, no piped music, a counter between customers and goods, chairs, kindly sales people, and I think we would have one of those marvellous sales devices where tubes go zinging off on wires to a central cash lady.

We would make such a fortune inevitably Coles Myer, David Jones and all, would want to take it over.

Oh dear, we would like to be good, pure Suzuki people but the withdrawal pains are awful. It is not easy making an immediate switch back to the Stone Age.

Last week the Sunday Age printed the 10 golden rules for survival according to the greenie prophet, Dr David Suzuki. Unless we followed his golden rules the planet would be in big trouble.

1 Reject all disposable products; lighters, pens, razors. That's easy, I use an electric razor and I have always found the disposable ballpoint pen an invention of the devil, a loathsome illegible thing. So I am the last human in Melbourne that uses a dear, old fountain pen.

2 Buy longlasting clothes and avoid the trendy and fashionable. Going well. My problem is that my clothes are so long lasting and so untrendy they have gone the full cycle and become fashionable again.

3 Team up with neighbours, buy in bulk to avoid unnecessary packaging. Is he kidding? You just end up with bigger and more packages. In my mother's day the local grocer sold everything in bulk, flour, rice, sugar, and even soap. Biscuits she bought loose from marvellous big containers, the size of kerosene tins. Now even our meat comes wrapped in plastic and the super market doles out steak and chops in plastic boxes as if they were pills. But we are trying, really; young Lotte our poodle is deliriously happy because we have given up canned dog food.

4 Go for compost and reduce your garbage to one small bin a month. That will take a revolution. I think our council really likes garbage. It gave us one of these gigantic tombstone bins on wheels. You feel you have failed as a citizen if you don't fill it weekly. Yes, we have a compost bin, but last week the horse next door put his head over the fence and ate the entire contents, chicken and meat bones. Result: the foxes came in and dug up the lot.

5 Recycle everything possible. O.K. The local church won't take our old newspapers or cardboard. Re-use all our jars, boxes, tins, paper and packaging. We started doing that and in two weeks we couldn't get the car into the garage and the place looked like Steptoe & Son, to say nothing of a threat from the rural fire brigade. But we are planning to do something decent and meaningful by returning to home brewing so that we can recycle all our bottles.

6 Refuse plastic bags when buying goods. We loathe the horrid things, but most of the super markets want you to use their bags, otherwise you buck their carefully organised get-rid-of-customers-quickly system. Grandfather always shopped with his own Gladstone bag. If the dear old gentleman were here today, the super market would see him as a potential shoplifter, fast as you lift a box of detergent.

7 Make it a rule if you are travelling less than 10 blocks, walk. Leave the car at home, use public transport. Nothing has changed since 30 years ago when I gave up public transport after the union kept leaving me on railway platforms. I took to the bicycle and found it infinitely more reliable.

8 Don't use paper napkins, towels, tissues or nappies. Done. The return to cotton is lovely. It is a step forward not having the remnants of tissues in the washing machine that you forgot to take out of our pockets. But we have yet to find an alternative to the plastic coverings in the fridge and the microwave.

9 Choose goods which have little or no packaging. Is there anything that's not packaged? At our hardware store all items are sealed in the hardest plastic known to science. Last week I bought a pair of secateurs and a torch with batteries. Both were sealed in plastic so hard it took me half an hour just to chisel them out of captivity.

10 Buy organic food. Probably could if I were prepared to get out the car and travel 20 kilometres, but that way I'd defeat the purpose by polluting the air in the process.

Tell your hardware store you want loose nails rather than boxed. He'd never do that, because that is the whole principle on which high speed cash and carry is based. And besides, how else can they sell the customer four times as much as he needs.

Dear Dr Suzuki, I have scored only three out of 10, but I'm trying, honestly I am.
