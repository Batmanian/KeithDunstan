*You know how we all have our Walter Mitty dreams?*

Mine was to acquire the ultimate wine palate. Perhaps you have attended one of these lunches or dinners run for would-be wine connoisseurs. All the bottles are masked, carefully wrapped in brown paper.

So the wine is poured into your glass and from your peerless knowledge, you are asked to identify it, naming the country of origin, the district, the grape variety, the year of its vintage, etc.

Always it is my dream to be invited to speak on the wine of the night, before this distinguished gathering of connoisseurs.

I hold up my glass by the stem, roll it between finger and thumb, sniff, lift it towards the light and say: 'Yes, Mr Chairman, I found the wine tonight particularly interesting and I am deeply grateful for this opportunity to taste it.' (I taste it again).

'Mmmm. My first thought was that this was a first growth, a great wine from Burgundy. But, of course, it isn't, and you have honoured us with something that is perhaps even better. I detect that distinctive honey smell on the nose, the brilliant amber in the colour, that smooth almost oily texture, which tells me it has to come from the Hunter.

'My almost computerised memory bank of tastes tells me even further it is a Lindeman's, undoubtedly the semillon grapes came from that superb and famous producer Alfred Bloggenheimer. I can read his careful use of oak and what with that just faint hint of raspberry in the after palate I detect that he picked the grapes from the home paddock on the left side. I have assessed the year. Ahhh, a dry year it was, lot of sunshine, 1969. I believe the grapes were picked on March the second. Thank-you, I am drinking it with great pleasure.'

The bottle is unmasked. 'Astounding! He is exactly right,' gasps the chairman. 'How did he do it?'

The truth is I have always been hopeless at this sort of thing. I have picked Minchinbury fizz as Veuve Cliquot champagne, Grange Hermitage as Jacob's Creek, named chardonnay for riesling, riesling for Gewurtztraminer, called Rutherglen sherry for dinkum Madeira; and retired red in the face not from the wine.

What's to be done? You can only begin to acquire a great palate by training like Robert de Castella. Instead of pounding a million hours out on the road, you have to put in a million hours at glass, never giving up, showing dedication and valour, going through bottle after bottle.

There is only one trouble with this program, you get to the stage where you only enjoy the high quality wines, and just lately the prices have all gone through the roof.

Did you notice all the full page Christmas ads from the wine houses? Australians allegedly are running about third amongst the world's greatest lovers of French champagne. You can't get a humble non vintage under $40 and a vintage will set you back nigh on $60. The latest Grange, 1983, is $58.99 a bottle or $690 a case. The best whites and reds from the Yarra Valley are all running at $25 or $30 a bottle.

Then if you like a Sauterne, Ye Gods, just a simple Chateau Guiraud 1983 is being offered down the road from our place at $75 a bottle.

I mean, you can't afford to train any more. We have to return to simple basics. I am offering a training course to all my friends. It is called De-palatisation. For example on Champagne, you start on Veuve Cliquot, Perignon and Bollinger. Second week you go to Salinger, to Yellowglen, to Seaview until finally you are enjoying Minchinbury and Great Western Imperial Reserve at $3.99 a bottle.

You do the same with the reds, work your way down through Browns, Chateau Tahbilk, Wolf Blass cabernet, Jacob's Creek, until you reach what appears to be your Nirvana; a Coolibah cask at $7.99 for four litres. There are other varieties at $6.99 a cask but my training here might take another three weeks. My de-palatisation program could save any true wine lover anything up to $5000 a year.

I am going to set up a consultancy and my rooms will be in one of these fancy new towers in Collins Street. The de-palatisation program will spread into many fields. I will run a course for golfers, particularly those who disappear four days a week or are never seen all week-end. I could start my de-golfing course with a short lecture from Greg Norman. He could show us how to put the golf ball down in the wrong spot and get disqualified.

We will have a de-Bridging course for those who are far-gone in Bridge. This will not be easy, for Bridge, like asthma, is a disease, marvellous for the doctors and the cure takes a very long time. The answer, I think, is to irritate your Bridge patients. Give them misdeals, always make sure they get poor hands. Then talk a lot during the game. If your patient, by some mischance, does get a good hand and has big six no trumps, think of some good conversation pointers. Just as the six-no-trumper is desperately pondering his/her strategy, say: 'Dear, don't you think this wood chipping in Tasmania is marvellous for the economy?' Or, 'We wouldn't be having all these troubles if only they had made Sir Joh prime minister. Do you think it's too late?' A little of this treatment and I think we can de-Bridge a patient very well.

The sweet-tooth creatures, lovers of Violet Crumbles, chocolates and jelly beans, we can put them through a course of de-palatisation. I would give them a special summer time feast. It is so easy to get the right stock; we could go to a milk bar and buy all the jaded, slightly grey material that they have had in their show case out of refrigeration. Two or three feeds of this and our sweet-tooth fans would be crying for a cleansing bran and yoghurt.

The round-the-bend cricket addict? I think I will force him or her to sit in front of a television set through an entire match, listening to the drone of talk on Channel Nine, taking in all the re-plays, plus all the commercials which come on at the end of every over and at every wicket fall. The student will be crying for mercy, asking even for old videos within three hours.

How do you de-palatise a football lover terminal case? Don't ask for too much too soon. I have been trying to do this for 23 years, and I think I'm on the point of giving up. There is no known cure.
