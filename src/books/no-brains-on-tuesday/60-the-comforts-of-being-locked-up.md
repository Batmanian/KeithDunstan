---
title: The Comforts of Being Locked Up
date: 1991-01-01
summary: A survey of Melbourne's escalating home and car security, from being locked out on the footpath in dirty jeans looking like a suspected housebreaker, to a son-in-law trapped outside in his dressing gown, to a brand new car's anti-theft locks defeating the RACV and requiring a $70 locksmith's call-out at the beach.

It is almost time to join some good religious order and give all one's property to the poor. I don't think I can stand it much longer.

Last week-end I went out to put something in the rubbish tin. I heard the gentle click of the front gate behind me and I knew I was locked out, me outside, keys inside, nobody at home. Almost everybody has been robbed in our street. The folk next door have a fence three metres high, which is permanently locked. You can only communicate with those inside, via one of those security phones. You press the button and eventually a disembowelled voice comes from inside, from afar off, as if you were trying to communicate with the Reserve Bank at 3 a.m.

The security at our place is also magnificent. We have bars on the windows. The side fence goes to three metres with nasty wire on top. The doors and gates are all deadlocked.

So there I was outside and I knew there wasn't a chance of getting back in. It was two hours before my wife came home. So what could a man do? Nothing. No money in pockets to go down to the pub for a drink. All I could do was sit on the footpath by the fence and meditate.

I was in my dirty jeans, joggers, and favourite old polo neck sweater. As people walked past they looked at me strangely, you could see what they were thinking. 'What's he doing there? Horrid! Typical suspicious looking housebreaker. I wonder if I should call the police?'

Exactly the same thing happened several weeks ago to a son-in-law. He went out to the rubbish bin and the wind blew the front door shut. Trapped outside by the latest in magnificent double deadlocks, worth around $70 a time. He was in his dressing gown, bare feet, nothing on underneath. His wife had already left for the office. It was one of these double working households.

He had to walk a kilometre to our place seeking help, thinking all the time he would be picked up for being the local mystery rapist.

Two weeks ago I went to north-eastern Victoria and stayed with some friends. They kept their house as open as a tree. When they went out they didn't lock anything. 'Why bother, everyone's honest around here,' they said.

Not in Melbourne. We have put fancy deadlocks on everything that opens and finally it is starting to get to me. The theory is that you must have deadlocks because the thieves come round with furniture vans and take not only your video, but the lot.

So you've got to have a key to get in and you've got to have a key to get out. 'Some thief could break the glass, put his hand round, undo the lock and mug us all,' I say to my wife. 'You don't have it deadlocked all the time?' 'Yes you do,' she says. 'Not when we're inside.'

So every time we go out we have to find the keys. Or the postman's ringing on the front door with a registered parcel. We're not very well organised at our place. 'HAS ANYONE SEEN THE DAMN KEYS?'

But first you have to find your glasses before you can find the damn keys. By the time you have sorted all this out the postman has gone and you have to go to the post office to get the parcel.

We haven't bought a burglar alarm yet, but that is the next stage of lunacy. Everyone over there in Toorak has one. You have to remember to turn the things on and off. Very sophisticated they are. There are the types that just work when someone forces a window, but there are the fancy ones that work by sonar, or by breaking a beam of light, or if they are ultra fancy they pick up a rise in temperature caused by a human body.

In some streets you get a better sonic display than Big Ben, with alarms going off all day and night. Meanwhile ours has a sign in his front window: 'We have a burglar alarm, but don't worry. There's nothing left. This house has been robbed three times already.'

Cars, now that's another thing. We have had cars stolen twice before. We bought a new one last year. We didn't think too much about dream upholstery, digital dash, fuel injection or any of that nonsense. Good locking we were after. So we bought a car with superb electronic thief-proof locks, one turn, kerlunk, all locked.

Well, we were down at the seaside, parked in the tea tree. Time 3 pm. I was putting things away after the picnic, keys on the carpet of the lovely hatchback. As soon as I closed the boot I knew what I had done. 'Where are your keys?' I said to my wife. 'They're in my handbag...uh...in the car.'

We called the RACV. 'No trouble,' they said. 'We do this all the time. Average time for getting into a car; 30 seconds.'

Not with our car. After 45 minutes he said it was hopeless. 'The security was magnificent. Your only hope is to get a locksmith.'

It was 7 p.m. and after dark by the time the mobile locksmith arrived. He looked at our car under the tea tree, and leered: 'What have you two been doing? Necking?'

He could just see my bunch of keys on the floor of the boot by shining his torch through the hatchback. He worked out the design, went back to his mobile workshop, scratched and filed for over an hour. He came back again and again to the lock, and just when we thought he was going to give up:

'Click,' all the locks went free in a beautiful synchronised electronic motion.

'That will be $70 thank-you,' said he.

And he told us what a marvellous business he had. It always started at around 8 a.m., he got locked out of their offices. About 11 a.m. he got the domestic trade, housewives locked out of their houses, lost the keys at the super market, then the folk coming home in the evening. And it was getting better all the time.
