After being a barbarian for most of my life I am fascinated by the great education debate.

Last week a Senate Education Committee produced a highly critical report which suggested that many Australian graduates were poor communicators, unanalytical, dull, and unfamiliar in any disciplined society. The education battle has raged in the letters column ever since.

Oh dear, these suffering universities were producing narrow-minded, uncreative, unskilled barbarians.

Barbarians indeed. Of course, it has always been that way. I think I went through the education system without managing to learn anything that was useful. I had a friend who once remarked: 'My dear father impoverished himself sending me to boarding school. I often wonder what I learned that has helped me since.

'Now if only he had booked me into the Southern Cross or the Hotel Australia it would have cost him less and I would have learned infinitely more.'

This is a slight exaggeration. But we should not entirely blame our universities for the current lack of education. When I went to school I learned Latin and I learned French but finally I spent eight years studying French and when I finally arrived in Paris I would have startled Parisians. They wondered if I were speaking Icelandic.

I studied four different species of mathematics, which didn't help in the slightest in understanding an income tax return or working my way through a company prospectus.

I had a marvellous run-down on all the kings of Europe and the battles in which the Brits were triumphant. I learned a complete list of all the countries coloured red on the map, and knew exactly which peoples' histories belonged to King George. But other places, Asia for instance, now where was that? Really quite unimportant.

I would like to devise a new program for schools. There would be a course titled 'Practical Materialism'. You would learn all about the Stock Exchange, how to buy and sell shares, how to solve the mysteries of imputation, how not to get diddled investing in fly-by-night firms.

Indeed our VCE course would have to include a subject titled Advanced Form Filling: how to unravel income tax forms, medibank forms, driving licence forms, passport forms, tax lotto forms.

There would be a chapter on company history, the theory and practice of belligerent take-overs, and a revealing study on the theory and working of building societies. We could even offer a lectureship to one of our failed corporate cowboys.

We did learn to do the fox trot, the waltz and the tango when we were at school, but that was the end concerning social graces. Food and drink is such a vital part of our lives, there would be the Social Graces course. We would learn the subtleties of Italian, Chinese and French cuisine, how to read a menu, how to tip a waiter.

We would learn about alcohol, what it does to you and how to use it. We would study the grape, the difference between chardonnay and riesling, traminer and sauvignon blanc, shiraz and cabernet, and naturally we would provide a thorough working knowledge of all the great wine districts. We don't want our graduates to be terrified of a wine list.

Our course will make the students familiar with all the modern Australian writers, musicians and film-makers, but our non barbarians will also make superb husbands and wives.

There will be a special course on how to fix washing machines, dishwashers, ailing videos, and lawn mowers. Handy-Mannery will be a VCE subject with the chance of taking honours at the University. It is my belief that the cost of fixing things will become so enormous in the next decade none of us will be able to afford a handy man. This being the new era of communication, then perhaps we should also have a course on how to understand the instruction booklets for some of these extraordinary gadgets we are buying and can't fix.

The automobile is unquestionably the most dangerous drug discovered in the 20th century. Therefore it will be a major subject; driving, handling and social consequences. I have never had the slightest understanding of the mysteries that go on under the bonnet. We might even have a lecturer who was a former RACV repair man. He could teach us how to overcome all those embarrassing moments, when the internal combustion engine misbehaves.

Now sex would also be a major subject. We went through school learning nothing about human reproduction. Practical work would be a little tricky, but we should learn how the body works, about human behaviour, about AIDS, about transmitted diseases, yes, and a few hints on how to live with a partner for the rest of your life.

Religion? I am glad you asked that. Certainly we should continue with Christian education in schools, but let's broaden it to give instruction on the other great religions, with also some practical work on racial tolerance. We could learn, too, why so often love of God causes so much bloodshed.

I would want a course on Government, how Parliament works; Federal, State, local. I mean, if we can really learn how politics works, who knows, we might learn how to love and trust Bob Hawke. We might learn how to instruct politicians. We could certainly find out why we need so many of them.

As for the study of English I do believe we taught this much better 30 or 40 years ago. Perhaps it is not too late to resurrect the English language. If we can't teach them how to write a letter we might be able to instruct how to send a lovely fax.

Yes, it would be interesting also to have a course in semantics, then we could discover how they find such extraordinary lines as 'Multi Function Polis'.
