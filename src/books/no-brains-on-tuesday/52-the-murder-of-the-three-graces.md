Just before we went off on two weeks holiday this column raised a deep moral question.

What do you do when your chooks are past two years old, not laying with the correct machine-like verve. The experts say no longer are they an economic production unit. They are ripe for murder.

The text books, we found, gave all the utterly correct methods for execution. How to hold them when you put their heads on the chopping block, how to string them up by their legs to cut their throats, or, simply, the smooth even way to stretch them and break their necks. Yes, the books even tell you how to stop them running around headless.

We were so in love with our friendly faithful chooks, weak creatures that we are, we could not go through with it.

Fellow chook people, letters came in from all over. 'Gawd love ya', said Anna Marsden of Emana Park, Riddells Creek, 'At last we've found someone who's as potty about chooks as we are.' She referred to the Emana Park Chook Retirement Home and it is clear that, apart from crook financiers, Chook Geriatricism is one of the greatest problems facing this country. You wouldn't believe how many Eventide Chook Retirement Homes we are supporting.

Then there was another letter titled 'henpathy'. It came from Joan Sampson Tucker of Sandy Bay, Hobart. It was in the form of a nursery rhyme:

*Three Blind Mice!*

*We skipped and sang*
*biffing threes on the can*
*on the way to fetch milk*
*from the farm -*
*we were running to look*
*for the farmer's wife -*
*would she cut off our tails*
*with the carving knife?*
*Huh! we felt no real cause for alarm.*

*Three, Four...*
*We knocked at the door*
*and shouted but nobody came*
*so we peeped through the fence*
*in the terrible game*
*and witnessed the*

*Ring a Ring o' Roses!*
*A gruesome Dance of Death;*
*each brutalised fowl*
*wore a sugar-bag cowl*
*to stifle its last gurgled breath.*

*See How They Run!*
*We watched them all hurtle*
*around a red circle*
*unable to screech-out or cluck*
*till each feathered clown*
*with its starched-paper crown*
*fell down, bloody down on its luck.*

*He Diddle Diddle!*
*She stood in the middle*
*wiping the knife on her frock*
*- the murderess who laughed*
*till we hurled after*
*to see such fun - till*
*everything came to a stop.*

*Whee! Whee! All the way home*
*We went shocked*
*by the shriek in her laughter*
*then we hurled down the can.*
*How it rattled and rang!*
*And the questions came tumbling after.*

Now one has to report the sequel to our own chook saga. Off we drove on the Monday morning. The four beautiful white creatures had food stocks in depth and regular chook-sitter visits were arranged. We telephoned from Mollymook, New South Wales. It is incredible how children are always first to the phone, answered with the strident announcement of Grandson Tim, aged four: 'Your chickens are dead... Your chickens are dead.'

Tim was the first to discover the bloody scene. Hey Diddle Diddle, indeed. Ah dear, we used to call our chooks the String Quartet. They used to follow us around more devoted than children.

A fox had torn a neat hole in the wire, and wriggled into the cage. The scene of carnage was terrible. He bit the head off the first chook then delivered the swift coup de grace to the other three. White feathers everywhere, four stiff, inert bodies and blood.

Tim and his father quietly buried the String Quartet under the pittosporum tree at the top end of the garden. Young Tim, aged four, had his first lesson in the frailty of life.

But why? If the fox, that's if it was a fox, had killed for food you would understand, but none of the chooks were eaten. Two years earlier a dog entered our bottom paddock and savaged our two sheep; Petal was all lacerated and bleeding but Bottlebrush was so mutilated the vet had to shoot her.

Again, chasing those sheep around the paddock and tearing at their throats with sharp teeth was simply huge fun.

How often have we heard it said that humans are the only species that kill for the pure joy of killing. It isn't true. Hey Diddle Diddle.

Sequel 2: But you should never give up. It's like getting all the trainee pilots into the air immediately after an air crash. Confidence has to be restored. Therefore we have rebuilt the chicken cage, covered it in steel mesh, so that it is a virtual Alcatraz, and installed String Quartet Two.

They are four gorgeous White Leghorns. Rather nervous, yes, but just this morning, God bless them, they produced their first egg. It was only the size of a ping pong ball but it was the triumph of the week.

O Ring a Ring o' Roses!
