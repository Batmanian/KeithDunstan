Columns Allied to the Grape and Associated Beverages

It was John C. Brown, the master vigneron from Milawa, who put his hand on my shoulder and said: 'Keith, I will give you some very good advice, it is much easier to get it out of the bottle.'

That was three years ago. Foolishly I didn't take note. How could one resist the romance of it all, the wonder of being able to sample one's very own wine, to gaze at one's very own label.

We went ahead and planted 1200 *pinot noir* vines.

Then I remembered a line I once read in the Readers Digest: 'Farming always looks better through a car window.'

We hadn't realised that every vine virtually was a child. It had to be fed, watered, clipped and trained. Well, more than trained, it had to be trellised. I mean Toorak matrons get their hair dyed, teased, twisted, permed and perfumed by Lillian Frank. *It's* even worse with a grape vine. Every cane has to be trained, nurtured, fertilised, pinned, put into its proper position on the wire.

The tender little things also have to battle against weeds. *There are about 240 varieties of weeds all of which are available in our vineyard.* Their speed and vigor of growth is beyond belief and every one of them tries to smother your vine.

I remembered one year going to the Department of Agriculture display at the Royal Show. An Ag. scientist was there with a display of all the common weeds, he had them all growing there in boxes and he was watering them one by one, ragwort, oxalis, chickweed, patterson's curse, copeweed, bindy-eye, potato weed, nettles...

I said to him: 'Were they difficult to grow?'

He looked at me with sad eyes: 'Were they ever. When you want to grow weeds they are the very devil. The cranky things kept withering up and dying.'

I thought as much. Everything to do with agriculture is designed to be difficult. The next problem after weeds was diseases. All right, grape vines are subject to even more pests and diseases than two-year-old humans, all of which require sprays and come with awesome names like chlorpyrifos, dithianon, and chlorothalonil. And the cost? No wonder chemical companies are the richest in the world. The stuff invariably costs about six times as much as Johnny Walker Black Label.

All this is leading up to tell you about Black Monday. The agricultural experts said after the damp soggy Spring, conditions were perfect for almost everything horrid; it was vital at once to spray against black spot, downy mildew, mites and caterpillars. After that look out for powdering mildew and bunch rot.

I waited a week for the right conditions; when it wasn't raining there were 70 knot gale winds. All right, the forecast was good, so I got up at 5 a.m. before the gales got under way, did my mixing, filled the backpack spray and then sprayed with Delan to get rid of the diseases and Lorspan to get rid of the bugs. It was barely light when I started but brilliant sunshine when I finished.

I returned to the garage, started to return canisters to the chemical cupboard and noted something very strange — the square plastic bottle of Delan was still there and in my hand was an exactly similar square plastic bottle of Surflan. How could that be? The terrible truth began to seep into my brain.

Surflan is a pre-emergent spray for killing weeds. Theoretically it stops weeds before they get out of the ground. Ohhh! Noooooo! I rushed to the bedroom where my wife Marie was just getting up, and blurted:

'I have just done something absolutely terrible.'

'What, dear? Tell me about it.'

'I have just sprayed the entire vineyard with weed killer.'

That sank in, she said nothing, so I added what should have been the right cross.

'What's more, I thought I was doing the right thing. I sprayed all your lovely roses with the same stuff.'

She should have committed homicide at that point, but she was marvellous. She took over like General Eisenhower organising the Second Front. 'Don't worry about it,' she said, 'there has to be an answer.'

Soon she was organising. We pulled out every hose we could find. She called up friends, good neighbours, fellow-vine growers, who came round with knapsack sprays. How could you exist without good friends. For three hours we hosed and sprayed down every leaf.

But even then work wasn't done. I hadn't disposed of the enemy. I had to spray with the chemicals I should have sprayed with in the first place.

That took the rest of the day.

All this happened three weeks ago, roughly the period it should take for a decent weed killer to destroy the lot. Well, all 1200 vines have survived. Indeed they have taken on the most extraordinary luxuriant growth and look very beautiful indeed. They look particularly lovely in the late evening as we gaze through the living room window and the western sun is shining through the leaves.

As for the roses, White Wings, Chianti, Marguerite, Tuscany Superb and Alba Maxima, have never been more prolific. They just keep on flowering.

Grapes are forming. All we have to do is cope with the next problem. I mentioned General Eisenhower. O.K. it has been a remarkable season for birds. Word has got around, rosellas, sulphur crested cockatoos, silver eyes, starlings, every conceivable type of bird, brown, black and brindle is gathering forces now like Rommel's Panzer divisions waiting for the feast. Many of them have even built nests under our eaves, bivouacs waiting for action.

Will any grapes remain? I have tried to work out eventual production costs. I think it will be around $100 a bottle.

But I am very worried about next season. I will have to get rid of those chemicals. Do you think it would be all right to plant garlic and place a religious symbol between each vine?
