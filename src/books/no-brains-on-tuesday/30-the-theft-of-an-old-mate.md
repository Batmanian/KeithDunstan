Columns on Lesser Muscular Activities

Some creature stole my bicycle last week. Champion cyclists were giving speed demonstrations in the City Square not more than 20 metres away.

So typical, don't you think? It was Bicycle Week.

My bicycle was chained to a lamp post outside Capitol House in Swanston Street. I couldn't have been more than 10 minutes.

When I returned it was gone, bicycle, chain, lock and all. You know how it is. First puzzlement. I'm pretty careful. I did leave it there didn't I? Then stunned disbelief.

I got down on my hands and knees and started studying the empty stretch of pavement. How could anyone take it?

Actually I knew darned well how they could take it. Alan Parker, president of the Bicycle Institute, had been warning about professional bicycle thieves. There were teams of them on the move. They used bolt cutters which could go through almost any chain or lock faster than you could bite a peanut.

Alan Parker says Victoria has very nearly the highest bike theft rate in the world — an estimated 35,841 went off last year, 940 per 100,000 of the population. New South Wales lost 32,766.

Oh yes, if you leave your bike in one of our densest cities your chances of having it stolen in any one year are about even money.

So maybe I'm lucky. This is the first time it has happened since I started commuting to town by bike 25 years ago.

I guess it wasn't the best bicycle in the world. Not a $2300 clean job made out of metal they invented for going to the moon. It was a Malvern Star and our relationship couldn't have been sweeter.

Funny that, you understand each other's idiosyncrasies. Except for a flat tyre here and there, no break downs. If you tried to compare it with the reliability of the railways or any form of public transport, you have to start laughing.

It took 22 minutes door to door, 10 kilometres from the city. But going home in the tram was perfectly horrible. You know how it is, sitting in a tram, with helmet on one's knee, absolutely dependent on others for transport.

I pedalled via parks, along the Yarra. The whole experience was magic. Before I had looked across to work and back as absolutely the most horrid part of the day. Now it was the best.

And I would keep varying my route for fun. Particularly I liked going home through Carlton. How those streets smell. You get all the marvellous Greek and Italian quarters of Melbourne smells. You don't get cooking smells in a car with all your windows up and the air conditioning on.

If you don't ride a bicycle yourself it is hard to explain exactly what all this means. You get very well acquainted with each other, you understand each other's strange quirks and angles. Such a relationship takes years to develop.

I used to have a friend from Alabama, an old aerospace pilot but an out-and-out bike nut. Marvin used to say: 'The most personal failure of a bike is like wearin' somebodah else's teeth.'

Then, of course, losing your bike is bad enough, but losing your own saddle is a real grief. That's irreplaceable. Hubert Opperman once said: 'You may one day get a new bicycle but never never does a serious cyclist sell his saddle.'

A well-worn, infinitely softened and battered saddle, featuring mysterious curves adjusted for pelvic bones — that is quite something.

There is a story about that saddle. Back in 1976 I was riding my Malvern Star across part of the United States. There was a Chip Haines, a fellow traveller. He was suffering the same agonies as me with his saddle.

I said to him: 'Look, we can do worse. Let's swap saddles.'

The result was magic. My saddle suited his bottom, his saddle suited mine. The saddle had a strange name: the 'Cool Seat'. A few weeks after I returned home I wrote to the Cool Seat company extolling the wonders of the saddle and asking if they could send me another. Americans are wonderful. Three weeks later there was a parcel in the mail. 'All compliments' was the message. 'Our saddles never break.'

Alas, I don't think the Cool Seat company exists any more, so I can't get another. I have the prospect of infinite exquisite agonies ahead, the months and months of breaking in a new saddle.

Yes indeed, I have ordered a new bicycle. How could I live without one? Nothing could be worse than trying to get around the city on inferior modes of transport, unreliable trains in particular.

But as Marvin pointed out, you have to learn how to use each other. It will take six months to a year, however, before we fully understand and love each other. But how will I save it from marauding bike thieves?

Mr Parker recommended a U-type lock made out of a miracle metal impervious to bolt cutters or even tungsten saws. So I bought one. It is called the Kryptonite K4. Presumably it came from that planet on which Superman was born.

Good grief, the darned thing cost $75. Once upon a time you could get an entire bike for that kind of money.
