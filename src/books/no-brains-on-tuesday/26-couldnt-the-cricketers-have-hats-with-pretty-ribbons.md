---
title: Couldn't the Cricketers Have Hats With Pretty Ribbons?
date: 1991-01-01
summary: An argument against numbers on cricketers' backs in favour of returning to the 1862 convention of dove-coloured shirts with red spots and straw hats trimmed with magenta ribbon, with coloured ribbons on the arm to identify players and the dancing duck on the electronic scoreboard as the ultimate modern indignity.
tags:
  - Melbourne Cricket Ground
---

Have you read the appalling news? Cricketers in one-day internationals will sport numbers on their backs next summer.

It is in one's mind which is the worst: the lime green of Pakistan, those burgundy stripes coupled with the blue dodge-grey of the West Indies, or could it be, the red and yellow of South Australia. In full cry the South Australians look like some brand of indigenous parrot.

The coloured uniforms were really the invention of Packer cricket. Colour television had not been with us for very long, so there was the naive idea that to get the value out of your match you had to be filled with a kaleidoscope of brilliant hues.

During the 1987 season the Australian cricketers wore a series of horizontals. There was the brilliant yellow collar, a green band across the shoulders, then came four green and yellow stripes, next a wide band of white, then four more green and gold stripes, and finally a gold band which went down to the trousers, which were all yellow.

If a red ball was used during day cricket the yellow trousers set off most brilliantly the red marks when a fast bowler rubbed it on his trousers.

Now it will be fascinating to see how they will use the numbers on these extraordinary shirts. Remember, back in the old days when scorecards were more simple, kindly affairs, if you were a great player like Kippax or Trumper at the MCG you didn't need a scoreboard.

At the Melbourne Cricket Ground, for example, they had the blinking light system. On a lazy afternoon, when there were 40,000 outside, it gave you something to do. They only blinked the light for a fiftieth of a second, identifying each fieldsman.

Now with the electronic scoreboard they give the whole works, with four replays from different angles. If the batsman is out for a duck he gets the ultimate humiliation of a little dancing duck on the big screen.

It used to be the British style to have a sense of understatement, to use a little subtlety. Even the footballer only has numbers on their backs. This, of course, is to force the unofficial spectators into buying copies of the Football Record.

Let's go back to the first international cricket match. It was between Mr Stephenson's XI from England and the Melbourne and Districts XVIII. George Marshall was captain of the Melbourne and Districts team. His XVIII wore dove-coloured shirts with red spots and round trousers and a straw-coloured hat trimmed with magenta ribbon.

Mr Stephenson's XI wore white shirts with blue belts and white caps with blue stripes. It was the custom too, not to have an open-necked shirt, but to wear a rather charming cravat.

How was it possible to identify anyone? There was a different-coloured ribbon tied around the arm of each player.

I commend the whole system to the Australian Cricket Board. Instead of the terrible vulgarity of their present uniform I like the idea of straw-coloured shirts with red spots and dove-coloured hats trimmed with magenta ribbon.

And how could they go wrong with pretty coloured ribbons tied around the arm to identify the players. They would produce extra funds through the sale of score cards just as they did in 1862.

But of course, I'm with Bill O'Reilly and Ian Johnson. I have never believed in the one-day game whether they wear pyjamas with names on their backs or not. There is a glorious majesty about a Test Match.

Where else can you find something that takes five days to complete? It is like a Mahler symphony, with lots of opportunities for slowly moving into a climax of excitement.

Everything else is being speeded up, jammed into shorter and shorter spaces of time. Cricket should be, somewhat unfashionably, played without names on the backs.
