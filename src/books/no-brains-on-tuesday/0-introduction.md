Sometimes in my blackest moments I compare my perfidy with that of Ron Barassi. After 34 years with Melbourne's *Sun News-Pictorial* I ratted, I left to write a column for the *Age*.

Yet I was just a tiny figure in the town so my perfidy could never be compared with that of Ron Barassi. In 1964 Ron Barassi was captain and coach of Melbourne, generally described as the greatest Demon of them all. He was Mr Football, and Melbourne, of course, was the most distinguished club, it invented the game of Australian Rules.

In 1964 Ron Barassi defected to become captain and coach of Carlton. It is generally agreed everywhere that the defection of Ron Barassi was the greatest news story of any kind in Melbourne. Every little kid in Melbourne as soon as he can walk is given a football sweater, offering curious allegiance to some club. In 1964 three out of four of those sweaters were exact replicas of official Melbourne sweaters and they all sported 31 on the back, Mr Barassi's number. Can you begin to imagine the impact of Mr Barassi's defection when he went to Carlton?

At the time I received an avalanche of letters of complaint. It is a mystery why they came to me. However if you write a daily column in a large city then you become a sort of public wailing wall. I had a remarkable number of letters from budgie owners. One lady at Lilydale reported how patiently she taught her budgie to say, 'Come on, the Demons', 'Kick a goal, Barassi', and 'Good kick, Barassi'. My advice was that budgies could be taught only at an early age. It would have been impossible for those budgies to understand Barassi's switch of allegiance. One can only presume all those budgies had to be strangled.

I repeat I was only a miniscule figure in town compared with Mr Barassi, but it was tricky nevertheless. I virtually grew up in the *Sun News-Pictorial*. My father was the General Manager, and I wrote the column 'A Place in the Sun' from 1958 to 1984. There were several interruptions, but let me say I battled there for six days a week.

It was late 1985 when I received calls from Max Suich, chief executive of the James Fairfax & Son and Creighton Burns, editor of the *Age*. Would I write columns for the *Sydney Morning Herald* and the *Age*? It seemed that I had written everything I could possibly write for the dear old *Sun News-Pictorial*. The same stories, the same situations kept returning. I needed a change badly and this seemed a wonderful change of course, a new spur, now working for the *Sun* just as a contributor and besides, the enemy, Fairfax, were offering double the money.

So I committed the act of perfidy, I did a Barassi and defected. The first column for the *Age* and *Sydney Morning Herald* was in September and I was overcome with terrible guilt. What would my late departed father have thought? Would my mother have been ashamed? After all these years I had spurned the *alma mater*. Right in the depth of my miseries there was a telephone call. 'Mr Carlyon wants to speak to you,' said the voice. Les Carlyon was editor in chief of the Herald & Weekly Times Ltd. I waited for the caning.

He said: 'Keith, hey, I wanted to offer my congratulations. I think you have done the right thing. So this is a call to wish you well. You will find them marvellous people at the *Sydney Morning Herald* and the *Age*.'

It was one of the most timely telephone calls I ever received. But not all former colleagues were so kindly. I met a former chairman of the Herald & Weekly Times several weeks later. It was at a reception in the Arts Centre and Prince Charles was about to make a speech. The chairman looked at me sourly and said simply: 'I used to like you but I don't any more.'

So much for my perfidy. *No Brains on Tuesday* is a collection of articles most of them written for the *Age* on Tuesday. Many of them appeared in the *Sydney Morning Herald* and some appeared in the *Sunday Age*. The book is meant to be a small sequel to an autobiography, *No Brains At All*, published by Viking O'Neil in November 1990. The title was a remark made to me by a kindly science teacher at Geelong Grammar, Charlie Cameron. He tried as hard as any man could, to teach me the mysteries of chemistry. Finally he gave up in desperation and said: 'Dunstan you're stupid. You've no brains at all.'

It is a problem I have battled with ever since.

Writing a column is a tricky business. Inevitably you find you are always running on empty. In a way it is like being a dairy farmer, no matter how you feel, no matter where you are, no matter if you have a pain in your back, or a hangover from yesterday, those cows still have to be milked. Ah, but the difference between being a columnist and a dairy farmer is this: the dairy farmer usually can find his cows. Mostly they will even walk up to be milked. The columnist has many days when he has no idea where he will find his cows.

I thought that writing a weekly or bi-weekly column would be easier. It did not turn out that way. Instead of having crises six days a week there were two crises a week. Yet it is a very curious thing and I am sure other columnists have had the same experience. You go away on holidays and you think, 'Marvellous, I will have a whole bank of ideas when I return.'

It does not work out that way. A great deal of nonsense has been written about writer's block. It is the deadline, the fear of the axe, that gets rid of the block. The deadline forces the dull brain cells into action and makes one think up new angles. It is odd indeed but often the best columns are written in times of greatest desperation.

After leaving the *Sun News-Pictorial* we settled on the Mornington Peninsula. My office is a little bungalow out in the garden with a window overlooking a small vineyard. Once upon a time I invented an organisation called the Society Against Progress, which had the premise that nothing ever gets better, things only get worse. However, the invention of the microchip and the personal computer is an incredible exception. I have always been a gadget lover and even as early as 1979 I fell in love with the computer word processor. The computer made it possible to live permanently 90 kilometres from the office. I had only to hook up the modem to the telephone, my computer went into an embrace with big brother computer in Spencer Street, press a button, and by extraordinary black magic my column went on its way at 1200 words a minute.

So here are the columns which have been delivered that way. They have a certain emphasis on eating, drinking, domestic life, cricket and the agonies of creating a vineyard. One only hopes they have remained readable.
