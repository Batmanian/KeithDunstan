You will have noticed that people are 'into' things. They are into mud bricks, into weaving. Dear Reader, at our place we are into the most deadly disease of all, we are into bridge.

The boom has spread amongst all our friends, although I get the impression that some of them find the combination of fun and intellectual challenge slightly absurd. I, on the contrary, treat a bid like Lincoln at Gettysburg — with great gravity and eloquence.

There are various methods of study. There is a hideous little game called Auto Bridge. You feed cards into it and you have to play cards with dummy and two opponents provided by the machine. At the end of each game the machine explains how you made a fool of yourself.

My friend Hilton has a home computer. You can now purchase special software and the computer plays an impeccable game against you, like a world master, never hesitating, all decisions made in a fiftieth of a second.

The big problem is should you play bridge with your wife. This is a matter of some delicacy. Our bridge teachers, Jim and Norma Borin, both world masters, compete as a team. However, it needs to be a strong marriage. It is like teaching your wife to drive or playing as her partner in mixed doubles. If you can survive that your marriage has an excellent chance.

The major crisis comes when you doze off while she is going two no trumps, or when you renege by failing to follow suit.

On those appalling nights like Fridays and Saturdays when the TV stations provide programs designed only for people who have not seen the unendurable repeats for the 19th time, I get down to training.

We have two imaginary characters, Basil and Shirley. We sit up and I get along remarkably well with Shirley and we have very few disagreements. It seems my bidding has come on considerably because she always allows me to have a peep at her hand.

The ultimate act of courage is to go up to the local bridge school for the duplicate sessions. On duplicate nights there are prepared hands, and it is all done at high speed. Each hand must be got through in about seven minutes and after each one you change and play with another pair.

The stress is terrible. Not a word is spoken. The first time I went, I sat beside the lady who was playing East at my left. She was a deaf mute. She was writing down all the cards.

Then it was explained to me that this is utterly the done thing. Back in 1965 a lady was accused of cheating in international bridge by making signs to her partner. It became a long battle; the Dreyfus case was trifling by comparison.

Jim Borin reports that there were equally dramatic events several months ago at the North American national contests in Washington DC. Edith Rosenkrantz, one of the competitors and wife of the famous George Rosenkrantz, was kidnapped. Ultimately there was a dramatic rescue and the team went on to win the championship.

At the same time one team was accused of cheating and for the first time the investigators used video evidence to back up the charges.

Speaking of drama, there are bridge cruises all over the globe. Bridge on the QEI, 17 nights of bridge on the beloved Queen down the Mississippi to New Orleans; bridge with Sitmar to the Caribbean; bridge to Japan, Asia, South America, Scandinavia.

Then one always remembers the story about the bridge players who kept on playing when the Titanic went down. They didn't know it had sprung a leak.

I wonder what hands they had.
