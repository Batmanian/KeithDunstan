---
title: Rows and Rows of Roses
date: 1991-01-01
summary: "An account of catching the rose disease: 119 plants and still not enough, conversations conducted entirely to the roses rather than to each other, and the full cast of rose-named celebrities from Mrs Thatcher to Dolly Parton."
tags:
---

It is the height of the season and I fear we have caught another appalling gardening disease.

We have been through this before: we have been through the dahlia disease. They had blooms as big as rubbish bin lids and the idea was to have them nodding over the front fence in order to stun the neighbours.

Then we went into azaleas, rhododendrons and there was a brief bout with fuchsias. During that flood of nationalism, when Bondy won the America's Cup it was essential to be non-Australian native. We put the place into melaleucas, grevillias, callistemons, and went mad for all sorts of darned things I can't spell.

Mid eighties was English cottage garden. Sensible garden that is not there if you didn't have a picket fence. The garden had to be a perfect display of phlox, delphiniums, hollyhocks, candytuft, cosmos with wafting summer smells of lavender. And hanging baskets! You couldn't walk around the house without crashing your head on a great suspended wire cage of petunias.

Now roses are all the go and those who have been touched will understand. They have a peculiar, possessive, totally seductive entanglement.

Every morning we drain petals off the dining room table onto the carpet.

'Yes, it is... When my wife is feeling a little blue she says I think I will go out and buy a rose.'

'You can't buy any more roses. We have 119 now. There isn't room for them all.'

'Yes there is. There's always room for another rose. We can move something.'

In our garden there are more moves and reshuffles than in the British Cabinet. 'I have just the spot,' she continued.

'Where?'

'The corner of the house by the gas bottles. I'll put in a climbing iceberg.'

Ye Gods, there is no point in reasoning with genuine rose people. In the end we had to use railway sleepers, and we have more icebergs than in the entire Antarctic.

You have to understand what they are talking about. They tend to get on first name terms. 'I am a bit worried about Papa Meilland who is suffering from Black Spot. There's the entire Meilland family. There's Michele Meilland, Sonia Meilland, Manou Meilland...'

On the other hand they might say: 'I have to go out and put some water on Derek. And Maggie needs tying up.'

You must appreciate Derek Nimmo and Mrs Thatcher both have had roses named after them. Mrs Thatcher is a particularly vibrant, vigorous rose.

There are those among the bitten: Bette Davis, Judy Garland and definitely Dolly Parton. Dolly is a beautiful red rose, a voluptuous pinky red.

Addicts are beyond all rose recognition. They can remember a rose they met six years ago. We had a group in last week. They didn't greet each other at all; they just talked to the roses.

'Did you ever see anything like Just Joey?'

'I think Souvenir de la Malmaison is absolutely my favourite. I'd like to grow nothing else.'

You can recognise a rose grower by the wrinkles and scratches up and down the arms; so much to be done outside there is no time left for what goes on inside.

My wife says: 'I will just slip outside for two minutes. I have to pick a rose.' She returns four hours later. You can't walk past a rose. You have to do something to it.

The craze for old-fashioned roses, like in the pioneering days, is particularly strong. We want English roses, we want things to climb. We want the great ramblers.
