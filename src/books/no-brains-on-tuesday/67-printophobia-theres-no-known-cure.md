At 7 pm during the week-end we cracked.

We turned off the news. 'Don't they ever broadcast anything that is even faintly bright? Do we have to keep on living with disaster?' said my wife.

It was true. It had been a very disagreeable week. Medical reports on the state of the dollar continued to worsen. The Commonwealth Games were in disarray, our wheat market was in ruins and our relations with the United States were in such a bad state we might even have to withdraw Greg Norman. What's more, we managed to turn the weather into a disaster, making it the worst for 40 years.

Actually I have always suffered from a disease called Printophobia. This is a fear of large print and banner headlines. Nothing good ever came out of a large headline. My printophobia was originally caused by newspapers like London's Daily Mirror which always carried headlines with letters larger than cigarette packets.

You know for a fact the bigger the print the more horrid the news. Inside stories avoiding the shock horror in the big print. So at times I have arrived at the office in a state of happy innocence. Then an editor has said something like this.

'Terrible about the Queen in the paper.'

'I didn't see anything about the Queen in the paper.'

'You're joking?'

'What's happened?'

'Geez mate, it was the front page lead. What did you do this morning, use the paper for fish and chips?'

'Aw, you can't read everything.'

Long sigh. 'I'll tell you. The Queen is fed up with Prince Phillip and she's run off with the President of Nigeria. Mrs Thatcher's livid, and it has been condemned both by the Archbishop of Canterbury and the Roman Catholic Bishop of London. Except Bill Hayden thinks it might be good for the Commonwealth.'

That's a somewhat bizarre example, but in these bad days I always approach the newspaper gingerly, in a somewhat odd manner. I begin on the back page. You get terrible news there too, but it is not quite so serious.

I check to see whether Dean Jones has made another century, whether Ian Botham has been forgiven yet, whether an Australian tennis player has magnificently survived into the second round, and which football teams we are exporting to friendly consenting States.

Next I look at the comic strips, take in the cartoons, particularly window box jobs like Tandberg and my old mate Jeff. I check to see whether there are any marvellously absurd letters to the editor then maybe I am sufficiently sanitized to face up to some hard news.

However, we get three newspapers so one has to be selective. I don't read any horror stories about footballers or coaches that have been taken out and shot. I steer clear of crime, bank robberies, murders and rapes. I skim prognostications by economists at Westpac, National Australia Bank, or various universities. My faith in their predictions on interest rates and the future of the economy died back around 1976.

Police court stories I rarely touch. Inside stories about the royal family, what they are wearing, how they often fall off their horses, palace romps and such, can be quickly avoided.

Then there are stories that suffer from the agony of over exposure, like Norm Gallagher's revival of the BLF, the state of the waterfront and the almost daily popularity ratings of Hawke, Hewson, Kirner and Keating. Surely we need to know how much we like them only once every six months.

I can skip tales about rock stars, Molly Meldrum, George Crawford, Susan Renouf, Neville Wran, the loves of Jacqui Onassis and tales about all the various vessels that will be performing in the America's Cup.

One has to continue reading lugubrious stories about Murdoch, Bond, Bell Resources, and all the various State Banks. There is a morbid fascination in the news of the razor activity in Canberra and the eternal disaster of the balance of payments.

But I don't know how to solve my printophobia. There have been many attempts to produce newspapers that carry only happy news. Such an attempt was made in the United States as recently as last year. It lasted only six months.

The truth is that human beings adore horrid news. They like horrid news particularly if it is happening to other people. Ask any editor. News about vile events sell much better than those about sweetness and happiness.

The time that puts editors in a state of terror is Christmas and the silly season. In the profession editors look back upon their favourite Christmases. They remember particularly with love and affection the Bogle murders. They were never solved and dragged on beautifully until Parliament and the courts were going again with their rich vein of awfulness.

Then there was the Christmas that Harold Holt was drowned at Portsea. Sales and public readership reached a record high that awful Yuletide.

If the dollar goes to 40 cents, if the Queen runs off with the president of Nigeria, if we break off relations with the United States, if Greg Norman abandons golf to play ping pong, if the Collingwood Football Club moved to Townsville, ah now, that would make a super silly season.
