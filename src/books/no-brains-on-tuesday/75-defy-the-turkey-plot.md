title: Defy the Turkey Plot
date: 1991-01-01
summary: A defence of the Christmas turkey against critics pushing chicken, duck or seafood instead, running through the full ritual of carving, leftovers and turkey curry, a grandfather who managed a ham and bacon firm, and one disastrous cold-chicken-and-ice-cream experiment that abandoned every tradition and left the family feeling nothing but flat.

There has been an ominous move afoot this Christmas, a plot against the turkey.

Some of our friends have openly announced they will be eating chicken. Yes, boring, wimpish, tasteless, battery produced chicken. Chicken that never once has walked outside into a decent open paddock.

Some are going for duck. Duck, greasy little objects nine times out of 10. Particularly in restaurants, they are a miserable disappointment. Others are having roasts, hunks of beef surrounded by potatoes cooked in fat.

But the biggest shock came in this newspaper last week, an unashamed assault on the turkey by that Sydney, bon viveur, Leo Schofield. He said it was unhealthy to eat too much turkey at midday in summer. He thought the taste was unexceptional, boring, and he had the nerve to add:

'Besides, I dread the leftovers, those obscene thighs, that gross carcass sitting in the fridge for weeks after the poor bird has been despatched.'

So do you know what he's going to eat? Fish! Snapper, prawns, oysters and small green lobsters.

Unhealthy to eat too much at Christmas in high summer? Of course it's unhealthy, he says. You must have one day in the year when you can be decently, voluptuously, unhealthy with a clear conscience.

Now look here, I adore turkey. You can eat fish any time. I'd like to have turkey in February, April, July and September, but you can never gear up our dear ladies to get out there and make the grand effort. On Christmas Day they are spurred by a beautiful tradition.

Turkey became popular in England in midwinter, because the bird comes to its prime in midwinter. But alas, nobody thinks about it here in midwinter, but I'm very happy to have two Christmases a year as long as Myer and David Jones keep out of it.

As an enthusiastic carver, turkey gives one the ultimate pleasure and challenge. The bigger and more obscene the better. I love that superb moment when the nut brown turkey is placed on the Christmas table, that splendid chest, those cathedral like dimensions, that wonderful nave filled with exquisite stuffing.

'Not stuffing,' I can hear my mother saying. 'It is seasoning, darling.'

So one would caress the razor sharp carving knife across the ample breast taking off delicious long thin slices. Then one would sever a leg and drumstick. You would take slices off those too, giving everyone a generous mix of white and dark meat.

Take off the wing and then pick out a favourite, a cute grandchild to swear the absolute piece de resistance was the pope's nose, or nez du pape. My father always used to offer it.

Dammit, I'm in mouth watering tears of anticipation as I write this. It's not just the turkey, it's the marvellous things that go with it, the cranberry sauce, the delicious bread sauce, the way mother used to make it.

Then, golly, the left-overs are the best part. I go for the sandwiches, sliced French bread, succulent turkey, the bits you get out of all the little nooks and caves; more bread sauce, more cranberry, lettuce, a little pepper. Blow your mind.

Finally, when all the bones and architecture of the turkey cathedral are exposed, it is time for the curry. We do it the old fashioned way; turkey, remains of the delectable sausage stuffing, sliced banana, apple, some good stock, lots of quality fruit chutney, the most gorgeous curry you have ever tasted.

You must realise tradition has bitten deep in our family. My grandfather was manager of Farmer's Ham and Bacon. Ham has never tasted quite the same since. In my early childhood I could remember always there was a ham on the sideboard. Not one of these effete deboned canned things you see now, but a huge majestic leg of ham.

So every time you passed the sideboard you grabbed the carving knife and nicked yourself a slice. Bliss.

Therefore at Christmas always there would be the turkey allied with the majestic ham. The plum puddings, at least half a dozen of them, would be assembled and lovingly put in their bags, come late November and boiled for seven hours. Christmas cakes would be made then too and father would lose all his best brandy.

Sixpences and shillings, silver content went out of all our coins. I guess that was the beginning of all our troubles. So my mother had to resort to subterfuge. No longer did the coins go into the cake, she would sneak two bob pieces under each lucky child's slice.

Of course, the Christmas pudding, regardless of temperature outside, would be carried in blazing. It still is.

Heat the brandy first in a saucepan, set it alight and for a moment you would think it was a terrorist invasion.

All pretty unhealthy, I guess, but worth it. And I remember almost every Christmas, going into a half coma at about 3.30 p.m. or 4 p.m. and saying: 'I'm stuffed.'

My mother then would say, 'You are not stuffed, dear. You are replete.'

It is vital to preserve old traditions. Once they are gone you are left with nothing. It is said that Dr David Livingstone, even when he was in remotest Africa, two months walk from the nearest white man, still dressed for dinner. An Englishman had to remain an Englishman.

I think our record for a Christmas dinner was 103 degrees Fahrenheit, in the shade, cooked on an Aga anthracite burning stove. The kitchen was very cosy. But bravely, like Dr Livingstone, my mother carried on.

One year we did make an experiment. We were ruthless. We had cold chicken, cold pickled pork, some cold riesling, followed by some pudding like home-churned ice cream.

It was all a hideous mistake. To be honest, when it was over, I didn't feel replete.
