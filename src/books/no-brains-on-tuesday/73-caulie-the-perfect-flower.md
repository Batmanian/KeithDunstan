There has always been a deep streak of vulgarity in my nature.

You know, a sort of terror of not being noticed. There was only one way to overcome this: grow the biggest plants and vegetables in the entire street.

The theory was that people would wander past and say: 'My, aren't they fantastic.'

So I grew dahlias, not the miserable little dwarfs, but the giant variety with heads as big as rubbish tin lids. I would stake them so that would nod over the front fence for every neighbour to admire.

They all had names, Vivien Leigh, Pop Harris, Sammy Davis jnr.

It gave me a lot of satisfaction to be able to put Vivien Leigh in the same bed as Sammy Davis jnr.

We don't grow dahlias so much any more, but we are deep into vegetables. They are a passion really. The trouble is we believe everything we read.

Remember the Canadian 5BX plan. We did that. We were almost the first to get into jogging. We were right in the vanguard of eating Percy Cerutty's raw oatmeal and dried apricots.

Nathan Pritikin, the Egg Diet, Beverly Hills diet, did all those. We believed Time magazine when it told us to give up salt. Was it the Reader's Digest or Women's Weekly that told us to lay off sugar? Anyway we followed their instructions.

Fibre? We were on to that. We ate enough fibre to start a rope factory.

This vegetable thing, though, has sort of crept up on us. Almost everyone we know is vegetarian or going that way.

We had an American student with us for a year. She was a vegetarian. Every time I put a chop on my plate she would glare. We used to like a wing rib of beef. This was pure Hereford homicide. I felt as cruel as a South African cop.

Dinner parties are becoming immensely complicated.

In the old days you would invite all the Catholics on Wednesdays, then perhaps get in the Protestants on Fridays. In the sixties one was careful never to mix ALP hard-liners with DLP lovers.

In the 70s you had to watch the affluent middle class. It could be dynamite if you let loose your Western District-top-end-of-Collins Street conservatives among your Range Rover keep-uranium-in-the-ground ALP trendies.

Now, my God, the worry is with the 'wets' and 'drys'. Can you invite them to the same dinner table?

But our problem goes deeper. Do you have all vegetarian dinner parties, or do you have to cook two whole dinners on the one night and offer them a choice. Quiche or Kebabs? Beef or beans? Liver or lettuce?

It goes even further than that. We have some mates who have gone vegan. This means they are so serious they don't eat fish. They wouldn't touch an anchovy in a fit. They don't eat eggs or dairy foods.

Heaven's above, you can't even give them a cheese dip before lunch or caramel custard for dessert. You have to think before you put a dollop of cream in the avocado soup.

Fred and Mary claim they don't drink milk any more because it produces mucus, and mucus is allied with flu and colds. Honestly, you can't even mix vegans with normal vegetarians...

But clearly being vegetarian is the thing of the moment, so we turned the garden over to vegetables. Oh I tell you, it hasn't been easy.

The place was infested with rabbits so I had to build a rabbit-proof fence. $160.

The soil was miserably impoverished, so I had to buy lime, blood and bone, organic soil improver; we made six excursions to the beach to gather seaweed. $80.

Snails, white butterflies, pests abounded. Derris dust, snail killer, insect sprays. $22.

We couldn't be on the spot all the time, so I had to buy sprinklers and a time clock device. $110.

The local water board spies were on to it immediately. Excess water rates. $125.

But it was pure bliss when the red runners, the lettuce, the squash, the tomatoes, the corn, the silverbeet, the red cabbage, the peas, all began to arrive.

The particular dream was the cauliflower. It is my belief that the cauliflower is the most beautiful flower God ever created. Drenched with white sauce or just made into a dish on its own with a little cheese, unquestionably you can get a sense of the divine order of things.

But just between you and me some of my vegetables have not quite reached the degree of vulgarity that I was looking for. The lettuce did not have the massive Phar Lap hearts that I had expected, some of the cabbage didn't heart at all.

There were spaghetti squash, which should have been bigger than league footballs, but only made the minor grade. And as for cauliflowers, they had a kinda grey look.

I did a little cost analysis and discovered that those caulies might have been worth around $5 each. If we had bought everything at the local market we would have saved $200.

But my wife said sweetly: 'But darling they tasted so much better and they were all organic.'
