---
title: Don't Hang Up on Phone Callers
date: 1991-01-01
summary: Telephone addiction is ranked among the most serious drugs, ahead of alcohol and tobacco, with evidence including a mother whose bill from Minnesota reached $400, the futility of 'Maximum three minutes' notices above the telephone, the exotic new handsets Telecom produces as inviting as lollipops, and a suggestion that whoever makes local calls free will be Prime Minister for a generation.
tags:
  - Telecom
---

There are two kinds of telephone callers. There is no sex difference. Either hair of another human is the trigger. Grandpa tells you the hair is quite sufficient — signals the hair — and the Richter scale goes up to three or four.

Tell them further their hair is too long; too short; the wrong colour; that beards look better on Moses, Karl Marx or Methuselah; that no-one in the Western world has a job if they do creative things, such as writing. You can take the shadow of a telephone jack, complete with spikes — explosions and they leave home.

The telephone is no different. This is why I cannot understand why a Government would dare to institute a thing as monstrous as time-charging for local calls. It is an innocent, good thing. You put in a call that almost certainly goes for a full hour. I know, because I have always made calls to their mates of a generous length. They have finished dressing for school, but don't try to call Aunt Freda before noon — the phone is always engaged. It wouldn't matter if the roof was on fire, you wouldn't get through.

Telephone is one of our most serious addictive drugs; perhaps not ahead of alcohol and tobacco but close. I am finding guiltily that I can spend as long as 40 minutes on the telephone fix. You get that nervous twitch in the right hand.

I know a truly pathetic case: a mother in — I have a daughter in Minnesota. I think their last phone bill was $400. A Government ought to provide compensation for this.

I have tried to exercise discipline, pasted notices on the wall above the telephone: 'Maximum three minutes.' It still makes no difference. The Telecom sends me a bill done in pretty colours. So one thunders: ring in off-peak or I'll have the bloody thing off.

It doesn't work. 'Look, if you don't mind, I'll ring her right now. But this once, because she's always home with the baby at 10.30.' What do you do? You can't fight someone who is panting for a telephone fix. I know I am doing the same thing at 11 a.m.

It doesn't stop. Telecom produces exotic telephones as inviting as lollipops. It runs nightly commercials showing weeping mothers in Turin, London, Vladivostok. It keeps bringing out new-fangled telephones so that you can ring from anywhere: in your car, in a plane, on the dunny. It has telephones that store 30 of your favourite numbers, so that to call Kate, Sarah, Mark, Jane, Joan, Judy, Marge — you have to press only one button. I reckon it's hilarious.

I even have a word processor with a modem that can take an infinite number of telephone numbers and dial for you. It is just a matter of time before the telephone becomes a permanent part of the human body.

If I could offer some simple advice to the man who is serious in his desire to be Prime Minister: make local phone calls free. It has been done in some U.S. states.

We would all remember Dr Hewson as the man who gave us Phoners Eldorado. He would probably remain Prime Minister for a generation.
