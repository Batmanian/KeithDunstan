Or how about the ecstatic joyful arrival of all the grandchildren, then being able to send them home when you don't need to.

As you may be aware this column is the headquarters for the Society Against Progress.

S.A.P. believes that nothing ever gets better, only worse. S.A.P.s look back with nostalgia to the days when the dollar was a splendid unchangeable thing not subject to daily absurd variations, to the days when there were two postal deliveries a day, and again on Saturday morning.

When you did not have to write a cheque to have an article delivered. Yes, your postage went up again yesterday. When the air was breathable and there was still a park or two along Batman Avenue.

Every so often S.A.P. likes to give a deterioration report. Last week your S.A.P. observer went shopping during the school holidays. In Bourke Street, the crowd was so intense one wondered whether this was a pre-election uprising, with characters flogging gadgets everywhere.

You had to fight your way into the big store because right at the entrance they had a jazz band, pumping out music. To make matters worse there was a perfume promotion. Languid looking blondes were wandering around with guns squirting smell almost at random.

I took quick evasion action and jumped on the escalator. I ask you to believe the scene that was on the first floor. Indeed in one area close to the ceiling there were six television sets in a row, all thumping out rock music. While I ask you to believe, there was a gentleman with a guitar and a face that appeared to be painted an extraordinary colour, making gymnastic leaps while he attempted to play his instrument. Meanwhile in front of him, a young female with short hair and a leather skirt about the width of a pair of shoelaces was indulging in the most remarkable writhing movements. They had males and females with microphones but here were flashing red and blue lights, precisely like those used by the police and ambulance. When first I saw the blue blinking light I had a feeling of security. At last a constable had arrived to restore law and order.

But, no, a salesman with full amplification was shouting: 'Get your French sun glasses, only $15 each. Never again will you get an opportunity like this. Buy seven of them. Have a different style of glasses to wear for every day of the week.'

In the midst of all this one couldn't help but note the difficulty one had in actually trying to purchase something. There was an amazing number of people employed to encourage excitement but few indeed to lead one to the article that was required.

The modern theory of shopping is 'seek and ye shall find'. Seeking for one item can take anything up to half an hour. Indeed in some stores and supermarkets you can virtually die of starvation before you find your quarry. You would think they would have a sort of Melway or handy chart to advise where you can find each item.

At one stage I was so desperate I went into a trance and a vision came to mind of how stores used to be. At the entrance there was an elegant gentleman, impeccably dressed in a morning suit. He would greet you, then direct you to the appropriate department.

The atmosphere was quiet. Piped music inside stores, mercifully, had not been invented. There was always a seat or a stool. You sat down and a sales person, gentleman or lady, asked what you required and brought it to you. This was known as a 'counter.' Ah, but between you and the goods there was a barrier.

A wonderfully convenient system it was. Then having bought your article they asked, respectfully, to which address you would like it delivered. Home delivery in those blissful days was automatic and free.

Under the 'Seek and Ye Can Find' system you hunt down your quarry, try to guess what size you would be in centimetres, then you take it to a sales island, where you might find someone with a cash register.

I bought two singlets, then a belt, both splendidly wrapped. But at each island they insisted on putting these items in a huge plastic bag. Apparently it is compulsory that everything purchased must go in these bags.

One noticed further, incredibly, that they had areas called 'WRAP BAR'. My wife explained one went to a Wrap Bar when one wanted something beautifully gift wrapped.

Surely it would be more useful if the Wrap Bar unwrapped things for you, quietly disposed of the mountain of plastic bags that you don't want. No wonder we are having the Greenhouse Effect and the world is going to ruin. It is the hundred billion plastic bags that have to be burned every day.

My next shopping item was a compact disc. I thought it would be nice if we could play some gentle harp music while we were having dinner. However, the record shops are the most unnerving of all. Where you would think there would be a counter between customers and goods, chairs, kindly sales people, instead you are assailed through the volume of their sound and the hi-finess of their hi-fi.

Even before you approach the shop you can hear the rock reverberating down the street. Entering the shop is like swimming under water. You know that the human system can endure such overwhelming forces no longer than two minutes.

Find what you want, get in and get out quickly. I found a shop assistant, mouthed what I wanted hoping he could lip read. It was impossible for humans to conduct conversation in a record shop back around 1978, and probably his hearing was destroyed back then too.

Alas, we did not establish communication. Gasping for silence I fled.
