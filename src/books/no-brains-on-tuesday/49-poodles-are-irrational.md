Regular readers of this column might just possibly remember an embarrassing account two years ago of the death of Bessie. Please stand by for the regrettable sequel.

Bessie was an apricot coloured 15-year-old poodle, who on a black terrible day had to be put down by the vet. We were very sensible about it. No creature could ever replace Bessie. Never again would we allow ourselves to suffer such dog domination.

Six months went by, then a friend, who understands our sometimes not very rational behaviour, telephoned: 'I have just been to see 'X' and she has a little baby poodle, one of two in a litter, the other died. Lovely little thing, an only child.'

'Oh well, poodles do get born,' I said, sounding terribly disinterested. 'Well, we're actually going right past X's door today,' said Marie, 'no harm in just looking, we can see how dog breeders go about things.'

'Yes, of course,' I said, 'we will go merely on an educational exercise.' We called on X. Like all true dog breeders there were few concessions to the human world. She was in jeans, boots, shirt, no make-up, intensely practical, and all loving for everything on four legs. She went away and returned with a tiny poodle the size of two tennis balls. It was a coal black poodle with intense black eyes. As an old hand at these matters I saw immediately that Madame X was going to insist on the full VIP treatment.

'I don't just sell poodles to anybody,' she said. 'Money is of no concern to me when I handle dogs. First I have to be absolutely sure you are poodle people.'

That was a worry. How was I to prove that we were up to proper poodle standards? I did point out we had had 15 years training, but I don't think she was listening.

She continued her assault. 'And anyway when did your poodle die?' 'Six months ago.' And I gave her the precise date.

'I don't think you have gone through the proper grief period.'

'I beg your pardon.'

'After your poodle has died,' X continued relentlessly, 'you should go through a grief period of at least a year. It is absolutely necessary if you are going to handle the next poodle properly.'

X said she would let us know in six weeks time and then, 'possibly', we could purchase the black creature which she called 'Puppity.' In the mean time I suspect inquiries were made similar to those the FBI conducts when an American president chooses a Defence Secretary or a Secretary of State.

The call did come and, suffering strange feelings of disloyalty to the late Bessie, we hurried round to Madame X. She was there all prepared. First we received a lecture on the care and handling of poodles. I ask you to believe that not only was puppity washed, brushed and ready to go, but also she had her worldly possessions supplied by X. These included all sorts of potions; de-worming pills, puppity's favourite cushion and blanket, puppity's toys and an electric warming cushion for puppity to sleep on.

Madame X proudly gave us a run down on puppity's elegant pedigree and announced she would be pleased if we called her Tiffany. 'You don't HAVE to call her Tiffany,' said X, 'but that is her name.' I felt this was an order.

Madame X took an unconscionable time to hand over the little black creature, but eventually Marie took puppity in her arms and we went off to the car. I had the strong suspicion that Madame X was crying.

As we drove home Marie said: 'Of course, she can't be Tiffany. You can see at once she isn't a Tiffany.' So for the first week we even consulted baby books and went through the Births columns of the Sun News-Pictorial. The only one that seemed to fit was Charlotte, Lotte for short.

I think that dogs, like many humans, have been on this earth before. Lotte proved immediately that she was a strong, independent spirit. She was not interested in any of the things that Madame X gave her on departure.

We had for the grandchildren a large, soft, furry mouse. It had big button eyes and a long tail, about three times the size of Lotte. Lotte immediately fell in love with mouse. Where she went, mouse had to go. She slept with mouse and still does.

But we were strong. Never would we display the weakness that we did with Bessie. Bessie was a creature of immense guile and intelligence. Whenever we would go away too, we would find Bessie sitting in the car waiting to go. We smuggled her into motel rooms in handbags. There wouldn't be any of that nonsense with Lotte.

So Lotte, at once, received firm orders, and we prepared a superbly comfortable basket with soft bedding which would have done credit to a Royal baby.

There were piteous cries. I called it doing a June Bronhill. It went on and on. I can't tell you, it could have been Mimi in La Boheme dying tragically of tuberculosis.

We both cracked at the same time. 'She can't stay there. Just this once.' She has been there in the bedroom ever since. Just like Bessie, we never allow her to sleep on the bed. Craftily she waits until she is sure we are both asleep, then she creeps up. Being all black you can't see her in the dark. You find this lump beside your feet in the morning.

Like all poodles she is quite irrational. Bessie detested children. Lotte adores them. However, adults you want her to like, she snarls at. Then out of the blue, for no reason at all, she takes a fancy to a visitor who is virtually a stranger, say the plumber, and jumps on to his lap.

She prefers human food to dog food, apples, peaches, cheese, and the Pritikin bran muffins we eat when dieting. She will absolutely kill for avocados. So you can see we are stuck with Lotte. I fear for the future.
