title: Divorce in NSW
date: 1988-03-04
summary: New South Wales passes the Matrimonial Clauses Bill in March 1874, allowing divorce on grounds of adultery, but with starkly different standards for men and women, in a parliamentary debate where opponents warn that allowing wives to prosecute husbands for adultery alone will open a very large door and overwhelm the barristers with work.
tags:

---

The Matrimonial Clauses Bill, which permits divorce, became law in New South Wales today. Heaven knows what trouble this will cause. Many fine citizens are worried that it will mean a complete breakdown in marriage. But then, of course, it is tilted in favour of men, which may add some morality.

Again you must understand, in our society we do not consider women have the power to understand all the implications. Males have the right to vote for the Legislative Assembly and the Council, but we do not extend that right to women and Aborigines. And as far as we do not admit them to institutions of higher learning. Furthermore, in the law courts males have the right to the earnings of a wife.

As it is now, divorce is extremely rare and I don't think I have ever met a divorced person. Here in the colonies there is still a preponderance of men. In New South Wales, roughly six women in a 100 go unmarried, whereas you find twenty-five in a 100 males unmarried.

Under the law, given assent by His Excellency today, males can sue for adultery, but for women it is a different matter. The New South Wales Parliament decided to follow the precepts of British justice. A woman will be able to sue for divorce only if her husband has been guilty of incestuous adultery, bigamy with adultery, the person committed rape, or adultery associated with desertion.

There was a heated debate. One member said he had the greatest regard for women's rights, but those rights in this land were not the same as those of men, he said. 'We must legislate for the children. A woman can always demand maintenance and a separate establishment and no more can be desired than that. But if the women are allowed to prosecute the husbands for adultery a very large door is opened and there will be no end to the divorce cases brought into court. The barristers will be overwhelmed with work.'

He pleaded with the House to show proper sense on this question. Sir William Manning said that when first the Bill came before the House he thought there should be an equity of justice between men and women, but now he had thought about it more deeply.

'I believe there is an opinion prevalent largely among both men and women that a wife who commits adultery is lost, and whilst a man who falls into the same error is not so regarded, in a moment of weakness might otherwise be a good man and useful to the community.'

Mr C.C. Campbell proposed that after a marriage had broken down the adulteress should never be allowed to remarry, as the person committed adultery associated committed that crime. Those of standing in the land, and I would affirm that at the conclusion, do not wish to take the soft sex from domestic matters.
