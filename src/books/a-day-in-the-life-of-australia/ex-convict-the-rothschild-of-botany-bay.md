We buried Samuel Terry in Sydney today. What a funeral! Unquestionably it was the grandest in the history of the colony. We are still trying to work out what he was worth. What with all his houses, land and business interests, it comes to over half a million pounds. How could anyone become so rich, particularly an ex-convict. They had a name for him in Sydney, the 'Rothschild of Botany Bay'.

Terry began his career as a labourer in England and received his free passage to this colony for stealing 400 pairs of stockings. He arrived in 1801, when the colony was only 13 years old. He worked as a labourer building the Parramatta female factory, then was a soldier.

Sam Terry learned quickly what it was like to be flogged. But he was diligent. His time expired quickly. He took every opportunity. First he was a soldier, next a self-employed stonemason, then he became a farmer with land on the Hawkesbury.

In 1808 he moved to Sydney, became an innkeeper. Then he married. That was the best thing he ever did. He married a widow, Mary Reeks, who had her own fortune. They combined forces and opened a hotel in Pitt Street.

He has a reputation for being tough and ruthless. Fail to pay your debts and he would have you in court as fast as lightning. But the better side of Terry was to put his hand in his pocket liberally and there was not much he missed. He has built the Sydney Public School, the Bible Society building and he was in the chair for the civic dinner for the fortieth anniversary of the colony.

Particularly fascinating is the fact that he was a Freemason, a member of Lodge number 54, the most prestigious lodge in the colonies. At first the lodge tried to bar ex-convicts from membership, however, a ruling came that Terry's behaviour was 'un-masonic'.

Shortly before he died, Terry asked to have a Masonic funeral, and a grand affair it was indeed.

His residence is in Pitt Street. He built the Terry buildings. Shareholder, he owned a bank. He has a reputation for being tough and ruthless. Not only is there the Sydney Public School, he funded the Bible Society. He was in the chair for the civic dinner for the fortieth anniversary of the colony, and he was involved in almost everything. The entire city, he could say with some justification, was his.
