“Criue V*m much" - FROM — - KEITH DUNSTAN "THEATRE LONDON, October 14.' — No major company put on The Cocktail Party in Aus tralia and your chances of see ing: The Confidential Clerk are practically nil. T. S. Eliot wrote both these plays. Londoners have just branded him as “the greatest poet-dramatist” since Shakespeare. And this be-spec tacled, rather melancholy man is without doubt the greatest man of letters of our time. You wouldn’t pick him as a poet, no eccentric clothes, no unruly shock of hair. More than anything he looks like a character from one of his plays, a confidential clerk. At 64, still the idol of the under-' graduates as he was 30 years ago, like no other poet in the world, he is watching the money roll in. The Cocktail Party in two years has been seen by more than 1,500,000. In New York, London, Germany and South Africa people flocked to see it. The Confidential Clerk has just opened in London. The Times says: “No contem porary play lias been more generally praised.” Five New York theatres have already put in bids for it. Wliy is it that so many of the best plays by-pass Australia? Pondering this I called up a rov ing buyer of stage productions for Australia. He said: “I haven’t seen The Con fidential Clerk, but generally we find that sort of play doesn’t pay in Australia. It’s too high falutin’. “You know what Australians like. They like to be entertained. They like down to earth, earthy humor, farce, comedies and good musicals. “Oh. yes, if you get a top company like The' Old Vic or Stratford on Avon players—the name’ll drag them along.” “But surely,” I said, “look at the success of The Cocktail Party in other countries, it’d be an enormous hit in Australia.” The man was getting nettled. Mow look,” he said, “people are al ways telling us we could make a fortune out of this play, or a for tune out of that. “If you had the money would you be prepared to back the production of Tlie Confidential Clerk or The Cocktail Party in Australia?” He had me cornered. “No, I don’t think I would.” “There.” He was in triumph. "That’s what we feel. Dame Nellie Melba once said of Australians, ‘Give ’em muck.’ “Well, they don’t want muck but they don’t want anything too high falutin’.” I went home and thought the thing over. About 2 a.m. I came to the conclusion, T. S. Eliot plays WOULD go over in Australia. For years people have been under rating the Intelligence of Austral ians. They are among the most highly educated people m the world —higher man for man than either the- English or the Americans. It is a pity that Australia can’t see something of the works of this great man, on a scale larger than repertory can provide.
