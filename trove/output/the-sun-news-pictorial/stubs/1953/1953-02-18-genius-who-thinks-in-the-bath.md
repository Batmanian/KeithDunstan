Genius who thinks in the bath! From Keith Dunstan London. — Bril liant Mr. Ustinov — everyone says he’s brilliant — has a ponderous, overhang ing upper lip. He is an untidy 15 - stone, amiable elephant. As far as this city is concerned he has the stage by the ears. His .play, The Love of Four Colonels, is being pro duced in 14 European countries and it has just opened in New York. The Love of Four Col onels is a world - wide ■hit, almost that is, but there is no suggestion of it or any other Peter Ustinov play going to Australia. Australia will have to wait, a long time probably. Your only chance of actually , seeing Mr Ustinov is on films. Do you remember him as Nero in Quo Vadis, slob bering, crying, gloating madly, moaning over his fiddle, his contorted face crimson in the reflection of burning Rome? His brilliance there stole tire He is like a small boy who sudddenly has dis covered two remarkable talents — he can whistle and he can snnp his fingers. So he does both at once, and the racket is terrible. Peter Ustinov’s bag of talents is so impressive a critic remarked: “It wouldn’t surprise me a bit if he suddenly took the baton from Sir Thomas Beecham one night and started to conduct the Royal Phil harmonic.” He has written a dozen plays and acted in most of them. He paints, broadcasts, lectures, ap pears op television and, at the moment, he is working on two plays and two novels. He is the sort of man who has 20 ideas in the bath in the morning and spends the rest of the day busting to put them into effect. “I And the bath won derful for creative thinking," he says. “I contemplate the ceiling. Actually, I would like to contemplate my navel like an Oriental mystic. But I can't see that far.” His bust is now in the Royal Academy, Lon don’s symbol of solid achievement. It hasn't been easy for die sculp tor. Ustinov passed his time during sitting com posing a violin concerto in his head and imitat ing all the instruments. But that's Ustinov. He is rarely himself He must entertain, act and mimic. In ' a recent show, one moment he was imitating Churchill, the next a U.S. senator, Mr Vyshinsky, an earthy Australian and he even parodied his sacred em ployers, the BBC. His zest for satire is both his brilliance and handicap. Critics com plain he loads his plays with witty satirical portraits and the overall effect makes for a won derful evening’s enter tainment, but there is no cohesion and it is not great writing. But Peter Ustinov is contemptuous of the critics and when the re views are unkind he says: “Anyway, I’m not even English. My father was half-Russian, half German, and my mother was half-R'ussian, and half-French.” But Mr Ustinov is an ex-English public school boy where he claims he was so dull he didn’t even get his leaving cer tificate. “Then I went on the stage because' I couldn’t do anything else.” So at 16 he was translating Maxim Gorky for the London stage, at 20 his plays were being performed privately, at 21 he had his first big London opening. His only other career was the Army. “I just wasn’t any good at it. I rose, after many struggles, to the rank of private, third class, smoking.” Now at 32, ire’s a veteran and building a fortune, a’ man who can write keeping a weather eye on all possibilities of revenue, fiction, stage, radio, television and films. He lives in an 18th century house in Chel sea, filled with gilt sofas, elaborate ornaments and lamp brackets. - Two secretaries and some Slav refugees look after his wants, serve cups of tea and pass the telephone so that he can hear progress of pro ductions in New York, Paris or Madrid. The visitors come and go and the flamboyant Peter talks, talks, talks— of course,, always bril liantly.
