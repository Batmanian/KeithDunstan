Of course, to de scribe Wimbledon as 'the championship of the world' is rather brash and excessively Aus tralian. In England tennis folk mostly refer to the All Eng land Croquet and Tennis Club at Wimbledon, quietly and more properly, as 'H.Q.' or headquarters, as if it were the GPO across the street. Even so, every year Wimbledon becomes more and more like a world title fight. As in boxing it is man against man with the individual clash of personalities. Players come from all over the ?world and with each set, ticking off like the rounds of an interna tional prize fight, Wimbledon has something of the drama of the ring. But for the reporter it is one of the most exasperating and frightening of all assignments. Rarely does the tournament ever turn out as expected, and this year was no exception. On the first day you sit down and write: 'Lew Hoad should beat Trabert in the Davis Cup. He's in good shape, his serve is terrific and he is hitting the peak of his form just at the right time. He has the temperament ... he can't lose.' But he does lose, and it has happened to our champions so many times before. Wimbledon, witn its 128 contestants, goes on [or a terrible, harrowing fort night., The winner needs not only to be ot top form at the finish. He also needs luck. Then there's something about the atmosphere of the place, the frightening aura of the 'big Lime' which makes some players produce form they have never had before and makes some champions play like Saturday afternoon hacks. When 'Brom' had it won Australia, accord ing to the run of form, should have had at least 12 Wimble don champions in the last 50 years. But up to this year there have been only four: Sir Norman Brookes, 1905; G. L. Patterson, 1919; Jack Crawford, 1933; and Frank Sedgman, 1952. In recent years Pails, Brom wich, Geoff Brown. Ken McGregor, all came close to winning the title. But at the last moment, inexplicably they let it drift away. The classic example was 1948. John Bromwich was leading Bob Falkenburg 5-2 in the fifth set of the final. He had two match points, it all seemed too good to be true, so carefully Brom put up the de fences. Falkenburg won that set, and the match, 7-5. But for tennis-hungry Australia every little happening at Wimble don has to be covered. And there's so much going on at the . one time. You may be covering a truly historic marathon on the centre court, say between Drobny and Patty, only to discover that you are missing a newsy little story on court 24. Miss Soand So from the. Argentine, a superb ly beautiful young lady, who is reported to be highly fancied by President Peron, is putting up a brave fight against Little Mo. Then you discover — there are 26 courts at Wimbledon — that on court number 2 — a young player has just stamped his foot and said something perfectly out rageous to the umpire. So you sprint back and hope that you can get the story in time for Australian editions. Last week a t joy of life J UST to be at Wimble don in the last week of June is one of the joys of life. Strangely enough it rarely rains and the weather is warm, breathless, and gentle. Perhaps the officials long ago carefully studied the weather re ports and arranged it thus, but the cynics will tell you that the brief English summer hurries in at the beginning of Wimbledon and hurries away at the end. However if the inexcusable should happen and it should rain, they have an answer for that, too. On the centre court there is a marvellous mechanical contriv ance which projects a cover over Ihe grass the minute the first drops fall out of the sky. So they have missed only three full days of play in 25 years. But to tell you what Wimble don actually looks like, that is not easy. The centre court is completely surrounded by one large stand so that it is like, maybe, the Yankee Stadium. . . . But no, that doesn't fit because the outside is covered by thick ivy, and the club has a more sophisticated atmosphere. Others will tell you that with the surrounding lawns, the trees, the refreshment tables with rows and rows of cabana-like um brellas it is more like Ascot or Flemington. The game of society AND there is some thing in this. For tennis, after all, in England is still the game of society. Parading from court to court you will see really 'beautiful women, magnificently dressed ac companied by smart young Eng lish men about town with their bowler hats and umbrellas rolled as thin as pencils. There are the little touches of glamour that lift it beyond the ordinary tennis tournament. Wimbledon Club employs a large fleet of limousines (you could only call them limousines) to carry the players from their hotels to the club. The limousines arrive at the front gate and drive slowly to the dressing room. And it is half the fun of the day to stand there, perhaps with an autograph book in your hand, and see the great men arrive, Drobny in his little black glasses, Patty invariably flamboyant with a spotted scarf about his neck or perhaps Herbie Flam with his sporty little crew cut. For the finalists on the centre court there is an old Wim bledon custom. The players walk out on to the court, the crowd roars, and the chief attendant in the locker room, wearing a white coat, follows behind carrying the racquets. Duchess is a patron I HEN as play goes on you hope, maybe, that some member of the Royal Family will arrive. In the old days Queen Mary was there every year. ?She would move quietly into the Royal Box, the great crowd would stand in silence. Play stopped immediately, and the players no matter what their nationality, would turn and bow. Queen Mary would smile and wave and so play went on. Now the Duchess of Kent is the only member of the Royal family who takes an interest in tennis., . / Wimbledon this year with its 128 entrants should make a pro fit of £50,000, a figure that can't be challenged by any other ten nis tournament. But the first Wimbledon in 1877 attracted only 22 players, yet there was still a profit — 1/10. Game began in Egypt rROM what I can dis cover in the dusty records of the game, tennis as a ball game had its origins in Persia or Egypt about 500 years ago. But the honour of starting modern lawn tennis is credited to a cer* tain Major Walter Wingfield, MVO. In February 1874 he devised a set of rules and introduced tennis to polite society as a 'not very taxing but agreeable game.' He called it Sphairistike. Neither Major Wingfield's rules, not the astonishing name Sphairistike lasted very long, but he set the ball rolling, so to speak. The game was a remarkable success at the All England Croquet Club at Wimbledon. So much so that the members almost forgot about their croquet., and in 1877 they decided to add 'Lawn Tennis' to the title, and it has been the All England Croquet and Tennis Club ever since. The winner of the first Wim bledon title was an. old Harrovian, Mr. Spencer W. Gore. He served from a point level with his shoulder and according to the reports 'would approach the net (very Hoadish), and by using his long reach -and flexible wrist, volley the ball for a win ning point.' From 1877 on Wimbledon never looked back, and by 1884 they were letting even ladies into the game. Yes, Major Wingfield would have something to think about if he were standing by the centre court to-day. The television cables trailing through the stands, the movie cameras, the great crowd, the flags, and most astonishing of all, young Rose wall making an incredible use of his "flexible wrist."
