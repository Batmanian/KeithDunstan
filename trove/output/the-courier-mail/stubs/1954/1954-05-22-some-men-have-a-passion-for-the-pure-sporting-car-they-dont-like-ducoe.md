Some men have a passion for the pure sporting car . . . They don't like ducoed whales by KEITH DUNSTAN THERE was the potent smell of methanol in the air at Strathpine, and one after another, screaming down the airstrip, came a box- like Lea Francis, a smooth Jaguar, then a neat, bouncy, little MG. The MG, at over 6000 revs a minute, gave out a fright- ening noise like a giant ripping up great sheets of calico, but to the enthusiasts of the Queensland Motor Sporting Club this was beautiful music — the sound that comes from perfect tuning and weeks of hard work. There's something irresistible about enthusiastic people, and I asked one man why he found this sport so fascinating. He looked startled for a moment and said:- 'Well— I just like cars. I like the look of them, the feel of 'em, the smell of 'em. It's difficult to say, but I do think that car racing satisfies a spirit of compet- ition that you just can't get anywhere else. "'Then the feel of a car going fast, really fast, doing everything it's asked to do, that's — yes, that's really some- thing." Maybe it is a tribute to this technical age, but there is a growing interest in motor sports all over Australia. In Queens- land there are now clubs in Bris- bane, Toowoomba, South Bur- nett, Ipswich, Roma, and Towns- ville. Cater for all types THESE clubs cater for everyone, from the man with a -true-to-type 140 mph rac- ing car to the driver of a Ford Prefect, who likes to get out with the club and show his navigation skill on an all night reliability trial. Then there's the lover of vintage cars, perhaps the greatest en- thusiast of them all. He be- lieves that mass-production ruined the motor car and that cars of true breeding and beauty went out some time around 1934. Ask George Ross of the club here and he'll tell you that the modern car looks like a ducoed whale playing a harmonica. But talk to him of the Red Badge 3 litre Bentley or the 30/98 Vauxhall and he will go into raptures as if the sub- ject were the Michelangelo Pieta or a Chippendale chair. But most sport car enthusiasts are starved all their lives for money. Racing fuel which can be a mixture of say petrol, meth- anol, benzol, or anything under the sun, costs up to 9/ a gallon Racing tyres, which cost £50 each, can burn up to nothing after eight hours of driving around the century mark. As for thoroughbred racing cars themselves, they sell for anything from £2000 to £10,000. So the car racing enthusiast, very often, has to build his rac- ing car himself. First he finds himself an en- gine, then he wraps around it an extraordinary mixture of parts which he gathers from junk yards all over the country. The result is what he calls 'a special.' Thousands of hours of work AND only the man who has ever built one knows the heartbreaks, the thou- sands of hours of work, the skill and improvisation that is in- volved. The best special in Queensland Is owned by Ken Richardson, a dealer in second-hand cars. It has a Ford V8 motor, built onto a Whippet chassis, shortened and turned upside down. It has a Ford gear box, Studebaker steer- ing, and the rest is hand-made. Enough to give it a top speed— hand-timed at Leyburn — of 124 mph. Charles Whatmore, a great veteran of the game who has been racing here since 1926, is just finishing a special with a pedigree chart more curious than that of an alley cat, but like many a cat this special will be tough and lively. It has a 1938 Standard 12 chassis, MG wheels, a Mark VII Jaguar motor, and a Model A Ford differential. But for the man who can't build himself a special and doesn't relish the idea of driving at ninety, then there's the re- liability trial. And in a reliability trial a Morris Minor is as good as a Jaguar. When sent out on the trial, the starter hands out a set of direc- tions, which may take you out all night, on back country roads, to lonely control points — and if you are good, arriving at each point dead on time. Biggest trial of all THE sporting car people say that the reliability trial is superb training for any future tank commander or navigator and in July we will have the biggest reliability trial of them all — the Redex. It starts on July 3 and will last approximately 18 days. In that 18 days — and if the com- petitors make it in that time it will be almost a miracle — the cars will drive 9600 miles right around Australia. Last year the route was only 6000 miles but the trial itself aroused more interest than any other motoring event seen in Australia. When the trial started at Sydney over 200,000 people gathered to see the cars go. They weren't racing, and they were only touring cars, but there was something romantic about the idea of ordinary touring cars setting out on such an adventurous journey. In this year's trial there will be mole than 300 entrants — every- thing from little Austins to big Humber Super Snipes. However the route is really frightening. One of the organisers, Charles Pleasance, while testing out the route got bogged down in the Kimberleys and could proceed no further. No water for 400 miles I HAVE just been looking at a road map of the north-west. The road from Darwin through Wave Hill to Hall's Creek is marked VRD, which stands for very rough dirt. Actually the roads in that area are nothing more than wheel tracks which disappear into sand in the dry and become slimy bogs in the wet, so slippery that car wheels spin, helplessly. The Redex trial men will have to drive anything up to 400 miles without seeing a garage, a shop, water, or hab- itation of any kind. But such things are all good for a trial, and the man who drives a little 8 horsepower machine to work every day is far more interested in the outcome of a reliability trial than the re- sult of a fabulous Continental car race, involving machines far beyond the reach of his pocket. The Redex trial men will be in Brisbane on Sunday, July 4, and they will assemble at a control point at the Exhibition. Thou sands are expected to cheer them on their way. They dream of open road CAR racing always attracts big crowds. For the Australian Grand Prix at Leyburn airfield, near Too- HERE is Ray Lewis at the wheel of a Cooper sliding crazilv through a corner at White's Hill, Brisbane. woomba, in 1949, some 30,000 people drove out for the day from Brisbane — 143 miles there and 143 miles back and that at a time of petrol restrictions. But the dream of all motor sporting people in Australia is to get the use of the open roads for racing as they do for the classic Continental races, the Grand Prix, Le Mans, and Italy's Mille Miglia. In Italy and France the authorities are prepared to close off the roads for anything up to 24 hours and these races are among the world's greatest tour ist attractions. The motor sporting people here talk with shining eyes of what they could do if only they had a road race finishing up in Brisbane just like the Mon- aco Grand Prix where the cars race through the main streets of Monte Carlo — imagine the spectacle, they say, of cars tak- ing the hill on Wickham Ter- race. Such a race would be Bris- bane's biggest tourist draw, a great festival, that would bring thousands into the town. And they quote the case of Bathurst, NSW. When car races are held there the week-end intake at the banks goes up by £80,000. The use of Brisbane's roads — maybe that's too big a thing to ask. But car racing men are big dreamers, and who knows, per- haps one day they may get their open road circuit in Queensland.
