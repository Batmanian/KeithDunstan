A TOWER THAT'S AWED THE WORLD FOR 9OO YEARS 'Saddest spot on earth' will reap a fortune from KEITH DUNSTAN LONDON- — The winter mists swirl around the ancient turrets, a moan of protest seems to come from the Bloody Tower, but the Beefeaters smile happily — they know the lower of London is becoming one of the greatesi tourist 'attractions on earth. William the Conqueror started the Tower in 107£ 'to awe the Londoners, and ever since it has awed the world. Nothing else can compete, Westminster Abbey, the Houses of Parliament, the Zoo — the Tower outdoes them all Visitors romp through at anything up to 9000 a day, all buying tickets, guide-books, postcards, ice cream nnd cake. Business is good now, but when Coronation time comes fortune! will be made. Thousands will come to see the Crown jewels thousands will come to in spect rare examples of medie val architecture, but for thou. sands more it will be the fascination of the macabre and the love of a good horror story. The process of making the Tower fit for tourists began in the last century. In those days the moat was a place for disease, and the stench was unbearable. The Duke of Wellington ordered the moat to be drained, and un derneath the slime of centuries they found human skeletons and bottles tossed from the windows by Tudor, soldiers. . A frightening place ? THE lofty stone halls now are lit with fluorescent tubes and some of thd dank ness is ? taken away with the warm breath of central heating Yet even so the Tower is a frightening place. For example, there are the six ravens, hideously ugly, sulky, overgrown crows, which are 'sworn-in' and on the strength at the Tower. According to legend dating back to Charles n, should ever the ravens leave the Tower of London the Tower walls will crumble and with it the British Empire. So those beady eyed, fat - birds are treated with .loving care. Their wings arc care fully clipped to stop them from flying away. . Smug, arrogant, and contented, they strut and waddle around the grounds, satisfied that on their health depends the future of the Empire. « The Yeomen, better known as the Beefeaters, in their superb red regalia lead mobs of wide eyed tourists round the ancient buildings, to the Crown Jewels, to the Bloody Tower, where the two little princes were murdered and to the armory. As I walked past the White , Tower a Beefeater was saying: 'Nch see that window up there? That's where they put Lady Jane Grey. And do yer want to know where they took off her head?' Naturally we did., 'Why right here,' and he pointed to a brass plate almost nt the feet of two American women. They gasped, and we all crowded in close for a better view, as if in the hope of find ing some last trace of the great lady. 'But of course most executions took place up there on Tower Hill, and for a good show they'd get 2000 spectators.' said the Beefeater cheerily. 'And those what got executed made it their custom to give the executioner a good tip, five or ten pound — a bribe, if you like, to make sure the blade was sharp.' The Beefeater was a master ol drama. He lowered his voice and said ominously: 'Yes, they paid to die. . . . And before you go home take a look at the execu tion block in the White Tower. See how sharp the blade is.' We saw. But It's a melancholy business to look at the gibbets, the axes, the instruments of tor ture, and think of Anne Boleyn, Sir Walter Raleigh, and the countless others who died there. Macauley said of it: 'In truth there is no sadder spot on earth.' But Londoners no longer look on the Tower with dread, they do so with pride, for it is . a superb piece of British show manship. The ceremonies of the guard haven't changed for more than 700 years, and it is exciting to be shown over the place by a real Beefeater. At least they were real Beef eaters once. It's 300 years now ?since the Grand Duke of Tus cany writing home from Lon don remarked on the gigantic quantities of beef eaten by the Yeomen. There's not much beef in the Tower now, and this week an other tradition crumbled. The Beefeaters held a meeting, joined a union, and became members of the civil service. 'Our beefeaters are now civil servants in fancy dress,' moaned one newspaper. Civil servants, maybe, but they will be mighty busy come next June. A ROYAL salute being fired from the Tower of London.
