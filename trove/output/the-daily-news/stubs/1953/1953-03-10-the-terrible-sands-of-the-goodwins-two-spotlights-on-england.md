When the boatmen let her go she bounced crazily down the skids, gathered speed and hit tne water witn a mighty smack — the pro pellers were racing and we were away. The crew of 10, all old sea-wise fishermen, had faces like beaten canvas, a curious contrast to the brilliant yellow of their oilskins. Coxswain Fred Upton, e man with silver medal and bar, pointed to the Goodwin Sands straight ahead — those terrible, sucking, shifting, sand-bars that make up an irregular mass 10 miles long off the Kentish coast. Tradition says that in 1097 the Goodwins were a pleasant English island until one terrible night when the tides and winds were running in that potent combination as they did a month ago, the island with all its inhabitants disappeared under water. Through the centuries these oozing sands have been a menace to all ships from lofty galleons to steaming tramps. , "PVEN in modern times ships foun- der there, sometimes at a rate of five a year. You can see them now, their masts and funnels sticking above the water in clusters like shoals of > fish. Fred Upton, his hands on the wheel, said: "You get to know these sands just like you would the streets of your town. "But in a gale the seas pound down from all directions. Their power is terrific — I've seen them break up a ship, dvernight, like a bis cuit. "Once on to the sands they have to get off by the next tide or they're done. The rushing water sucks away the sand from either end of the hull, the weight bears down, then they just crack in half." Fred began telling the story of the Agen, a French freighter, which ran aground on South Goodwin Bank last year. TT was 10.30 on A a dark squally night and a furious sea was running. The roc kets were fired from the headland, the old bell at the boathouse rang and the men came running to the life boat. In five minutes they were on their way. The boat cut her way through waves, the men hanging on for life, the water often up to their necks — it was as if the boat were a submarine. It took four hours to find and get to the Agen. She was in two pieces with the crew huddled up in the front half, and 30ft. of water in between. Fred Upton tried 14 times to get close, but every time the rollers threatened to smash them against the jagged metal of the Agen. At 6.15 a.m. by careful manoeuvring with the engines, Upton got the boat between the halves of the Agen. TTIE 37 crew members slid down ropes into the boat. All except the captain — the wreck was his tragedy and he wouldn't leave. Upton had to go back three times before the captain would change his mind. Any time a sunken wreck could tear the bottom out of that lifeboat, an unseen wave broadside could send the crew members flying, irretrievably lost, into the water as it did to one crew a few weeks ago.
