KEITH DUNSTAN, from a lifeboat, sees — TERROR SANDS OF GOODWINS LONDON. — March has gone — an unpredictable time in Britain, when white flecks cover the sea, when waves crash against the white cliffs and throw spray 1000' feet into the air. < THE coastlines of England and Ireland, an in credible 6000 miles, are savage enough to make one think they were de signed specially to slash the hulls of ships. The gales come and the perilous job of the life- boatman is never-ending, fighting his way to wreck ed ships, pulling airmen out of the water, rescu ing disabled pleasure launches. Last year the lifeboats were out 651 times, but this last month has been particularly treacherous. In 14 days the boats were out 34 times and eight lifeboatmen were drowned. I have just been out in the Wnlmer lifeboat on the Kentish coast not far from Dover. The lifeboat was moun ted high on a steep pebbly beach When the boat men let her go she bounced crazily down the skids, gathered speed and hit the water with a mighty smnck — the pro pellers .were racing and we were away. The crew of 10. all old sea-wise fishermen, had faces like beaten canvas, a curious contrast with the brilliant yellow of their oilskins. The coxswain. Fred Up ton. a man with silver medal and bar, pointed to the Goodwin Sands straight ahead — those terrible, sucking, shifting, sand - bars that make up an Irregular mass 10 miles long off the Kentish coast. Tradition says that In 1097 the Goodwins were a pleasant English island until one terrible night when the tides and winds were running In that do- tcnt combination as they did a month ago, the is land with all its Inhabi tants disappeared under water. Now through the cen turies. these oozing sands have been a menace to all ships from lofty galleons to steaming tramps. Even in modern times ships founder there sometimes at a rate of five a year. You can see them now. their masts and funnels sticking above the water, in clusters like shoals of fishes. Fred upton, his hands on the wheel, said: "You pet to know these sands just like you would the streets of your town. "But In a gale the seas pound down from all di rections. Their power is terrific — I've seen them break up a ship, over night, like a biscuit. "Once on the Sands they have to get off by the next tide or they're done. The rushing water sucks away the sand from either end of the hull, the weight bears down, then they just crack in half." One crewman was blinking the signal light to the coastguards and down below the radio was crackling and a voice came through the loud speaker: "There's a bit of fog on the river. What's It like on the Goodwins?" Fred began telling the story of the Agen. a French freighter which ran aground on South Goodwin Bank lnat year It was 10.30 on a dark squally night and a furi ous sea was running, The rockets were fired from the headland, the old bell at the boat house rang and the men came run ning to the life boat. In five minutes they were on their wny. There was rain and mist. The boat cut her way through the waves, the men hang ing on for life, the water often up to their necks — it was if the boat were a submarine. IT took four hours to find and get to the Agen. She was in two pieces. The crew huddled up in the front half, and 30 feet of water in between, Fred Upton tried 14 times to get close but every time the rollers threatened to smash them against the jagged metal of the Agen. At 6.15 a.m., by careful manoeuvring with the en gines, Upton got the boat between the halves of the Agen. "It was like going into the Underground." he said. The 37 crew members slid down ropes Into the boat. All except the cap tain: the wreck was his tragedy and he wouldn't leave. Upton had to go back three times before the captain would change his mind. As Fred Upton, very re luctantly, gave me a few details of these exploits. I was overcome with ad miration for him and all lifeboatmen. They are the real seamen and surely among the bravest In the world. Any time a sunken wreck .could tear the bot tom out of that lifeboat, an unseen wave broadside could send the crew mem bers flying, Irretrievably lost, Into the water as it did to one crew a few weeks ago. Upton and his crew, all volunteers, have never re fused to go out, no matter what the conditions. And one curious thing — Fred can't swim. Wouldn't make any difference, he says, if he went overboard —not in those Goodwins. SHIPS sunk on the Goodwins, seen from the Walmer lifeboat.
