Londoners love our flowers By KEITH DUNSTAN LONDON. — One of the greatest attractions of the London crowds flocking to Kew Gardens this spring is the Australian house. IT is a sleek affair, made of alumin ium and glass, just a year old this week .It Is packed with ' more than 500 varieties of Australian plant life. When the Kew garden ers first worked on the strange plants from Aus tralia they gave them the richest soil and nurtured them like babies. But It was no good; the plants wilted and looked sickly. Yet it was clear enough. Australian plants didn't want tender treatment; they wanted a desert. Now In the new house, with Its great glass panes angled for every second of sun light, the plants are in arid, stony soil, and they get little water. Natur ally, they thrive and the display is marvellous. This week the Austra lian house is filled with the noady scent of bo- ronia and the wattle is in bloom. The British enthusiasm for Australian plants has had some interesting re sults. For example, there was the mystery of the missing hides. The hides came here from Australia, and when they cracked open the cases the importers found there had been some funny business. Some of the hides had been stolen and the thieves had shov elled in earth to take their place. The mystery was this— where were the hides stolen: in England or Australia? In the earth there were a few odd pieces of plant life. Detectives took the plants and the earth to Kew and the scientists there told them in a min ute. Australians stole thfe hides. When you go to Kew they much prefer to talk of the scientific side of things. It is the world's greatest treasure store house of plants. They have 6 million type specimens on file and every yehr 8000 packages of seeds go out to gardens all over the world. They are in touch with every botanical garden in Australia, constantly ex changing seeds and infor mation. Kew has had much to do with the growing of every crop you can think of, and particularly rub ber. It was the English in 1876 who smuggled 70,000 rubber seeds out of Bra zil. They grew and flour ished in The hothouses of Kew. These same plants started the rubber indus try in Malaya and be came the mother to all the rubber of the Pacific. Kew is an information centre for the world and the queries come in at a rate of 10,000 a year. Sometimes they get the darndest questions. A packet came in the other day — a buttercup at tached to a daisy stalk, with the question: "Is this a rarity?"
