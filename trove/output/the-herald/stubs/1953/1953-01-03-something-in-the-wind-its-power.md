SOMETHING IN THE WIND; IT'S POWER By KEITH DUN ST AN LONDON. — The cold sea wind sweeps the spray across the beach, moans around the cottages and makes men from the Hebrides to Cornwall button tight their coats. Hp HE wind on I Britain s west X coast has a bitterness all of its own but the day may be coming when it will pro vide 10 per cent, ol the nation's power and save four million tons of coal a year. The wind experts have chosen 70 sites. On these they will raise the world's >most powerful windmills. The mills will be long, lean, gaunt like emu heads, some 250 feet high with propellers that will thrash the air with a 100 foot sweep. A big mill will cost £50,000, a 'chain of them £75 million, but they will be cheap at the price. Lonely, unattended, burn ing no fuel, feeding no gases Into the nlr, each of them will pour 1000 kilo- I watts into the main grid. And the electricity will come in at almost half the price of the steam-made stuff. The poets and senti mentalists love the idea. You see the use of wind for power is a fundamen tal, romantic thing, a re turn to nature. Man back through his tory for thousands of years, has used the wind to power his sailing ships, to grind his corn, to pump water and these "wind machines" were things of beauty. But for the occa sional windmill on the lonely sheep station, the age of steam killed all that and this is a return to simplicity. Yet the wind Is fickle and flighty, one day a gale, the next just a breeze too soft to disturb the ash on your cigarette. Unless you can get an average wind speed around 15 m.p.h., as far as power-making is concerned you are wasting your time. The wind experts are now charting all the hill tops and it's a slow, deadly slow business. They have found that the windiest spots are all on the west coast, the Orkneys, the Hebrides, the Scottish west coast and from Wales down to Cornwall. In the Orkneys, for six months on end, the wind keeps up an average of 27 mph and even for the sum mer months it rarely drops below 20 mph. ? FOR some months now a stream lined 100 kilo watt mill has been operating there and soon another machine 100 feet high will go up at Myndd Anelog on the Lleyn Pen insula, North Wales. Curiously enough, the local people arc choking with rage about it. The windmill is a slen der, beautiful enough thing, but the Welsh be lieve the 500 ft. skyline of Myndd Anelog Is more beautiful than anything made by man. Furthermore it is a tour ist attraction, a place that would be helped hardly at all by an un-Celtic, whin ing, mechanical gadget. The British Electricity Authority is learning as It goes. The windmills will get bigger and bigger until they are massive affairs capable of producing 3000 kilowatts. The wind experts believe they are on to something really big. Imagine 500 windmills on 500 hilltops all whirring the music of wealth and power. AN EXPERIMENTAL power - windmill- like this loill be built on a hilltop in Wales. It is 100 ft. high, but later wind mills will be bigger. The Welsh aren't pleased; they sa.y it spoils the skyline.
