Famous Murder Cases In Victoria After 20 years in the homicide squad, a former detective tells KEITH DUNSTAN of his experiences as he recalls "That was the most fascinating murder I ever had anything to do with. It had everything. It all started one night in 1923. There was a ring on the phone at Russell Street ..." Heavyweight Serg eant McGuffie leaned back in his chair, and started to muse about the murders he had been as sociated with since he started in the force 37 years ago, He is well able to do this, because last week he retired to his rose garden. He is now feeling the effect of those years. More than 20 in the homicide squad, a sec tion of the force where work never finishes. Many a time he went for a whole week with out sleep— not even giving him self a minute to change his clothes. Nevertheless, he always look ed smart on the job. He in variably wore a rose or a car nation in his buttonhole, a homberg hat, and, of course, he carried an umbrella. Apart from roses he was a student of forensic medicine, and often he was able to give country doctors a tip regard ing a post mortem examina tion. His chief claim to fame was a wonderful capacity for searching for detail. 'Buried In Hills' BUT we were talking about that murder in 1923. Nurse Hannah Mitchell rang the police and said she had just shot her divorced husband, an Italian named Bonfiglio. The police rushed out to the house. Nurse Mitchell was there, but Bonfiglio had gone They found a trail of blood from a window to where Bon figlio had caught a taxi to hospital. McGuffie interviewed Bon figlio at St. Vincent's Hos pital, and, expecting him to die any moment, took a state ment. Bonfiglio said Nurse Mitchell had performed an illegal operation on a girl, Bertha Couglin. After the operation she died and they had to bury her up in the hills at Coldstream. Sgt. McGufflie said: "A sim- ple phone call was now turn- ng into a double murder. We went to Coldstream, found the spot, but the body had gone! "Now we didn't know what to do. For months investiga tions were at a standstill. Then one night we received a report that a man had seen someone throwing a coffin-like object into the Yarra at An derson Street bridge. "Next morning I went down into the Yarra in a diving suit. To my disgust I found that coffin-like object was a motor bike that, had been stolen the night before. But I searched further, and right underneath where the motor bike had been was a sack. Inside it was the body of Bertha Couglin. | "This was a most amazing SGT. McGUFFIE coincidence. That sack had been there at least three months. "We charged Nurse Mitchell with murder. She alleged in court that the girl had oper ated on herself, and that Bon figlio had attacked her with a stiletto. She was acquitted. "Bonfiglio subsequently re covered and died a few years ago. Nurse Mitchell also died a few years ago." Body In Mine THERE was another very interesting case in 1940. The policfe re ceived word that a body had been found in an old min ing shaft at Miner's Rest. Sgt. McGuffie and Superin tendent F. W. Lyon went to investigate. There was 80ft. of water in the shaft, and it took three days to pump It dry. McGuffie went down the shaft in a bosun's chair and found the torso of a man. There were five tons of mud at the bottom. For several days McGuffie worked below, send ing the mud up, while Supt. Lyon directed sifting opera tions. They found the skull, Jaws and even loose teeth. The teeth were sent to Ballarat, and a dentist was able to identify the man as George Davidson, who had disappear ed 18 months before. McGuffle said: "I think the man was murdered. In the shaft near the body we found a large bar of Iron and a piece of wire. Tills apparently had been tied round the neck of the body. "There were also some strands of wool from a sweater in the mud similar to strand that were caught on a barbed wire fence near Ballarat the night he was last seen. We had our theories, but the mys tery was never solved. "I had the luckiest escape of my life. Just after we fin ished the shaft collapsed, and 10 tons of earth and rock dropped down into where I bad been working." Plans Misfired THERE was another ex traordinary case about 15 years ago, when Syd McGuffie and Detective Seager arrested five men for having conspired to rob a bank. They discovered it by acci dent. They were questioning a man, who protested his innocence. To illustrate his point, he cited the case of the bank at Clifton Hill which they had decided not to rob, Here is the story. Five men were in it — a bank clerk, a builder, a garage proprietor, a motor mechanic, and an ex- policeman. The manager was away, and the bank clerk was living at the bank. They had hoped to clear £40.000. On the night the robbery was to take place the garage proprietor could not get any oxy-acetylene cylinders. The bank manager came back, and the robbery fell through. Four of the men were sen tenced. and the ex-pollceman was acquitted. Leonski Case SGT. McGUFFIE also played a big part In the Leonski murder investigations in 1942. Private Edward Joseph Leonski, an American soldier, was hanged for the murder of three Australian girls. The trial was conducted by ,the American Army, but be cause of his knowledge of the case Sgt. McGuffle assisted in the prosecution. This is what he said about it:- "Now there was a court case for you. The Americans did a magnificent job. The chief prosecutor was Colonel Hayford O. Emwal — the finest prosecutor I ever heard. Judge Book was very good in his day, but I think Colonel Emwal had the edge." There are countless mur ders that Syd. McGuffle could tell you about — enough to fill a book. He could tell you about the time he was a member of the "Terrible Ten" In 1916— a nickname they won from criminals when as plain clothes men they cleaned up Melbourne's parks. He'll tell you about this and other things if you pull him up one day while he's pruning the roses out at Essendon.
