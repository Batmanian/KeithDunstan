THE STARS PLEADED FOR JOBS KEITH DUNSTAN'S FILM WORLD NEW YORK. - Cecil Blount de Mille has done most things. He has parted the Red Sea, burned Rome and crumbled the Temple of Gaza, but all his past achievements seem puny beside "The Greatest Show on Earth." Mr de mille has always been Holly wood's showman, the man who could do things bigger, better and more brassy than anyone else. New York critics have looked upon his colossal epics such as "Samson and Delilah" as little more than gaudy fakes. Yet 'Samson and Delilah" con sistently made more money than most other films around the city and de Mille commented: "When ever I make a film the re viewers' opinion of the public goes down 10 per cent." But now for the first time in years de Mille has the praise of the critics as well as the public. "The Greatest Show on Earth" is an extraordinary hit. probably the biggest since the war years. The big star of this show is Ringling Bros. & Barnum and Bailey, America's greatest circus, which has a staff of 1450 and for 65 years has been putting on better shows than Holly wood ever dreamed out. . The extraordinary per sonality of this circus plus the ballyhoo of de Mille all seem to mesh and there's no doubt the film will be doing the rounds for years. Work on it started three years ago, and immedi ately , a feeling of excite ment and anticipation went through Hollywood. Everybody wanted to get into the act. , Jimmy Stewart. who rates 150.0U0 dollars a pic ture, pleaded with de Mille to take him in at a barely nominal salary. He accept ed the minor role of a clown, just so that he would have the honor of being in the big show. But Betty Hutton was after the top role of a trapeze artist and she out- Milled de Mille. She sent him a floral creation, 18 ft. wide, with a model of Betty herself sitting on a trapeze made of orchids. It cost her something " like 1000. dollars. . but it helped swing the deal. "I figured you gotta be practical." she said. You've got to hand it to de Mille. The film cost a million dollars, but with shrewd staging he gave it a publicity build-up worth three times that amount. He announced that he didn't want stand-ins, and all those in the cast would have to be real circus per formers. Betty Hutton and Cornel Wilde had to learn the trapeze. Gloria Graham practised putting her head under an eiepnant's foot, and Dorothy Lamour learnt the tricks of an iron jaw woman. Then to show the pub lic he wasn't fooling, de Mille put on a show under canvas with the circus in Philadelphia. The news papermen of America were there plus 10.000 excited Philadelphians. I They saw Betty Hutton climb to a platform 40 feet up and do a somersault in mid-air with amazing pre cision. They saw her swinging by the ankles to be caught by a good look ing young man from Ring- ling Bros. Philadelphia yelled its heart out that night and when she came down she said to the Press: "How about that, fellows? Pretty damn good, huh!" It was. too. Poor Gloria Graham was nearly scared out of her wits. She lay on her back while an elephant lowered its foot almost on to her face. She was almost hit by the elephant's foot as she rolled clear. She mounted the animal and just had time to grab the trappings when it reared up on its hind legs. But the person who really puts on a show in chis film is Dorothy La mour. Actually it is her comeback, and, for the first time in years, she wears a sarong, rolls her eyes, rolls her hips, turns on an honest hula. She also rides an ele phant and. believe me, when you have seen Dottle Lamour take a piece of chewing gum out of her mouth and stick it on the back of an elephant, you've seen something. The story of the film is a little absurd, but at the same time it is an actual circus documentary. You find out how they feed 1450 people three times a day, how they solve their squabbles, how they move from town to town and how they put up their mam moth, sprawling canvas ;ent,s. It's fascinating stuff BETTY HUTTON — no stand-in allowed.
