Bin., and his face is like the setting sun. His little eyes look over- bright and nls cheeks puff out as if he were blowing an Invisible trumpet. As the screen's master mystery director he delights In scaring people, but he also does It Just for fun. For example, there Is the famous case when he and Joseph Cotten stepped into a crowded lift at the St. Regis. Hitchcock in a muttered whisper: . "Rotten luck. Didn't know the gun was loaded." Cotten: "God! Do you suppose she's dead!" Hitchcock: "Yes steady old man!" The crowd In the lift stiffened up. .and became quite silent. They strained to pick up the words. Hitchcock (low shocked voice): "Terrible hole In her neck where the bullet came but— frightful mess- made my shoes stick to the floor coming down the hall, you know . . ." Woman: "Let me out at the next floor . . . PLEASE." ."A" Hitchcock thought this little incident one of his greatest successes, a perfect piece of suspense. "It jolly . well served the woman right for eavesdropping," he said. Hitchcock became Eng land's greatest screen director in the late 30's, and his films are now classics which come up for redistribution every other year. His two greats were: "The Lady Vanishes" and "The 39 Steps." James Thurber claims to have seen "The Lady" 13 times, and Orson Welles 11 times. You don't have to go as often as that, but par for a genuine Hitchcock fan is around seven times. Each film has one ego- tistical little trade-mark. He always appears some where in the cast. In his current film, "Strangers In The Train," you will see him for just a moment, puffing along the platform with a bull fiddle. Mr Hitchcock explained: "You see there is a sequence in this thing. In 'Spellbound' I carried a violin, in 'Stage Fright' a 'cello, and in 'Strangers' a bull fiddle. I intend to proceed with the wood winds and if all goes well eventually I will be en titled to wield a conduc tor's baton. "Toughest picture to squeeze into was 'Life boat.' I was the man in the ad., before and after, both were Hitchcock." .? It was little tricks such as these that lifted Hitchcock from the unknown race of directors to the notoriety of a -top star. He gave a new powerful style to the thriller. The average mys tery film starts off well then steadily goes to pieces. It is so easy to get the characters into tough spots, but it's hard to get them out again. Hollywood has never been able to make good mystery films, and for this reason they are usually made by the Grade B de partments for quickies. There' it doesn't matter if they are bad, for they are cheap anyway. Hitchcock always had the Ingenuity and the imagination to make a mystery hum from begin ning to end: He builds up his suspense with artful tricks. In "Strangers in the Train" there is a tennis match. You see the heads of the crowd go back and forth, back and forth, all except one. The head of the villain remains dead steady looking at nobody else but the hero tennis player. Quite terrifying!
