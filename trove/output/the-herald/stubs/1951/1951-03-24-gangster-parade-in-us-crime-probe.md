Gangster parade in U.S. crime probe From — -| RANDAL HEYMANSON Herald Special Cor respondent in New York. NEW YORK , Friday.— Even in America there has been nothing like it before . Housewives neglect their duties to spend hours of slightly morbid excitement watching a procession of underworld leaders and their girl friends flicker across the television screens. , Their husbands find excuses to slip out of their offices to neighbor ing bars, where patrons watch the telecasts with an intentness normally reserved for major sporting events. The ostensible purpose of the U.S. Senators, who are holding hearings in the Federal Court Building, New York, is to investigate or ganised crime. Actually they are staging the greatest show on earth, and they know it. Barely 60 people, apart from officials, can be crowded into the smallish room in which the committee meets. Many are cinema and television cameramen. Their lenses are a window through which mil lions' watch every detail of the extraordinary performance. The three Senators ON" the bench sit three senators. They ask few ..questions, :of the . wit nesses who - are brought "one by one, to a table beneath them. Examination is con ducted by Mr Halley, their highly competent counsel, whose questions come with the rapidity and the ruthlessness of machine-gun fire. Witness es are almost completely at the mercy of their tormentors; their lawyers can do little but offer whispered advice; The three Senators are al most as interesting to the ob server as the underworld bosses, police chiefs, bookmak ers and government agents who appear before them. Senator Kefauver, chairman of the committee, is a lanky, youngish Southerner, obviously fair-minded and sincere. His colleagues originally wonder- . ed why he should have started an investigation which "of fered no percentage"; for the connection between polities ' and the underworld is notori ous, and Senator Kefauver seemed to.be jeopardising his future. They were ' wrong; . overnight he has become a national figure. Senator O'Connor ' is a typical Irish politician, , shrewd and tough. The steeli- ness . of his eyes belies his quick smile. He is watchful and non-committal. Senator Tobey, a Republi can, is the "ham" of the com mittee. A dapper little man who takes himself very seri ously, his one idea is to steal the limelight. His notions of judicial fairness are rudi mentary, and his intelligence" seems limited. His mind is furnished with the politician's usual stock-in- trade of trite tags and hack neyed quotations. He parades his schoolboy Latin -with great portentousness; he makes atrocious puns. Once, in a supreme bid for the leading role, he expressed the fear that gangsters had so undermined the foundations of the republic that it could never be rebuilt, and, dab bing his eyes with a hand kerchief, he intoned in a quavering voice: "Solution there is none "Save in the G.olden Rule of Christ alone." - Cause of Senator Tobey's distress was Frank Costello, a king-maker feared and court ed by politicians, a czar who exacts unquestioning obed ience from the underworld. This fabulous figure, who is reputed to rule an empire vaster than anything Caponc and his contemporaries ever envisaged and whose methods are as subtle as theirs were crude and vio lent, was subjected to such remorseless questioning by Mr Halley that after three days of it he copied the tac tics, of Gromyko and Malik and walked out of the room. So poorly equipped ONE was sur prised to find such a man so poorly equipped to de fend himself. Mr Halley soon reduced' his testimony to a tangle of self - contradictory .statements. The more he tried to explain away, the contradic tions, the more deeply involved in theih did he become. His voice was rough and throaty, his vocabulary that of a dock worker. Lacking edu cation and verbal dexterity; his manner was that of a schoolboy questioned by his headmaster for some mis demeanor. His face was sul len and expressionless; his favorite phrases: "I don't re member," and "I mighta or I might not" — anything but a . direct answer. When asked to explain how he said one thing under oath one day and its "direct opposite another day, he replied in geniously: "You ask me to re- fresha de memory: well I re fresh it." Examination of Costello established pretty definitely that bookmakers looked to him for instructions and that he was the man to see whenever political appoint- mcnts were in question. It was even suggested I that to become Mayor of New York one had to have his approval. Other witnesses were less powerful and 'their testimony less productive. They inclu ded Joe Adonis, reputed asso ciate of America's most ruth less killers, . hard, an'ogant and handsome, fa'ultlessly tail ored, bored and openly con temptuous of the whole pro- ceedings; Frank Erickson, one of New York's leading book makers, who looked like the president of a bank, his gen eral air of" family respectability heightened by an expensive blue pin-stripe suit; Charles Llpsky, a Republican party boss, suave and adroit; George Levy, who admitted curious transactions with Costello in volving large sums. Quick to think on his "feet, Levy seemed to enjoy the battle of wits with the com mittee. ' Only Adonis conform ed to the Hollywood picture of an underworld boss. He out- Bogartcd Bogart. The public, however, recog nised an identifiable type in Virginia Hill Hauser, former girl-friend of "Bugsy" Sie- gel, notorious killer who was murdered : in the Hollywood house he rented for her. True fiery tempered lady, who admitted to spending £6000 on a six-week vacation, and claimed to have won large sums at the races, described the photographers as "bums," punched the jaw of a woman reporter who sought to inter view her, and kicked a news paperman in the shins. Her testimony, however, re vealed little of the ways of the underworld, save its ex travagant spending and strict social standards. Siegel, she said, had de clared she was no lady be- oause she hit a girl in a fashionable night club. They had a proper fight, and she was so mad that she went to Paris to let him cool off. The Senators were no match for her. When they tried to establish a connec- tion between her gangster friends and politics, she in dignantly retorted that she knew nothing of such non sense as politics and wouldn't waste time iisten- ing to talk about them. They are obviously out to "get" Costello. One schooled in the tradi tions of English law cannot, however, jvatch the operation of .these congressional commit tees without misgiving. Nor mal constitutional safeguards are swept aside and, under threat of gaol for contempt, witnesses are virtually re quired to supply evidence against themselves on which criminal proceedings cah be launched against them. Moreover, these committees seem to be either ignorant of or indifferent to the laws of evidence. The accused has no right of reply, and charges which are no more than hear say may, be made against him and broadcast in the Press. Even the Costellos of this world have the right to a fair trial: but in America trial' by committee and in the news papers is fast replacing the time-honored traditions of English jurisprudence.
