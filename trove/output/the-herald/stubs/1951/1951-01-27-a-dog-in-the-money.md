A dog in the money iiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiifiiiiiiiiiiiiiiiw Keith Dunst tin's Hollywood NEW YORK. — The best way to make money in Holly- wood is to buy a cast-off dog for a few dollars, train it to do as many tricks as the average human being, and then sit back and watch it earn 1000 dollars a week. That, briefly, is the story of Lassie. This remarkable dog is now one of the most suc- cessful male stars In Holly- wood and the best paid dog in the world. It js better to use the word male here and now because Lassie is not a female dog. He recently finished making "The Painted Hill," and now he has made six films— all In color. Since he came to fame seven years ago collies have risen from twelfth to third place in popularity among pure-bred pets in American households. There is a Lassie radio show which is broadcast on a 163 station hook-up every Satur- day afternoon, Eric Knight's story, "Lassie Come Home," has been trans lated into 10 languages, and has remained a fop seller in juvenile literature since 1940. His owner and trainer, Rudd B. Weatherwax. has just written a book "The Story of Lassie," which is' selling well on the news stands. Lassie was born on June 8, 1941. His owner named him Pal. He was rather a grubby, disobedient little dog. lie chased motor cars and refused to obey any order. His owner passed him on to Mr Weather- wax as a settlement for a debt of 10 dollars. Weatherwax, after much hard work, taught Paul to do some tricks, but he much pre ferred to chase cars. When Weatherwax heard MGM was looking for a collie to play the lead in "Lassie Come Home." JOSE FERRER as Cyrano he really went to work, Pal eventually turned into a bril liant pupil and he won a five- year contract. THE boy wonder, Stanley Kramer, has done it again. The critics are all acclaiming his production of "Cyrano de Bergerac," and they say it is certain to . win an academy award of some kind. Cyrano was a dashing 16th century soldier, poet and swordsman, but he was han dicapped by an unsightly nose of prodigious proportions, Fer rer's nose for the film cost about 5000 dollars. Stanley Kramer looked over 40 differ ent designs . before he de cided on a great turned up affair, about twice the size of a normal nose. The making of It was a big problem. Conventional putty was ruled out because it is Impossible to taper it in visibly down to the skin. Even tually, Kramer found the answer in a compound a vinyl plastic. Applied with alcohol It blen ded perfectly into the skin. However, a new nose, padded inside with sponge rubber, had to be made for every day's shooting. This was a dally eight-hour job for the "nose designer," In the 'words of Cyrano " 'tis a rook, a crag, a cape — nay rather a peninsular. It pre cedes me by a quarter of an hour.
