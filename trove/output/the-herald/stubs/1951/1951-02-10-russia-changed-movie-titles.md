Keifh Dunstan's Hollywood Russia changed movie titles NEW YORK. — The film industry is complaining bitterly to the State De partment because two pre-war Ameri can films are now showing in Moscow without the prior knowledge or consent of Hollywood. The films are "Mr Smith Goes To Wash ington" which goes under the Russian title "The Senator," and "Mr Deeds Goes to Town," changed to "In The Grip of The Dollar." Moscow is filming them in their original English versions with Russian sub-titles. An announcement at the begin ning of each picture, accord ing to the New York Herald Tribune, explains the pictures are war booty captured by the Red Army in Berlin in 1945. Frank Capra made both pic tures for Columbia and they were popular throughout the world. However, Hollywood knew their propaganda value 'and Columbia has not released them outside America since the war. "Mr Smith Goes To Wash ington," filmed in ,1939. starred James Stewart . and Jean Arthur. It was the story of a young Senator's fight against a corrupt legislator, who was backed by the press and a powerful political machine. Gary Cooper and Jean Arthur were the stars of "Mr Deeds Goes To Town." Re leased in 1936, it was the story of a disillusioned man who tried to give away 20 million dollars to luckless farmers. Hollywood has asked Dean Acheson to protest to Moscow, but it is unlikely anything will come of it.- No copyright con ventions or film agreements exist between the U.S. and Rursia. However. During that time his family turned against him, including his own son. His mother died thinking him a traitor, and still he kept his secret and provided valuable information for the Government. Hollywood is desperately looking round for ways to at tract people Into picture theatres and the great hope for 1951 is theatre-television. Several electronic manufac turers now have on sale tele vision equipment which will project images to the size necessary for motion picture screens. The idea Is this: Hollywood intends t.o gain the , exclusive rights to stage shows, important spectacles and tele cast them straight into pic ture theatres. They believe these on-the- spot shows plus better feature length films will help to fill the theatres. Already there have been many successful tryouts. Last year football fans saw all Chi cago's 10 critical matches on theatre-TV. In two theatres away from the cold, mud and congestion of the football ground, the fans sat back in air-conditioned comfort. Yet all the atmosphere of the American game was there. Rival brass bands were in the theatre, cheer leaders coached the audiences into a barrack ing frenzy and attendants ambled down the aisles selling pop-corn, chewing gum and hot dogs. The theatres were packed. They saw a film as well as the game and the football pro moters who received a share of the profits decided theatre- TV was here to stay.
