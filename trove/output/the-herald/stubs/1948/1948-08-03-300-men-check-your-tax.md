300 men check your tax cent. Interest.— KEITH DUNSTAN. THIS morning 70 mail bags bulging with income tax returns, cheques and pleading letters were carried into the Taxa tion Office in Lonsdale Street. July 31 has passed; so if you are a 'wage-earner, your return should be in the building along with 850,000 others. For days how the 11 Inquiry boxes on the ground floor nave not been empty. Some of the In quirers are indignant people. A frequent sort of complaint Is: "Mrs woof next door earns twice as much money as me and I pay twice the tax she does. . . ." Inquiry officers who withstand the indignation usually point out that it Is no good being annoyed with them. They have to pay taxes, too, and, after all, they are just employes. There Is Invariably a simple ex planation. It turns out, for In stance, that Mrs Woof had six de pendants and the other person none. But everyone is not Indignant. Usually the Inquirers ask simple questions about forms, rebates and refunds. All those Income tax returns that come In have to be sorted, filed, checked and given a num ber. The work Is Immense. Three hundred assessors go through all forms. Each man Is a specialist in some Industry or profession. He has to work out the taxable Income on each form and look for anomalies. As many as 30 per rent, of forms are made out In correctly. This means that thou sands of extra letters have to be sent out. Some people charge too much for expenses, and others make ex traordinary claims for deductions. One man asked for a rebate for "horses killed for rations." Many Refunds AFTER the assessors hnve worked out the taxable Income another team checks the tax that has to be paid. And so the assessments go out. About 65 per cent, of wage-earners receive a refund each year. Taxation men. apart from hav ing to understand the workings of every type of business, have to know the Income Tax Act almost by heart. It is a volume of 200 closely-printed pages. It does not do to attempt to evade taxation. There Is a special investigation branch which each year brings in from tax-evading Victorians an average of £300,000. One inspector Is so good at track ing down "dodgers that he Is known in the department as the "Red Terror." These special investigators get information from their own re cords, from the Titles Office, from company records, dividend returns and such Institutions as the Land Tax office. Sometimes careless conversation in public puts them on the track of a tax-evader. Some people write to the de partment and give Information about people who have not de clared earnings. One man. when he did this, said: "Don't tell Jack what I have done. If he knew It would ruin a life-long friendship." Another, some years ago, gave In formation about his brother Just to collect a reward that was offered. For those who toss In bed at night worrying about the £10,000 buried in the woodshed, all Is not lost. The Investigators will listen sympathetically to voluntary de clarations.
