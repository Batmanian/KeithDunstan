New jobs for old stors Keith Dunstan's HOLLYWOOD NEW YORK. — Hollywood has been kind, often too kind, to its great stars whose names are plastered on the billboards of a hundred nations. But like prize fight- ing or running a speakeasy it is a precarious business. An actor's popularity can fade overnight and that is the position in Hollywood now. Be cause of rising costs and fall ing business, the industry has been put on a diet, and there are many big name stars on the unemployed lists. The studios Just cannot af ford to support them any more and there is nobody quite so pathetic as the down-and-out actor trying to keep up with a memory. Yet an independent pro ducer this week had an idea. Hollywood has always been a tougn place for visitors— the big studios hate them almost as much as television. This is understandable, because a motion picture costs as much as £12,000 a day to make. Visitors running round the set, popping in front of cam eras, demanding autographs and talking their way into sound tracks, can wreck a day's production, This producer plans to solve the problem by put ting grandstand seats around a sound stage at one of , the studios not In pro duction. He will use nut- . of-work actors, directors and technicians at a minimum cost and with no film in the camera he will show visitors just how films arc made. Small charge, of course. He said: "I can bring in personalities who will knock their eyes out. They're still big to the fans outside, but some haven't had a job in months. They'll bury their pride in order to eat. As for me, I think I can make more money than I could by turn ing out a quickie western." ? "RUT not everyone wants to stay in Alms. Alice Paye has not made a film for some years, and ever since the end of the war 20th Century Fox has been pleading with her to come back. Fox last week sent her the script for a film named "Jack Pot," in the hope that it would lure her back. But no, she is off to Europe next week with her husband, Phil Harris. She has been married to him for nine years now, and she is one of those who believe that film careers and marriage don't mix. ATAYBE you have never heard of Jackie Robinson. He doesn't get into the world news like President Truman, Mr. Vishinsky or Bine Crosby, but he is one of trie best- known personalities in America and the hero of all Brooklyn. Before the war he was a great football star, one of the best baseball players on the west coast and an athlete capable of leaping 25 ft. 6 in. During the second world war he won a commission in the army, and after his discharge the Brooklyn Dodgers signed him up to play baseball. Tills was natural enough, but there was a hitch— Jackie was a coal black negro, and no negro nad ever played in the major league. Furthermore, negroes were not wanted in big baseball. At ground after ground they booed and hissed him. He had to go to different hotels from the other players and res taurants refused to serve him. The feeling became so in tense, and this was only four years ago, that the whole future of baseball was threatened. Some of the players on the Dodgers team even made a petition of pro test against him. Other teams dared him to play on th'cir home grounds "at his peril." However, Jackie Robinson had two things on his side. He was one of the finest gentle men in the game and he was one terrific baseball player. The film, "The Jackie Rob inson Story," had its pre miere this week. The story is straight with no humbug and the man who plays the lend is Jackie Robinson himself. He plays the part extremely well, and It is the best film yet of the many we have had on "Jim Crowism." ALICE FAYE
