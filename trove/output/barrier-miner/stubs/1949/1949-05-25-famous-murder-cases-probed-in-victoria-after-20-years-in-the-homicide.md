After 20 years in the homicide squad, a former Victorian detective tells Keith Dunstan, of the Melbourne "Herald," of his experiences as he recalls. Famous Murder Cases Probed In Victoria "That was the most fascinating murder I ever had anything to do with. It had everything. It all started one night in 1923. There was a ring on the phone at Russell Street . . ." Heavyweight Sergeant McGuffie leaned back in his chair, and started to muse about the murders he had been associated with since he started in the force 37 years ago. He is well able to do this, be- cause he recently retired to his rose garden. He is now feeling the effect of those years. More than 20 in the homicide squad, a section of the force where work never finishes. Many a time he went for a whole week without sleep—not even giv- ing himself a minute to change his clothes. Nevertheless, he always looked smart on the job. He invariably wore a rose or a carnation in his buttonhole, a homberg hat, and, of course, he carried an um- brella. Apart from roses he was a student of forensic medicine, and often he was able to give country doctors a tip regarding a post- mortem examination. His chief claim to fame was a wonderful capacity for searching for detail. "Buried In Hills" But we were talking about that murder in 1923. Nurse Hannah Mitchell rang the police and said she had just shot her divorced hus- band, an Italian named Bonfiglio. The police rushed out to the house. Nurse Mitchell was there, but Bonfiglio was gone. They found a trail of blood from a win- dow to where Bonfiglio had caught a taxi to hospital. McGuffie interviewed Bonfiglio at St. Vincent's Hospital, and, ex- pecting him to die any moment, took a statement. Bonfiglio said Nurse Mitchell had performed an illegal operation on a girl, Bertha Couglin. After the operation she died and they had to bury her up in the hills at Coldstream. Sergt. McGuffie said: "A simple phone call was now turning into a double murder. We went to Coldstream, found the spot, but the body had gone. "Now we didn't know what to do. For months investigations were at a standstill. Then one night we received a report that a man had seen someone throw- ing a coffin-like object into the Yarra at Anderson Street bridge. "Next morning I went down into the Yarra in a diving suit. To my disgust I found that coffin-like ob- &nbsp; ject was a motor bike that had been stolen the night before. But I searched further, and right un- derneath where the motor bike had been was a sack. Inside it &nbsp; was the body of Bertha Couglin. "This was a most amazing coin- cidence. That sack had been there at least three months. "We charged Nurse Mitchell with murder. She alleged in court that the girl had operated on herself, and that Bonfiglio had attacked her with a stiletto. She was ac- quitted. "Bonfiglio subsequently re- covered and died a few years ago. Nurse Mitchell also died a few years ago." Body In Mine There was another very interest- ing case in 1940. The police re- ceived word that a body had been found in an old mining shaft at Miner's Rest. Sgt. McGuffie and Superinten dent F. W. Lyon went to investi- gate. There was 80ft. of water in the shaft, and it took three days to pump it dry. McGuffie went down the shaft in a bosun's chair and found the torso of a man. There were five tons of mud at the bottom. For several days McGuffie worked be- low, sending the mud up, while Supt. Lyon directed sifting opera- tions. They found the skull, jaws and even loose teeth. The teeth were sent to Ballarat, and a dentist was able to identify the man as George Davidson, who had disappeared 12 months before. McGuffie said: "I think the man was murdered. In the shaft near the body we found a large bar of iron and a piece of wire. This ap- parently had been tied round the neck of the body. "There were also some strands of wool from a sweater in the mud similar to strands that were caught on a barbed wire fence near Bal- larat the night he was last seen. We had our theories, but the mystery was never solved. "I had the luckiest escape of my life. Just after we finished the shaft collapsed, and 10 tons of earth and rock dropped down into where I had been working." Plans Misfired There was another extraordin- ary case about 15 years ago, when Syd McGuffie and Detective Seager arrested five men for having con- spired to rob a bank. They discovered it by accident. &nbsp; They were questioning a man, who protested his innocence. To illus- trate his point, he cited the case of the bank at Clifton Hill which they had decided not to rob. Here is the story. Five men were in it—a bank clerk, a builder, a garage proprietor, a motor me- chanic, and an ex-policeman. The manager was away, and the bank clerk was living at the bank. They had hoped to clear £40,000. On the night the robbery was to take place the garage proprie- tor could not get any oxy-acety- lene cylinders. The bank manager came back and the robbery fell through. Four of the men were sentenced and the ex-policeman was ac- quitted. Leonski Case Sgt. McGuffie also played a big part in the Leonski murder in- vestigations in 1942. Private Edward Joseph Leonski, an American soldier, was hanged for the murder of three Australian girls. The trial was conducted by the American Army, but because of his knowledge of the case Sgt. McGuffie assisted in the prosecu- tion. This is what he said about it: "Now there was a court case for you. The Americans did a magni- ficent job. Tho chief prosecutor was Colonel Hayford O. Emwal— the finest prosecutor I ever heard. Judge Book was very good in his day, but I think Colonel Emwal had the edge." There are countless murders that Syd. McGuffie could tell you about—enough to fill a book. He could tell you about the time he was a member of the "Terrible Ten" in 1916—a nickname they won from criminals when as plain- clothes men they cleaned up Mel- bourne's parks. He'll tell you about this and other things if you pull him up one day while he's pruning the roses out at Essendon.
