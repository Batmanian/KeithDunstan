In his foreword, he says . . please don't bother to rebuke Keith Dunstan for hav ing documented our most en dearing vice between the covcrs of this book. It won't sell"'. It will of course, but the pity is that it is not really a very good book. The publicity, the dust cover, and Humphries fore word, all strive to sell the book for its supposed humour — knocking is fun, and funny, and this is supposed to be "the most entertaining book you're likely to read for some time". But most of the really funny lines are printed on the dust cover, and lor the rest the book presents a serious thesis: "Of course we are not the original knockers. The English are more skilled at it and they enjoy it more. The difference is that Australians really mean it. The knocking comes from the depths of their tortured feeling of in adequacy". The truth of the thesis is never convincingly demon strated. What we are lett with is simply a catalogue of Aus tralian criticisms, generally in distinguishable trom those in any other country, of places, people, politicians, artists. Jn fact, the whole thing bears the marks of a hurriedly thrown together attempt to ex pand Qn an idea stemming from a few very funny lines (many of them delivered by overseas visitors to Australia). The second part of Dun stan's thesis is that our willing nests, even eagerness, to hear criticism of ourselves from visitors is distinctively Aus tralian, and he is on safer ground here. The evidence is all there — the questions to the visiting celebrity about his impressions of the country five minutes after landing here and so on. The "tortured feelings of inadequacy" certainly are in evidence in that sort of situation. But that is not as important in Dunstan's argument as the notion that Australians them selves are the greatest of knockers. In failing to prove it. he overlooks some of the most fascinating aspects of the great Australian inferiority complex. He devotes quite a time, for example, to the over seas worship syndrome in Australia. Everything is better overseas, the Australian be lieves, and Dunstan lists the cultural, educational, gastro nomic and other examples of how we fall over ourselves to copy anything from overseas. How can Dunstan have managed to overlook com pletely the fact that Austra lians are also, paradoxically and quite hypocritically, the greatest knockers of anything "foreign"? Bloody Poms, bloody rclTos, bloody boongs, bloody Yanks, and all the rest, are the targets for some of our most scathing knocks. We hate them all, and their ideas, and their styles, and their products, but we will do anything to imitate them, especially when they are at their worst. My own favourite manifes tation of this is the invariable reaction of the Australian, particularly in his work situa tion, to the suggestion lhat a new technique or idea or product from overseas might be worth trying. "It wouldn't work here, mate!" might almost qualify as a national slogan. But having got ihat off our chests, we rush out and buy and copy everything they throw at us, especially if it is cheap and nasty. One of the problems Dun stan faces is his own uncer tainty about what "knocking" really is. In a somewhat des perate attempt to assemble evidence to support his thesis, he catalogues everything he can find which is a criticism of 'anything, even when it is sound criticism. An absurd extreme is reached when dc Groot's rib bon - cutting escapade at the opening of the Sydney Har bour Bridge is included as an example of the kind of "knocking" which Jack Lang had to put up with. Two chapters offer some promise of rising above this level. 'The Australian Land scape' and 'Australian Writers' both examine the great nineteenth century knock — ,the notion that nothing which existed or was produced in Australia could possibly be any good. These feelings of inferiority about the local scenery and scene have been noted and examined elsewhere but Dunstan does come closest here to proving some kind of special sense of inferiority and some special delight in knocking. The im pact of the chapter on literature was lessened tor me by an apalling error, which ascribes The Man from Snowy River' to Henry Lawson, says it was published in 1898.^ and then quotes a "knocking re view of it from \ 896! This is bv far the worst error in the book which has quite a few others, mostly typographical. These tend to reinforce the im pression of a book thrown to gether in a hurry to illustiate ■a "thesis", an attempt which fails. The notion that we are a nation of knockers is, 1 sup pose, part of our folklore imzice of ourselves. K.cith Dunstan might well have found a lot more evidence it he had searched the sort or material Bill Wannan collects. Wannan's notions of what "folklore" is are very wide, and probably unacceptable to folklore scholars. But he does collect stories, poems, jokes and songs widely and energeti cally, and most of his material is either fairly "genuine" folk lore or in current oral circula tion. He has produced at least 20 books (at least three last vear) and they are all readable collections of Aus traliana. 'Bill Wannan's Folklore of the Australian Pub* is a brief book, and one feels that Wan nan probably had to exclude a lot of material which could have made the book even livelier. Well, how much of the talk you hear in pubs could be published? But the material he has in cluded is mostly pretty enter taining, and you wouldn t want to knock a bloke who spends his time collecting all that good Aussie stuff, would you?
