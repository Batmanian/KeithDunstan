The Beautiful People By LEONARD WARD SUPPORTING A COLUMN h y Keith Dunstan. Cassell Australia, pp 180. Price $3-25. QNE of the hazards of earning one's living by writing a column for a daily newspaper is over-exposure to what an American news magazine used once to bracket under the felicitous heading The Beautiful People. Keith Dunstan, columnist for the Melbourne Sun, at various times found himself in the thick of them, and in a book he has just written gives us the lowdown on one of the most recent rounds in the cen turies-old main event, Per fidious Albion versus La Belle France. For the 1965 Melbourne Cup, the VRC decidcd to pep things up a bit by importing some overseas talent for the Lashions in the Field contest. To use his own words: "In the right corner was Christine Borge of France who was brought out by the Wool Board and as a dream assign ment ((he Wool Board) turned on a scarlet Thunderbird auto matic and my instructions were to show Miss Borge Mel bourne . . . she was really a very, very beautiful girl". In the left corner of coursc, was Miss Jean Shrimpton of England. "When ees the Shrimp due to arrive" said Christine in the course of this Melbourne odyssey. When informed that she would be very late, prob ably just in time for the Derby, "Christine began to smile, a very contented, feline smile" at the tremendous handicap fronting her rival in the face of all the publicity she — Christine — had already had. But the rest of course, is his tory. The Shrimp showed up at sacred Flemington corrcctly attired for Saturday morning shopping at Manuka and "won the publicity stakes by the length of the straight". Involvement in this sort of nonsense occurs only occasion ally in the life of a newspaper columnist. Most of it is very hard work. It is a life of con stant anxiety in meeting dead lines and maintaining a stan dard every day. In Dunstan's ease, he also regularly found himself as signed to VIP tours and he writes about these sensibly and with restraint. Anyone who even Temotely came into contact with those two royal charmcrs, Princess Alexandra and Queen Sirikit of Thailand, will nod approval at what he has to say of them, and because of the reasoned dignity of his reproof to Prince Philip for his lack of con sideration for over-weary re porters and photographers on some of his earlier tours, at least, will align himself with Dunstan and his colleagues of these occasions. In the course of his duties, Keith Dunstan has travelled widely and written with under standing. compassion or hum our as the occasion required. His tale of the children left behind in Japan by Australian occupation forces is a pitiful one. He did his own best to smooth the way through his Australian contacts for those who were endeavouring to help but there was not very much he could do. One of the best and most . amusing of his chapters is about a tour of North Austra lia he did with three others in a light aircraft, which was he found surprisingly the cheapest way of doing it. Dunstan revelled in the charactcrs he met and looked forward to the warmth of Marble Bar, but when they arrived the tempera ture was 36.1 degrees. Throughout the book re miniscence follows reminis cence. Let me quote the one about the grazier from Cunna mulla in Brisbane onholidays: "At a very late hour he took a taxi back to his beach house, paid off the driver and received 16/- change. While fumbling for his key he dropped the change on his doorstep. His coordina tion wasn't so good so he decidcd to leave it there until the morning. "Lo, next morning on his door step he found 21 bottles of milk." Dunstan at the time was writing a column for the Bris bane Courier and several days later there was a call from Surfers Paradise. "A slow voice said: 'Hello, I'm your Cunnamulla gra zier .... Look, do you want to know what I did with those 21 bottles of milk'. " 'Yes, I do indeed.' " 'I bought four bottles of rum and we downed the lot.'" Most readable.
