IAN NIAGPHEE LEADER OF THE WET BRIGADE By KEITHDUNSTAN IS IT possible to infuriate Ian Macphee? Not easily. He is too calm, too self-analytic. Never would, he use words-like liar, cur, or socialist rat, not even drover's dog. It would be imoossible to imagine him in the midst of a true House of Representatives name-calling spat. He is too civilised; too well-mannered, too lucid. He is your good family lawyer. There is never anything quite out of place. But politics is a peculiar occupation. Say the race for leader or even prime minister was the Melbourne Cup. You would say Ian Macphee was an outsider but a very interesting tip. Good position on the barrier, handled himself* well in weight-for-age events, not a sprinter but a marvellous stayer who could come through. The speculators put it this way! John Howard is fine but say the Drys turned out to be deadly dry and not quite trendy, say those deadly popularity ratings continued to sink. Where would you go then? To the Wets? It is doubtful whether the market would accept a Macarthur "I will ' return" Andrew . Peacock. The obvious alternative is Ian Macphee. i Ian Malcolm Macphee, LL.B, MA, Liberal MHR for Goldstein, Victoria, 47 years old, , is the - man. After holding . a. series of ministerial roles in the Fraser Government he is now shadow minister for communications. You can try to irritate him by calling him the Super Wet, the Leader of the Wet Brigade, then ask him whether such epithets make him angry. "No, they don't make me angry," he said, "just disapointed, disappointed at the superficial nature of such labels." But it is undeniable there are Wets and Drys in the Liberal Party yet few people really know what it is all about. Ian Macphee patiently began to explain. . "It all seemed to begin with Mrs Thatcher. She had a certain view of what' was good for Britain and anyone who was opposed to her was a Wet. 'You're wet,' she said. A highly derogatory term. Someone then, it was probably John Hyde, who was then in Parliament with me, he thought the opposite of Wet was a Dry. He now writes a column On the Dry Side. "A Dry is now seen to be an arch de regulator, or so called privatiser. By definition a ■ Wet is- someone who is highly interventionist, one who wants to regulate or control the public sector, a terrible mis-translation of my views." And he began to explain: "When you recognise we are not alone, that we have an inter-dependence on the economies of other nations, what's left for us? There are very limited powers for an Australian government under a Federal system. I question whether we should lightly dispense with the powers that we have, so that we are buffeted around by decisions made in Detroit, Chicago or Tokyo. "But if one argues for some control, I would say a true Liberal policy is one that looks at the nature and degree of government intervention. Some say: 'Let's have 'an unfettered system.1 It won't be unfettered so far as other governments ae concerned." But one wondered whether there was a genuine cell of Wets like Bill Hartley's Socialist Left. Ian Macphee explained it was true there was a group in the party of kindred spirits but if you put them in one room there would be all sorts of subtle differences of opinion. But on the other hand was there a firm, tight group which .was utterly Martini dry which could label themselves with the philosophy: The Market Place is God? ' "I think," he said, "someone like a Malcolm Fraser could sit down and iron out these differences. He never did because these issues didn't run in his ',time. But what is needed is for someone - to come along and say: 'Come on, what is all this nonsense?' The groups might hot be that far apart." Ian Macphee in his dark blue blazer, club tie, you could be forgiven for thinking he was a member of the Melbourne establishment. Actually' he was a State school boy born in Sydney. His grandfather, of Scottish descent, was born near Creswick, home of the Lindsays. His father was an electrical engineer with the navy and half way through World War II he was switched to the civilian side to work in. the shipyards at Whyalla. Ian was born in 1938 at Neutral Bay, Sydney. He went to Neutral Bay public school and then to North Sydney Technical College. From there he went into the Public Solicitor's office and did law part-time at Sydney University. After graduation he became a barrister in the Public Solicitor's Office in Papua New Guinea. He remembers1 appearing for the PNG trade unions in their first national wage case. He sought advice from Bob Hawke, who was industrial advocate for the ACTU. "He was enormously helpful," said Ian. "I came down* to Melbourne and stayed with him in Sandringham. He went up to Moresby and we developed a very close relationship. We had a couple of years working together on this fundamental wage fixation." But then he applied for and won a scholarship to'the East West Centre,;in Honolulu. This was like a Fulbright scholarship, giving him a year at the University of Hawaii and a year at Yale. So he studied for an MA specialising in Indonesian, South-East Asian history and politics. • "It was wonderful," he said. ,"I learned sb much, not just from the course but people. There were people there from 40 or 50 different countries." He came back to Australia via Vietnam just after the Tet offensive. "I felt strongly about Australia's involvement there. It was historically wrong,: a misreading of the situation." One suggested that would hardly,win him many smiles in the Liberal.Party. "No, you're wrong," said he. "You'd be surprised. A lot of people were doubting our involvement there " He sought pre-sclection for the seat of Berowra. lie narrowly missed out It went to Tom Hughes, QC And one wondered what drove a young man to politics — "Oh, very idealistic reasons. I thought politics was the one area where you could do things,.;,where fundamental changes could; be made. I don't believe that now. Yes, marginal changes can be made but politics in many ways reflects changes that are taking place regardless of politicians ... economics, education, social conditions.
