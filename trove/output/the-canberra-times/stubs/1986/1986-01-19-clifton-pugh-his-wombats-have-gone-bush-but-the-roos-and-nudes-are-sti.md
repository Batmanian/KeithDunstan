CLIFTON PUGH HIS WOMBATS HAVE GONE BUSH BUT THE ROOS AND NUDES ARE STILL AROUND By KEITH DUNSTAN DUNMOOCHIN hasn't changed much. Except it is bigger. Clifton Pugh has added a couple more rooms upstairs. It is a combination of mud brick, Swiss chalet, Whelan-the-Wrecker recycled dream and Miss Haversham's Great Expectations. There is even the wall that Clif made out of bottles. Wine bottles, mostly, one notices. Clif doesn't go much for beer. It takes an hour to get there from Melbourne, trailing out through Greensborough and Hurstbridge. The first thing you see is massive iron gates made by Matcham Skipper. Then there's an inner sanctum to the monastery with a wonderful Victoriana beserkus portico taken from an old house in Melbourne. It has lots of stained glass which catches the declining sun rays. It has always been compulsory for the great to come up here to be painted. You can almost feel their ghosts or spirits as you go inside ... Whitlam, Kerr, Mannix, Don Dunstan, Lionel Murphy, McEwen, Uren, Cameron, Manning Clark ... Inside there's the skull of a human here, the skull of a steer over there, harness, hurricane lamp, an enormous baronial table Clif made from CLIFTON PUGH the slabs of a railways bridge, you could carve an elephant on it. There are Papua New Guinea artefacts and a lifesize cassowary hanging from the ceiling. And there are pictures, heavens yes, Perceval, Dickerson, Williams . .. There's practically the best Fred Williams you ever saw. Clif said Fred Williams used to live at Upwey and they'd see each other maybe twice a fortnight. They made a deal to swap paintings. Fred grabbed his straight away but Clif bided his time for 20 months. "I went.there for lunch one Saturday. He'd just finished the painting that morning, still wet. I walked in and said: 'That's the one—'that's the one I want.' He was a bit upset, but he gave it to me. Very honorable man Fred." There's studio after studio and there's the Aviary. This is filled entirely with pictures of Clif's wife, Adriane Strampp, by various artists but mostly by Clif. There is one very tender and charming head portrait, but the others are all nude and very nude indeed. By the door there's a butch picture of nude Adriane with carpenter's belt and tools around her waist. Very attractive girl; 26 according to Clif. Is she his third or fourth wife? Clif grins: "Can't remember." Fourth by our reckoning. There are emus in the yard outside and two young kangaroos inside, gorgeous creatures, just like puppies and Clif is Top picture, Pugh with paintings of his wife, Adriane, and above, nude figure on an outside wall feeding them carrots. He says the kangaroos are tremendously affectionate, follow you around and they'll eat anything even pinch your toast off the table. They like toast, lightly browned. But what happened to the wombats? There was always a wombat at Dunmoochin? He explained the last one went back to the bush. "Once you have domesticated a kangaroo, its yours for life. You couldn't let it go, shooters would get it. But a wombat reverts to a bush animal almost immediately." Lovely animals wombats, he said, also very friendly but, as he explained, tricky to have in the house. They become very bulky and they tend to go through doors rather than open them. And they are chewers. Wombats' teeth keep growing and they have to grind them down. They would go upstairs into your bedroom and chew your clothes. Then, one thought, there must be interesting house training problems. Clif took a piece of paper and collected an offering right in front of him on the floor. *'They just drop little pellets, not a problem really." Clifton Pugh, at 61, has mellowed. His hair is a halo of grey curls. Of course, artists are never what you expect them to be, shy sensitive creatures. Pugh has bluntness, a macho directness about him. You could picture him, say, as secretary of a powerful trade union. One remembers the time when Clif Pugh had a fascination for stark pictures featuring; carcasses of drought stricken animals and whitened skeletons. '' Yes, I still paint them but if I do they tend to be more beautiful with richer colours." And one wondered about the aging process of the artist. Surely there would be times of self doubt, of waking in the morning and thinking: "My God, this talent of mine might be disappearing." "No," said Clif, "it's not like that at all. I am painting more now than I ever did. There just isn't enough time. I come in here every day, start painting at 9.30 am and keep on painting until the light goes. Ideas? Look I can walk out into the bush over there in five minutes I've got something to paint." When he came of the army in 1945 he vowed he would never work more than two days a week, so two days was devoted to labouring jobs to raise money; the rest of the time he would paint. The days are long since gone when he has had to be concerned about money. In the old days he had to sell pictures immediately to raise cash. "That was a mistake," he said. "Now I keep a picture around for at least eight months. You need time to look at what you've done. "First I meet the subject, get an idea about him. Then I make a lot of p> CLIFTON PUGH inquiries from other people about the person, get a background. Then I confront him, have dinner with him, go to his place, he comes up here. And when I'm painting I get someone else to talk to him, so he won't get stiff. And I don't necessarily paint him in what he's wearing or how he's sitting. I often make it up, get something they do characteristically. How long? "Gough Whitlam took eight months. The man was such a chameleon. He kept changing and I did eight portraits of him. Of course, they got painted over. But I wish I had those eight pictures now, I'd put them side by side. They would be a story of Labor's rise to power. When I started in 1971 he was worried and uncertain. But when I finished it was a week before the elections in 1972 and he knew he was going to win. He really did. He was getting bigger and bigger, more flamboyant." That was the picture that went on the cover of Time magazine and was one of his three Archibald triumphs. He did two portraits last year, Professor Manning Clark, the historian, and John Perceval, the painter and both are hanging in the Archibald exhibition. He explained that the Perceval was just a small, very quiet picture. Perceval was a sick man and unhappy when he painted him. The Manning Clark picture was large, spectacular. "I wasn't going to send the Perceval because usually you don't make much impact with that sort of thing. But Adriane talked me into sending.it. She said it was the better portrait and as it happened it wasn't the big one that did well. I was told that Perceval only missed out by one vote." Yes, Clif and Adriane criticise each other's paintings. His previous wives complained because he was always painting but this works. Adriane paints too. She goes off to a studio in Fitzroy and paints all day. Oh, he's very proud of Adriane. He says: "She's a damn good painter." And he told of a recent exhibition at the Holdsworth Gallery in Sydney. They called for paintings from all over the country and Adriane was chosen to represent Victoria. "That was her first show exhibition as such. The critics ignored the others and went mad over her. She sold all her paintings. She's walking on air at the moment." Terry Lane wrote a good piece on Clifton Pugh in his book As the Twig is Bent. It depicted Pugh as a loner and how this desire to be alone affected all his relationships. He commented: "I'm pretty gregarious now, I think. I like to go to the Outback twice a year and I usually go with a couple of other artists." But as for being a loner he explained that his brother was born before World War I. His other brother was born when his father came back. He was born late, 1924, an afterthought, a mistake. His father was a designer, railways engineer, inventor. "Father got gassed in the war and that killed him eventually. Such a silly noble shit, he thought he'd fight for God, King and Country; didn't take any pension. He was on a big superannuation with the Victorian railways, he chucked the whole lot in because he couldn't transfer it and went to the Queensland Railways to develop a design and as soon as he had done that he died. So he left us in a mess. My brothers had gone to Ivanhoe grammar, mother had to sell off everything just to keep me there. But I was there less than a year and I left school when I was 14. Funny I received a note from the school the other day. They wanted to know if they could put up a plaque in my memory. I wrote back and told them it was a lovely idea." He was always a wanderer. His father had a hobby farm at Greensborough and the young Clif used to wander in the bush on his own, developing a love for it that has been with him all his life. As a small boy he took to trailing off with the garbage man on his rounds. His mother in desperation put a harness on him and attached him to a peg in the middle of the lawn like a dog on a chain. When he left school he went to work for the Radio Times, a good radical left- wing newspaper. The opposition did everything they could to close them down: cut off their newsprint, forced them out of the newsagents into milk bars, then having done that complained to the Health Department about a newspaper being sold in the same establishment as milk and butter. All this taught him how to be a radical. After the war he took up residence in Royal Parade, Melbourne, where International House is now. It was a big boarding house and he had the stables out the back for 17 shillings a week. "It was naughty of me," he said. "We had no electricity in the stables but the lines came up to the house from the back lane. So I borrowed a ladder, got some rubber gloves and scratched the insulation back, one line at a time, joined my wires on, put in a plug and got my electricity free. "Remember when Frank Hardy was having his battle with Power Without Glory and they had to dismantle the printing press in the middle of the night. They hid it in my studio for several months before they shifted it out and started printing again somewhere else." Clif said he tried twice to join the Communist Party but even with Frank Hardy's blessing they wouldn't have him. All because he was involved in a union squabble while cane cutting in Queensland. He is still left-wing inclined, a Labor man but you wouldn't catch him marching for the cause any more. He has lost faith in politicians. He left the stables at the end of 1951. Why should he keep paying 17 shillings a week. It cost a day's labouring to get that. So he hunted around and found his place at Hurtsbridge and before he built his house lived in a tent. "I borrowed a utility and came up here on the day of the Yes — No Vote on the Communist Party. At 8 o'clock it was obvious the No vote was going to win. I went to a party over the way where they had an 18 gallon keg. About three days later I tottered back and started building my house." So what is he doing now? He is doing a series on Arnhem Land. He has a wall covered with quick, colourful daubs done on paper. He explained: "In 1967, I took Fred Williams out back for the first time in his life. We went up to Tibooburra and stayed about seven weeks. We would go out painting. I would sit about thinking. Fred would be into it immediately and at the end of the day, he'd have done five gouaches. I'd have done two." We continued our wander around the house. On one wall he had the famous paintings, done from his hospital bed after his heart attack. There's just the extraordinary eye view down of the Pugh stomach, the Pugh toes, and intravenous tubes attached to the Pugh body. But right down in a far corner, we found the last remaining relic of Pugh refrigerator art. Many years ago Kelvinator asked eight artists to paint the doors of refrigerators. It was a stunt, a promotion. Clifton Pugh was a brilliant refrigerator man. The door was just the right size. How many still exist? "Well Max Walsh still has one on his veranda in Sydney. He keeps his beer in it. And there's this one. They tend to be a bit erotic. And it was marvellously erotic, breasts, penis, bottoms, lots of this and that." A McCaughey or a Mollison should call in all the refrigerators and stage a retrospective exhibition.
