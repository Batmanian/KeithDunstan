sir ian Mclennan THE MAN WHO MADE BHP THE BIG AUSTRALIAN By KEITH DUNSTAN SOMETIMES you wonder whether there is a relationship between Scots and splendid eyebrows. Sir Robert Menzies had noble eyebrows, much beloved by every cartoonist. Sir John McEwen had rich black eloquent eyebrows which helped give him a formidable mien. Sir Ian McLennan also has fine eyebrows, quite grey but of lustrous proportions, verandas which help focus that steady McLennan gaze. You always know where you are with Sir Ian McLennan. He's the ultimate boss, the eternal chairman, the super corporate man, the manager of everything in sight ... He has an office on the 26th floor of BHP House in Melbourne, very stark, few pictures, not a sheet, not a pencil where it ought not to be. Unlike Sir Arvi Parbo, there are no mementos, no mining lamp, no hunk of rock, bottle of oil as memories of a superb career, or should one say multiple superb careers. He's all go Sir Ian McLennan. As he talks he slaps the side of his chair, as if to say, "let's get on with it." Sir Ian McLennan has just retired at 76 as chairman of Elders IXL. Before that he retired as chairman of the ANZ Bank and before that as chairman of BHP. During his reign all three underwent periods of phenomenal expansion. You tend to forget that Ian McLennan was almost a father figure to the ever hungry John Elliott and those two worked in tandem. When asked how he felt about retirement Sir Ian said bluntly: "The short answer is, I hate it." He is not so much interested in the power. He loves the vibrations you get from running very big companies that are actually achieving something. "But I'm 76 you know. I can't do as much as I once did and I don't remember things as well. You have to stand down." The number of organisations he belongs to or has belonged to goes on for several pages; medals and honours are equally prolific including three honorary doctorates, a KBE and KCMG. In 1977 he organised the Queen Elizabeth II Silver Jubilee Trust and raised $6.8 million which must have been double what anyone thought was possible. The same year he retired from BHP and bought an 80 hectare farm at Narre Warren, easy drive from Melbourne and he planned to do some serious raising of Herefords. The theory was that he and Lady McLennan would go there on Fridays, return on Mondays and the weekends would get progressively longer and longer. Well they never have and one suspects even now they are not exactly expanding. Ian McLennan grew up in Mooroopna where he rode his bike to the local State school. He says: "My paternal grandfather came to Australia as a blacksmith from Scotland, from Maryborough, just north of Inverness. I am exactly 50 per cent Scots and 50 per cent Welsh. My maternal grandfather came from Merthyr Tydfil. He was a winding engine driver and he became a winding engine driver at a gold mine at Stawell. I had engineering and gold mining in my blood." He thinks he was greatly advantaged by being brought up in the country and two of the men he most admires, Henry Bolte and R. G. Menzies, were country boys. At Mooroopna school there were less than 100 students and just three teachers. His headmaster was a man named Christopher Crane, "a wonderful fellow." He remembers him vividly. He went on to Shepparton High, got his intermediate, then went to Scotch College in Melbourne as a boarder. Scotch had just moved out of the city to Hawthorn. He wasn't much good at sport but he graduated equal dux with two and a half exhibitions — exhibitions in mechanics, geometry and the half was in algebra. It's ironic to think that Edward Cohen, now Sir Edward, was just behind him at Scotch. Eddie Cohen also scored exhibitions at Scotch but in the classics Greek and Roman. He was also a champion athlete. Later he was to be chairman of Carlton and United Breweries when the McLennan-Elliott, Elders team took over CUB. Ian McLennan went to Melbourne University on a scholarship and graduated in 1931 right in the depths of the depression. He said: "I went to BHP and asked for a cadetship. They told me they were putting people off rather than putting them on. There was nowhere to go, so my father sent me back to university for another year." However, BHP did take him on in January, 1933. "I started at Whyalla," lie said. "I did all the snotty things that a cadet does, a bit of labouring, a bit of surveying. I suppose I did wonder what I had done all the studying for but it was a marvellous time. What I didn't know then, I already had my foot on the ladder. They just didn't tell me." Next he went to Kalgoorlie for six months, worked underground, sometimes with a pick and shovel. He had a period as manager of a limestone quarry in Tasmania, then in 1937 Essington Lewis picked him out as one of four cadets to go to Newcastle to get experience at the Newcastle Steel Works and Port Kembla. The rise of young McLennan is well documented. He became assistant general manager in 1947, general manager in 1950 and chairman in 1971. When he took over as general manager BHP was really just a steel company and you can well speculate what would have happened had they not discovered oil. Very likely BHP would be just a nationalised steel company making majestic non profits like the railways. But McLennan's background was resources, minerals. He said: "When I took over we didn't have many geologists. Three years later we had 58 of them." Oh yes, not everyone in the company thought it was a good idea. Many believed they should have stuck to steel. "But a whole lot of things happened," he continued. "We found iron ore in West Australia. We found what is probably one of the best manganese ore mines in the world at Groote Eylandt. Then Ampol found oil at Rough Range in West Australia. They only found a cup full of the black stuff, but it made it popular. Our fellers thought it would be a good idea to prospect around Newcastle and Port Kembla known as the Sydney Basin. I was worrying a bit about this because we were spending a great deal of money and one night I thought we must get the best oil geologist in the world to help us. "Sir John Norgard went off to America and, God bless him, he found Lewis Weeks, who had just retired as the chief geologist of the Standard Oil Company, New Jersey." Weeks made a detailed study looking at the BHP drill holes then came' to McLennan and said there was little hope there of finding oil. "But I remember as distinctly as anything," said Sir Ian, "he added a 'but.' And I told him, "I know you [> sir ian Mclennan geologists. I know what you're going to say when you say 'but.' He said, 'What?1 " 'You're going to say, 'But I know where you can find it.' He said, 'That's exactly what I'm going to tell you and he said to look for it in Bass Strait." Sir Ian knew that Bass Strait was one of the most stormy areas of water anywhere, and the techniques for off shore exploration were just evolving. Yet legend has it that he went to the board and got the money to start exploration in just five minutes. One wondered whether he remembered what he was doing the exact moment when Barracouta 1 made its historic strike 46 metres below the surface off Ninety Mile Beach. "I was at the office, 500 Collins Street, which used to be Essington Lewis House. I heard they had struck gas, not oil, and it had blown up all the apparatus. Thank God it didn't catch fire." It wasn't until some time later that they struck oil. But weren't there great celebrations, the throwing of hats in the air at BHP? "I suppose we went off and had a few beers," he said laconically. That was all. He explained carefully that oil at the time was $3 a barrel, not $25 or $30 as it is now. They had to be careful that the madness and euphoria of Rough Range was not repeated. "BHP shares just gyrated," he said. "Our shares got up to $25, 45 times earning capacity. Just think of that. If we put out a wrong bulletin we would have been roundly criticised. That put a damper on the parties. But I think often, there are three great days in the history of BHP, the day we started at Broken Hill, the day we decided to go into steel back in 1911 and the third was finding oil. Oil has been the mother and father of almost everything that has happened since." Sir Ian is very politic. He won't say a word about the running of companies he has departed. As a recycled chairman was it remarkable that an engineer became a banking chief in 1977? "I was a bit surprised myself," he said. But he didn't think the operations of the board of ANZ were all that different from BHP. He explained that he was part of the Australianisation of ANZ. It had a London board. He was the first Australian chairman. When he arrived 6 or 7 per cent of the shares were owned in Australia and when he departed the holding was over 70 per cent. As for brewer, pastoralist, food processor McLennan, according to Sir Ian, it all goes back to the days when his paternal grandfather, Donald McLennan, started a small flour mill at Mooroopna. There was a long process of mergers. The flour mill blossomed into Kimpton, Minifie and McLennan, which merged with Barrett Burston maltsters. Barrett and Burston merged with Henry Jones. Henry Jones took over Elders. Elders took over CUB and so the frog kept getting larger and larger. What's more, he remembers John Elliott from the days when he was a management consultant with McKinseys and it was Elliott who architected the Kimpton, Minifie & McLennan merger. One gets the irresistible feeling that had Ian McLennan been on the board of CUB rather than Elders IXL, the company would be CUB IXL. He didn't wish to comment on that but he did point out that takeover moves began | when Ron Brierley of Industrial Equity made a pass at CUB. "You see, Ron Brierley made a pass at CUB. CUB owned 49 per cent of Elders. Now Mr Brierley wouldn't have just sat there with 49 per cent, he would have wanted the whole lot and would have told us what to do . .. which neither P John Elliott nor myself would have taken ! very kindly to."
