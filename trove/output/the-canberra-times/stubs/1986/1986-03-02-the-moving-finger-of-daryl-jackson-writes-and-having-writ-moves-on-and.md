THE MOVING FINGER OF DARYL JACKSON WRITES AND HAVING WRIT MOVES ON AND ON AND ON By KEITH DUNSTAN SOMETIMES you get the impression that Daryl Jackson is designing everything. Good heavens, you see his name all over. He's there with the enormous Jack Chia project in South Yarra. You know the one: hotel, offices, apartments, restaurants, canals; the project which will turn South Yarra into a combination of Venice, Paris Left Bank and Century City. One newspaper said he was doing three towers in Collins Street. He's working on a great travelling show for the Bicentennial, along with his Sydney partners, Basil Carter and Robin Dike, an aquarium for the Wran Show at Darling Harbour, the Australian School of Film in Sydney, a sports centre in Perth, a medical research centre in Darwin. He has just finished, with Godfrey and Spowers, the great Walter and Eliza Hall Institute in Melbourne. He's doing an extraordinary thing with two 18-hole golf courses at Cape Schanck in Victoria. One could go on and on. He receives some remarkable compliments. Patrick McCaughey, director of Melbourne's National Gallery, calls him "the representative architect of his generation . . . not only pre-eminent among his own generation of Australian architects but among his immediate predecessors and successors as well." That's some praise. But also I have heard some snarls. "He's past it now. Not up with the new generation. Daryl Jackson is interesting but he's really just the flavour of the month architect." Past it? He's far too young for that. Daryl Jackson is 49 years old. He is blond, boyish, has a smile that works splendidly. We found him in really ghastly premises in a lane at the back of Melbourne's T & G building. No fine office of his own. He doesn't believe in offices. He likes to be out in the middle, in an open space with everything happening all around him. As you read this, maybe right this minute, Daryl Jackson Architects Pty Ltd is moving into a three-storey building at the top of Little Bourke Street. Circa 1912, it opened as one of Melbourne's first auto dealers then became a printery. Daryl, of course, has recycled it beautifully. He has all the top floor; vintage style terrazzo floor, space bigger than your shire hall. He has pulled out the ceiling so that you look straight up at the church-like fan-shaped beams and rain- pattering, unadulterated galvanised iron. He thinks there's something nice, pure and symbolic about galvanised iron. Immediately you say: "Every north- winder you are going to boil." Back comes the Jackson smile. No sir. Up above the galvanised iron there is another layer of galvanised iron with insulation in between. Daryl Jackson Pty Ltd in Melbourne has a staff of more than 40 with 35 architects and there is an office in Sydney. The old style was to have a whole team of draughtsmen. He doesn't go for that. He likes to work with real architects. He likes to have them in charge of their own projects with, of course, Daryl hovering, looking over their shoulder. "We're a collaborative team," he says. You can see he is sensitive, too, about this Daryl - Jackson - doing - everything Jackson at Melbourne University's Graduate School of Management line. For a start he is not doing the Jack Chia project on his own. Four firms are involved, each presenting a principal architect, and he's only one. He is not doing three towers in Collins Street, only one: 90 Collins Street, again with the collaboration of four firms. That's the trouble, he explained. You put in for a project, or you become associated with a project, you either miss out or it falls through but the public still gets the idea you are doing it. For example, he put in for the Westpac building. There were interviews but Harry Seidler got the job. "His greater experience won the day," said Daryl modestly. He wipes his thinning hair and says people have a misconception of what architecture is all about. You have a lot of projects on hand but they are not all going ahead. Some are waiting up to six months for government approval, some are being designed, some are on hold for finance, some are under construction. Architecture now is like the medical profession. You have your specialists and you all have to work together to produce the healthy patient. Architecture, he claims, is an incredibly insecure profession. "I have been in practice for 21 years and there is just six to nine months lead time. It is just like being an actor. You are entirely dependent on the climate and the shows that are coming up. And just because supposedly you are a good architect, it doesn't mean you'll get the job either. There are so many factors; involved. You've got the cost, you've got inflation, you've got clients only interested in a quick return on a building . . . Twenty-five years ago in a practice like this a client would ask tentatively if they could have drawings for a building in six months. Now they want them in two. They can't afford to wait." The Jackson working day starts at 7.30 am and he does 12 hours. That goes through to Saturday mornings when he likes to prowl around the sites checking on how things are going. If he's lucky he goes off to Shoreham to his weekender on Saturday. The weekender won a prize from the timber industry. He describes it as a "building that evokes one's understanding of Australian place-making . . a solid, not too refined corrugated iron and timber exterior supported by bush poles and a marvellous pre-Captain Cook white eucalypt." So he goes there with his wife, Kay, son and three daughters. He reckons he makes a pretty good strapper when the girls go off riding. Daryl Jackson was born in Clunes, a mining town near Ballarat, on February 7, 1937. He said: "I grew up the son of a country bank manager. We lived upstairs Continued on Page 28 DARYL JACKSON in the bank, a marvellous Greek revival building, built at the height of the gold era. "In those days the bank manager had the pistol under his pillow and he was meant to go down and address any robber who came in. Dad wouldn't have liked to shoot anybody. I don't think he even knew how to use the pistol." Daryl explained that every three to five years the manager moved to another small town. It was important not to get over-indoctrinated by the locals. They went on to Garfield in Gippsland, then to the city, Essendon, where Dad eventually became the local newsagent. "I always accused him of taking over the newspaper shop and running it down. He has a great love of people." Daryl went to Essendon High School and then to Wesley College. Even from the time he was 12 he always wanted to be an architect. "I was the sort of kid who liked making things," he said. "My mother was a milliner. She was and still is very creative with fabric, sewing and putting together tactile material. Then I had an uncle who was a builder. My brother and I used to go round buildings with him; climb under old houses, look at the blocking, that sort of thing. Maybe the idea came from there." From Wesley he went to the Melbourne Tech, as it was called then. He didn't have the grades to get into Melbourne University. He admits if he were starting now he simply wouldn't get into architecture. "Wouldn't even get close," he said. And he complains that there is something wrong with an entire system that doesn't take note of aptitude. However, after three years at the technical school it was possible to go to Melbourne University for his final two years and he believes he benefited hugely from being in both places. After graduation he worked in Sydney with Colin Madigan, "a terrific inspiration," then he went to Europe and the US for five years, working with Paul Rudolph in Connecticut and Skidmore, Owings & Merrill in San Francisco. In 1965, he went into partnership with Evan Walker, now Victoria's Minister for Planning, Environment and Agriculture. One of his early great buildings was the Harold Holt Memorial Swimming Centre in Malvern which he did with Kevin Borland. Then there have been such landmarks as the School of Music in Canberra, Canberra's National Swimming Centre, the Australian chancery complex in Saudi Arabia, City Edge in Melbourne and the Conference Centre at Melbourne University. There is great satisfaction in leaving your mark on a city, driving past a: building every day and saying: "That's one of mine." But what if there are mistakes; what if there is a lack of standards in design? He agrees that there is practically no serious architectural criticism in this country. Writers, painters, poets, actors, all are accountable, usually the next morning, but architects, no. Architects are loath to comment on architects. Robin Boyd was an exception. "Robin Boyd," he said, "didn't get opportunities to do larger scale work because he was seen as a writer and a critic by his immediate architect peers. He used to say: 'I would get the managing director's house to do but I wouldn't get the factory or the office building'." But Daryl Jackson believes it goes further than this. Australians don't think a great deal about architecture at all. He puts it down to what he calls the open space metaphor. We might have a passion for ant heaps, we might be the most thoroughly citified people in the world, but by tradition we are used to looking into the distance. He says: "The mind and the eye are on the garden, on the open space, and the Artist's view of 90 Collins Street DARYL JACKSON city is a kind of an aberration, not possessed consciously. The metaphor is still there of living in the broad open spaces and the mind is in the Outback. We don't want to concentrate on urban issues. A city is a place we evacuate every evening and there is a very strong suburbanism." He believes cities should have individual character. The architect has to do this delicate balancing tip-toe act, not destroying too much of the old but at the same time not turning the city into a living museum. There have been suggestions that he is the man who has put art deco back into buildings. He doesn't go for that. He prefers to think of buildings as humane creations, not clinical products of a technical era. It was irresistible, for example, not to ask him about the Rialto of Collins Street, Australia's tallest building. He called it "the grey shroud." . . . "It doesn't have enough interpretive quality. Buildings, like all good art, must have interpretive quality for the eye of the observer to ponder about. There's a banality about so much modern architecture. It is too simple, therefore disengaging to the eye. The eye doesn't want to encounter it. It doesn't have the riches or joy that come from a more complex composition." So 90 Collins Street, done with the collaborative Denton Corker & Marshall, Godrey and Spowers and Robert Peck & Company, is not one of your reflecting ice boxes. There is much detail for the eye to linger over. Nor does Daryl Jackson go for howling gale forecourts. He believes a building should marry its environment, so that the lower part of the building is on the street matching the front and height line of 19th century Collins. He describes it this way. "The horizontal line has a series of relationships with the other buildings. It starts with a density of glass which gets lighter as it goes up, so the building tends to filter away, giving it head, shoulders and some legs. The building has personality instead of being an inanimate mass; not just a grid in the sky." The busy designing hand of Daryl Jackson moves in many directions. The Victorian Government asked him to give a plan, an idea on how to use the square between Lonsdale and La Trobe, Swanston and Russell streets for a new State Library and Museum. Then it went into a competition among nine firms including Daryl Jackson Pty Ltd. This has caused some in-fighting and you wonder how much jealousy abounds in the architectural battle for too few jobs. "Sure it's competitive," he said. "But the competition is far worse in Italy and in England. It so happens in Australia we have a particularly talented body of architects right across the board. You do make enemies. If you are doing a lot of work and you are doing it well the knives come out. Unfortunately it is one of those professions where the people tend to cannibalise their own. "I received some criticism because we were doing small work and not giving it away. But in an office with young kids the aim is to give them all an opportunity. That's healthy. We have just finished Eric Westbrook's (a former director of the National Gallery of Victoria) little house in a renovated warehouse in Prahran. It gave very good working experience for two young people working with Eric and myself." An amiable man but, if you want to bring a steely look to his eyes, ask him about the big firms that love to employ architects from the US, Canada and elsewhere. "Yes, I get pretty annoyed about that. I think it's quite unnecessary. We can stand alone in this area. The attitude still prevails but only at the top end of the high flying companies. It will disappear within the next decade." Time was running out, a conference with clients was coming up, so one left Daryl Jackson. Down the street I met an old friend who said: "Daryl Jackson? Plays full forward for Geelong, doesn't he?" It seemed to confirm an Australian attitude to architecture.
