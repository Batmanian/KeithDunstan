So Igor went, even though she thought she was the better violinist. Did she cry with disappointment? "Oh, no; I don't cry," said Nelli very firmly. You could see she was trained in a tough school. But she did go to Paris and she won. From then on, there was no more earning roubles in a small orchestra; she became a soloist, ready to perform with the big orchestras. This was in 1953, the year Josef Stalin died. Until then, the Iron Curtain was almost complete. To get outside it you had to be an Oistrakh, a"Kogan or a Rostropovich. But then Nelli, too, began to tour. She went to Europe, to America and to Asia. She had two tours to Australia and 1970's lasted for three months. When she returned, she found that the head of Gos Koncert had changed. This was the organisation that controlled everything, sent out the artists and in turn brought artists in from overseas. Nelli said: "He stopped everything. When invitations came in for me to play, he would say stupid things - I'm ill, I'm not in good form or I don't want to go. But I was clear. I had done nothing wrong with the KGB. Always on tour I had been very patriotic. "I found out the new man was anti-Semitic. He erased from the list a few Jewish names. He couldn't do it to big names like Oistrakh, Gilles and Kogan . .. too much scandal. You have to be a pushing person in Russia with very good friends at Gos Koncert. You have to bring home presents for them. I am not like that, on the one hand too shy and the other too proud to beg to them. So I thought, OK, Russia is a very big country with many fine orchestras; I would be happy playing at home." But 10 years passed and it was like living in a cage. It was fine in big musical centres such as Moscow and Leningrad. However, times changed. There were so many places where the local concert organisation was run by bureaucrats who knew nothing about music. They preferred pop groups and jazz. "I would go to a concert hall and I would say, 'Where is the piano?' They would say, 'Sorry, we haven't got piano. You play without piano or maybe with an accordion.' Then there were times when it was easier for them not to have the concert at all. 'Oh, do you mind if you say you already played this concert? Sign here. We will give you the money and goodbye.' Terrible. I wanted to play. I felt they didn't need my art. They paid me very well. But I wasn't happy just with receiving money. I wanted to PLAY. This was my profession. I started to suffer, get sick with all these things." So Nelli defected. The news went round the world. John Hopkins read it in the newspapers; hence the invitation to come to Australia. Now at the College of the Arts she has 14 students, including three postgraduates. "No," she says, "they don't work the way they do in Russia. They have too good a life, good climate, good beaches, sunshine. They like to do many things like swimming, dancing. Maybe a few of them are really serious. I tell you, music is a very hard profession. You have to be very strict with yourself." And she says being a top violinist is exactly like being a top athlete. If you don't train, you slip from form immediately. She has a large repertoire including concertos by Mozart, Tchaikovsky, Mendelssohn and Beethoven. If she had to pick the greatest it would be the violin concerto by Beethoven. You do these entirely as a feat of memory. Only if you are playing, say, a sonata do you use the music. Nelli is in her mid 50s. The older you are, she says, the harder it is to memorise a new work. Particularly contemporary music; that is really hard. Nor is it merely memorising the notes. "You have to feel it is ripe before you perform on stage." No longer can she absorb a work in a fortnight as she did when she was 19 or 20. And is she happy here? Yes, she likes Australia, likes the climate. Last year, New York's famous Julliard School of Music offered her a teaching post but she turned it down. There had been enough upheavals in her life. "New York frightens me a little, that huge city." She is not quite so happy musically. The ABC gives her five or six concerts a year, which she thinks is extraordinary. She played 38 in 1970 when they imported her from Moscow. "They invite someone from overseas, pay them thousands. Yet here I am resident. Why don't they use me more? I think I should go over to New York and come back. That would please them." So Nelli has discovered the old Australian complex: overseas is always better. But how about Russia? "It would be unnatural if I didn't miss Russia. I miss the language. I miss the culture. I miss my friends. I spent nearly all my life there. But I don't regret. I knew what I was coming to." And Nelli sets her Ukrainian jaw. She is not the type that cries. ?
