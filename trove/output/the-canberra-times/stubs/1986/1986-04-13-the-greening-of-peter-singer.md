THE GREENING OF PETER SMGER By KEITH DUNSTAN IV ETER SINGER says ■ modestly that he did not J really invent the word "speciesism." Ah, but he is the man who has put it up there with sexism, racism and all the other isms. Some people bracket Peter Singer with Germaine Greer. Like Germaine, he has a genuine international repu tation, the creator of a grand cause. He is the man who rates animals as being as important on this planet as humans. Why should they be any different? He is virtually the father of Animal Liberation. But there the similarity with Germaine Greer ends. There are no sulphurous lashings of criticism, no vehement attempts to shock. There is not a quiver of violence in the man. When you tell him that Animal Lib fanatics in Britain have gone about smashing windows in butchers' shops, putting sugar in the petrol of animal experimenters, pouring Superglue into the locks of those who keep battery hens, he looks pained. Singer does not approve of that kind of activity. He dislikes fanatics of all kinds. Peter Singer is Professor of Philosophy at Melbourne's Monash University. Even though he is only 39, he has an ageless quality about him and a slightly melancholy air. You rarely see him in suit and tie and he has a reputation for never wearing leather. Does this make him a ratbag? He admits that, when he went to Monash in 1977, there were all sorts of cracks about Singer and the Chair of Animal Liberation. However, this is just one side of Peter Singer. His reputation as a philosopher is secure indeed. He has been published in all the right places. But what turned him in this direction? It was almost an accident. He says that his parents both migrated from Austria in 1938. "My father was a businessman but he was very interested in books, history and foreign languages. He liked to teach himself .French. My mother, Cora Singer, is a doctor. She came from a family that was intellectually inclined. Her father was a professor of classics who worked with Freud on some psycho interpretations of classic myths." Singer went to Scotch College, then on to Melbourne University to study Law. But his matric results were so good that the Law adviser said Peter should broaden his course and do Arts. As if this were not enough, a friend advised him that straight Arts was "pretty boring." He should do an honours Arts degree as well as Law. So he did history and philosophy. He graduated in Arts with first-class honours. Law was forgotten. "Even then," he said, "I wasn't sure whether I would go into History or Philosophy. I began a Master's thesis in history but I switched after just one term. History is such an isolating study. You have to find your own bit of turf no one else has dug over. In' three months I was the world's greatest expert on a subject no one was interested in. There was nothing to talk to people about. I thrive on argument and discussion. Philosophy covers subjects you can go down to the pub and talk about, so that was it." Peter Singer completed his Masters in Melbourne, then did a Bachelor of Philosophy degree at Oxford. His . thesis on "Democracy and Disobedience" was read by H.L.A. Hart, one of the great philosophers of the day — now over 80 — and he recommended it be published. So, barely out of his 20s, Peter Singer had a book published by the Oxford University Press, no less. He made his moves at the right time. Philosophy had been concerned for a century or more with dust-dry esoteric matters, the meaning of words; now, it was moving into living issues. "At Oxford," he said, "I met two Canadian couples who were vegetarians. I wasn't a vegetarian. I hadn't- even thought about it in a serious way. But, when you meet people and they don't eat meat, you ask them why. At first I thought it just a challenge to rebut their arguments and show them why they were wrong but, in the end, it worked the other way. • "I was working in ethics, so I was concerned to show that what I was doing was ethically justified because of the rights of human beings. Then they said, 'OK, what's so special about human beings?' The inclination is to say humans are more aware of things, they are rational, conscious. Then the answer was, 'That's not true of all- humans; there are some who are infants or have brain damage or something like that — we wouldn't consider it's all right to treat them like animals.' That struck me as a powerful argument. "I began to see our practice as a basic prejudice along with certain parallels." He cited the way we treated blacks 300 years ago as the way we treated animals now. He took part in demonstrations at Oxford. "We had some models of hens in cages, veal calves in their crates and so on. People were in total ignorance then of the way animals were kept. They were shocked when they realised the eggs they ate for breakfast came from hens raised in quite horrendous conditions. They had no idea." Peter Singer is very eloquent about the life of the battery hen, the prison-like tiers, the lights raised and dimmed every two hours to make them eat and sleep quicker, food and water from automatic hoppers and the space for a 1.5 kilogram bird being less than the area of a sheet of quarto paper. It was toward the end of his days at Oxford that he wrote an article on animal liberation and sent it to the New York Review of Books. Many people would consider it extraordinarily pre sumptuous for an unknown postgraduate student at Oxford to send an article to such an illustrious journal and expect it to be published. But it was and that was the turning point. He took up a lectureship at New York University for two years. While he was there, the New York Review backed him to expand his article into the book [> PETER SINGER Animal Liberation and so the worldwide movement was on its way. Singer returned to Australia, did some years as a senior lecturer at Latrobe University and then accepted the chair at Monash. Now it is fascinating to observe the quiet and restrained Peter Singer, the one who is constantly penning letters to the editor, railing against duck shooters or pointing out the suffering of dolphins confined in little concrete pools for the amusement of humans. But farmers tend to react. As if times aren't bad enough without Animal Libbers. Some of the more aggressive country journals see it as an attempt to wipe them out. Animal Lib people, they say, are people who 'never venture beyond Collins or Pitt streets. As for this Peter Singer, he's never been out of the city. As for his knowledge of animals, good God, he doesn't even own a dog. Singer replies: "It's true that I have never lived in the country for anything but holiday periods and it's true that I don't own a dog. We do have a cat, a stray we picked up, but this argument is not relevant. If you look at our membership, we have a significant number out in the country and we get a very broad support from there. National Farmer ran a poll of subscribers to see what they thought and a majority opposed things like factory hens, opposed sow stalls. A farmer who has animals running free doesn't agree with locking them up so that they can hardly move ... I don't believe we are threatening the lives of farmers in general. Australia needs to keep eating. It's a matter of what they eat and how they eat it." Peter Singer does not thump tables and demand that farmers cease meat production. That should be a long, gradual process but in Animal Liberation he sets out to prove that meat eating is costly and wasteful. He says it takes 9.5 kilograms of protein fed to a calf to produce .45 kilograms of animal protein for humans. That is, we get back less than 5 per cent of what we put in. Put it another way: you grow high protein feed such as peas or beans and get 135 to 225 kilograms to 4,000 square metres. Use that crop to feed to the animals, eat those animals, you end up with 18 to 25 kilograms to 4,000 square metres. Eggs, on the other hand, are more efficient — they yield a quarter of protein to that provided by plant food. Singer says the implications of all this for the world food situation are staggering. So he's a vegetarian, as are wife Renata and their three children. He has no desire to go back. Meat eating, he says, is just a habit. "We get plenty of variety ... we have shelves of Peter Singer: family is vegetarian vegetarian recipes at home we haven't even tried yet." Educating the public on the issues of speciesism is a slow business and he believes completely in sticking to democratic processes but it's a nice point whether sometimes the means justify the ends. He cites the extraordinary story of animal libbers who broke into a laboratory at the University of Pennsylvania in the US. The scientific team there had been making videotapes of experiments with animals, baboons having their heads smashed in machines. The liberationists stole those tapes, edited down the 34 hours of material and had it placed on national television. The public outcry eventually put an end to those experiments. "Maybe it shouldn't have happened," said the Professor, "but, if they hadn't stolen those tapes, the experiments would still be going on." If books such as Animal Factories and Animal Liberation ruffled some sensitive souls, he has gone further with items such as Test Tube Babies (written with William Walters), Reproduction Revo lution (with Deane Wells) and Should the Baby Live? (with Helga Kuhse). Should the Baby Live? so enraged the right-to-lifers that they accused him of having the attitude of a Nazi, purifying the nation by executing the weak. Singer said he deeply resented the Nazi accusation. No doubt he was thinking of his Austrian-born parents but there was no relation, he felt, between his views and the compulsory killing indulged in by Hitler. The book very lucidly discusses the problems of spina bifida babies, of Down's syndrome babies, of premature babies born after 23 or 24 weeks, the cost of keeping them alive — maybe $50,000 — the child doomed to a life of intense suffering to itself and parents, too. Is it all worthwhile? Some times, should they be allowed to die? On this subject, he talks with much passion: "We feel some of the hostility that has greeted the book is misplaced. These people are saying every baby should be given the maximum amount of treatment to keep it alive no matter what its handicap. They are at odds with 98 per cent of pediatricians in Victoria; 98 per cent don't believe you should keep babies alive at all costs. You have to be realistic in terms of cost and sheer suffering inflicted on babies and families." He explained that it was common for a doctor to let a baby die by not applying therapy, by ceasing treatment — a neat camouflage which relieved him or her of responsibility. They did not kill the baby, nature did. But there was no difference. There was no ethical reason for doing it that way. As Professor Singer sees it, it is more painful for the child and for adults in a similar situation. Peter Singer divides his time between his professorship and directing the Centre of Human Bioethics at Monash. He says that the idea for the centre came in January 1977 when the Associate Professor of Obstetrics and Gynaecology at the Queen Victoria Hospital, Bill Walters, gave a talk at a Monash law seminar. He told of the problems that would arise with in vitro fertilisation babies ... Theologians, philosophers, lawyers and others should be talking about it. So John Swan, Dean of the Science faculty, Walters and Singer had meetings. It was 1980 before they got the centre going and 1983 when it reached its present form with a part-time director and Dr Helga Kuhse as deputy director and research fellow. There was a Bioethics Centre at Hastings on Hudson in New York State; there was the Kennedy Centre for Bioethics in Washington DC. That was all. At Monash, they were among the first. So the Bioethics Centre is conducting research into a whole range of subjects — the problems of human embryos being created outside the mother's body, the legal implications of sex changes, the ethics of human foetal tissue transplants and the role of nurses. Should nurses be regarded as subservient to doctors, the doctor's handmaiden, or should they be seen as independent professionals and sometimes act as advocate for the patient against the doctor? Peter Singer believes that the centre has proved its worth already. Its representatives have appeared before both State and Federal inquiries. As a humanist, as a world authority, his reputation grows. Encyclopaedia Britannica asked him to write the entry on ethics for its next edition. The entry has just gone off, 35,000 words of it. And, pondering these vital questions of deep human values, does it make him religious? "No," said he. "I don't believe in God. It strikes me as a fairly bizarre hypothesis, an all-powerful God could have created a world like this." □
