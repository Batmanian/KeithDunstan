THE BEST The cost of freedom on the toad: Bike: $500 Regotnil This week Good Weekend starts a new column, The Best. It's a guide to the best of everything you can buy and enjoy. By KEITH DUNSTAN Tm HE TRUTH is I don't entirely loathe automobiles. But when the ultimate crunch comes and we are all forced to return to public transport, could I suggest a number of uses for your family vehicle: . Seal all the doors and windows, fill it with water and turn it into a charming aquarium; . Take out the seats, fit it with shelves and turn it into a hand linen cupboard; . Put it in the garden, fit it with a telephone and a hi-fi and never again be worried by rowdy teenagers; . Cut the top off" evenly and smoothly, fill it with earth and you have an enchanting flower bed to brighten your patio. Also ideal for cumquats, roses and camellias. Oh, automobiles do have their uses. They are good for carting firewood, collecting aunty from the station, carrying rubbish to the tip and coping with 15 suitcases when you go on holidays. Yet, as you will have noticed from recent petrol increases, apart from whisky and beer, they are the most clever taxation device ever devised by government. It would be cheaper to keep a child at a religion-blessed boarding school or run a mistress. Now it baffles me why more people do not switch to bicycles. I have been commuting to work by bicycle for 25 years. No fuel problems, no parking problems, no dieting problems and one gains an utterly marvellous relationship with the dry cleaner. But bicyclists are a persecuted minority in Australia. You will notice 38 that there are signs on all the freeways "No animals, dogs, pedestrians, cyclists." It is almost as if they forgot to add "and other pests." I became a born-again cyclist during one of Melbourne's countless transport strikes, no trams, buses or trains. I borrowed a bicycle and rode the 12-odd kilometres to the city. To my surprise I got to the office more quickly and efficiently than by train, tram, bus or car. I never got over it. It wasn't so much the saving in time. What had been a dreadful bore before, now was the best time, of the day. One could look at the changing of the seasons along the Yarra, observe the birds, smell what everyone was having for breakfast or dinner. V PHOTOGRAPHY BY VANTUAN Above right: the Repco Sierra. Price: $359 Bicycles at right from Sydney's Clarence St Cyclery But you can imagine what it was like at the office. Giving up automobiles is very unhealthy. It arouses suspicion, like not wearing a tie or saying you love John Halfpenny. Some people even too"k me aside and said that if I was really down on my luck they could assist with a small loan that would help to buy a Mini-Minor. If you are forced into a minority group you become progressively more strange. Bicyclists, if they are going to survive, must look vulgar. Bright orange is a Freedom good colour, shrieking red is splendid, plus helmets with reflective tape. Then those who are truly sexually devoted wear tight little black pants with chamois leather in the crotch. As for wet weather it is important to look like a Japanese sunset so that bleary eyed motorists will see you through their windscreen wipers. On arrival at work I would do a complete change at the bottom: of the office garage, hiding behind the latest folly of the motoring writer, a monster Benz or a 12-cylinder Jag. My first bicycle was a little Moulton. It was a two-speed and it'would fold to go into the boot of the car. It weighed a ton. I remember panting up Punt Road Hill and a little kid sailed past with the words: "Move over grandpa."; It took. a while to get over that. Next, I bought a 10-speed Malvern Star. The chain kept coming off and I'd arrive at the office hiding my black hands under my gloves. Ah, but then I bought Malvern Star's top-of-the-line, a Sovereign Star. It was one of a limited edition of 500, every one personally numbered like a precious vintage. It cost, $550 in 1975, which was a fortune then. Yet, it was beautiful. You know how a trout fisherman adores a perfect flyrod. It was the same with me. I adored this Sovereign Star and now I was irretrievably bitten. Wives of impossible nuts have to be very patient. We acquired His and Hers Sovereign Stars, went to the US in 1976 and rode right across, 5,400 kilometres, coast to coast. One time we were battling over the Rockies. We climbed all day to reach the Hoosier Pass in Colorado, 4,150 metres with patches of snow all around. My wife put her lovely Sovereign Star on the ground and started kicking it. "What are you doing that for?" I said innocently." She replied: "I hate the bloody thing." We returned to Australia and I thought it would be marvellously sociable if we could ride the one bike, a tandem. Now a good tandem must be handmade. The frame should be constructed with such skill there is no flex. It should be made to measure, the right size for the driver in front, the right size for "the stoker" in the rear. I saw an ad for Jack Taylor tandems in an American . magazine, -They;-were described as the best bicycles in the world, alloy frame, 15-speed Campagnolo gears, cantilever and disc brakes, everything. A year went by before it arrived on my doorstep and I ask you to believe it cost $2,000 and a merciless auto-oriented Customs department charged $1,000 duty. I cried, I wrote to three ministers, appeared on radio and TV, even took Peter Couchman for a ride in an appearance on This Day Tonight, but the Customs folk were inflexible: "The money or the bike." As I explained to my - wife, our marriage now was perfectly sealed. I could never afford another tandem made to measure for a different girlfriend. Even at the outrageous cost, we never regretted the purchase; the tandem has covered thousands of kilometres: all over Victoria, round the coast to Adelaide, up and down California. Then there was a blissful ride by. the Mississippi River from its source down to New Orleans. No Rockies there; it had to be downhill all the way. The Clatnont Tandem comes with 5-speed gears. Price: $575 from Clarence St Cyclery We are not quite such a persecuted minority as we were. Indeed, it is even trendy to own a bicycle that goes on the road, rather than a machine pedalled in the bedroom. Now old mates and others even come along and ask what bike they ' should buy. The newspapers are filled with road tests for automobiles but not much space is given to road tests for bicycles. On your behalf I have just tested the Repco Sierra. It has 18 gears, rather more than a semi-trailer. The rear sprocket has six cogs, 14 to 34. On the front there are three chain wheels - 48, 38, 28. Now if you have had any experience" with triple chainwheels you will know that unless the adjustments are perfect, they are more trouble than living with three girl friends. What's more, the 15 or 18 gears is a misnomer, because there are areas the suffering chain dare not go. This bike is equipped with a Derailleur Sun Tour Mountech, which has a particularly long rear arm - it would wrap up 800 metres of chain if necessary - and a very deep front derailleur. I have s. galloped over paddocks, up ? near-mountains like Arthur's Set at Dromana, the whole thing works without a whimper. It was no trouble to hit all 18 variations. Most modern bikes are designed - presuming you are a Sid Patterson, or an Olympic speedster. They have bent down handle bars, so you have to crouch like the ? Hunchback of Notre Dame. This has _ straight across Bull Moose handlebars, no crouching. The gear levers are right at your thumbs and so too are the brakes, which are Dia Compe' 980s with massive cantilevers. They could stop a truck. The wheels are 65 centimetres, with strong Araya alloy rims and fat. tyres. I never had those before. I've seen little kids do it. There is a certain bliss in bouncing over gutters. The pedals are big. They are called Bear Traps designed for non slip operation, no fancy toe clips. You lose a little speed with big tyres, the gearing is low, so you won't win the downhill canter, and even though everything is alloy, including the frame, I thought the bike was heavier than expected at 15 kilograms. But it makes up for all this with astonishing. go anywhere manoeuvrability. The average price to put it on the road is $500 from any Repco agent. Registration: nil. Third party: nil. Petrol costs: nil. All it needs is a few drops of oil occasionally and the ability to cope with a ravenous appetite.. My fuel bill is three health bars, a handful of muesli, a banana and a quart of water per 40 kilometres. i ?
