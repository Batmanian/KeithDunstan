THE PHILLIP ADAMS INDUSTRY By KEITH DUNSTAN PHILLIP ADAMS has just brought six broad-brimmed hats from Akubra. Three are authentic Outback Bryan Brown dung khaki but three are black; Mr Adams in black hat, black leather jacket, black trousers and black boots, standing beside his red Ferrari, is a sight to be cherished. A distinguished Mexican gangster on his way to Acapulco? A representative of Fidel Castro, do you think? No, his expression is a little too benign, too whimsical for that. Perhaps Peter Ustinov playing Clint Eastwood. He is somewhat miffed that he is continually on lists of Australia's worst dressed males. Last time he was just beaten out of first place by Norm Gallagher. Jack Thompson, who had a shop in Sydney that made male clothing, gave him that leather jacket and he has improved out of sight. There have been several occasions recently when he has worn black tie and evening jacket, looking utterly svelte. One remembers that celebrated occasion when an Adams production was the first feature movie to score an AFI award. Phillip didn't own a suit, let alone a tie. The scene was the National. Library in Canberra. The Prime Minister, John Gorton, had to come to his rescue. He is still devoted to the black skivvy, the perfect, most utterly useful garment. He has a dilly bag, a magic hold-all, which never leaves him. It contains razor, toothbrush, alarm clock, pills, 800 credit cards, micro tape-recorder and, ah yes, black skivvies. When you are in Sydney on Monday, Auckland on Tuesday, Perth on Wednesday and defying God in Adelaide on Thursday; a spare black skivvy is your answer to non existent prayers. The brief was to find out about the evolving Adams; what does he really do? He appears to know everybody; great names roll off his tongue: Bob, Kerry, Lionel, Neville, Gough, Andrew, Patrick, John ... the suspicion is that the man behind the scenes, the character who really runs this country, is Phillip Adams. He does it in his spare time between 2 am and 4 am. In recent weeks there has been some acid flowing back and forth. He has been accused of verbal diarrhoea, of being unfunny and, good grief, perhaps even going ultra conservative. He has been attacked by Terry McCrann on the business pages of The Age, assaulted by Anthony McAdam and even his old mate, Frank Hardy, has had a go. Much of the counter artillery came when Adams attacked Brian Toohey of The National Times. "I very rarely attack people,", said Adams. "I can't think of the last time I attacked an individual. I don't do that, apart from Toohey. That's because he's attacking others without sense or judgment. "I suppose I've got to know a lot of the victims of what Patrick Cook calls vendetta journalism. I know Neville Wran. He's not a crook. He may be incompetent but he.'s not a crook. Yet every journalist knows he's a crook. They know there's a Swiss bank account. It's bullshit. "I know Lionel Murphy and I like the bloke.' I don't agree with Kerry Packer about anything but I like him as a friend. I know what he does. He didn't murder anyone. He doesn't sell drugs and he is not Mr Big of Australian crime. He's got a pathological gambling urge, which he acknowledges. I just keep seeing people whom the press in a sort of manic energy has done over and I know it's not true. That throws you." If Adams hands it out, is he sensitive, thin-skinned, when assaulted in return? Well, yes, sometimes, particularly when the attack seems inexplicable. Terry McCrann's piece was on the business page, of all places. Normally it is a part of the paper he never visits. "The thing I found offensive," he said, "was the first paragraph. The only way those blokes can get at me is the advertising. You know, ad man with irreversible brain damage. They don't say that about Donald Home. They don't say it about Peter Carey. "Donald Home was creative director of a multi-national advertising agency for 15 years. Peter Carey owns an agency. Funny thing, in my generation there was nowhere else to work. Bruce Petty was in it too. There's hardly anyone who didn't Dj H3NAVH naVHOIW Adams work in advertising. If you couldn't get work in newspapers there was nowhere else; no theatre, no local drama, no film industry. So advertising was the sort of Australia Council before anything else." No, he hasn't gone all conservative. He is not a bearded Bjelke-Petersen or a city Sinclair. "I am still an old-fashioned left-winger," he says. The problem, he finds, is that you get older, you get to know too many people, you learn to understand what motivates them. And he says: "I think a journalist is often better off if he doesn't know too much; you can be more angry or righteously indignant. It is difficult to be annoyed with anybody if you know them well. I don't think any truth is that simple." Achieving a precise list of Adamsian activities is difficult. He keeps remembering other items. He is chairman of the Australian Film Commission. That's been daunting. He thought it would be one day a week. But it's been four days a week for the past six months. He is chairman of the Commission of the Future and he was off the same day to Perth to give three speeches on that very subject. Rhonda Galbally is the director and he is amused that their offices are in a de-consecrated church in Drummond Street, Carlton, Melbourne. It is curious that the future should be in the hands of Phillip Adams rather than God. He is president of the Victorian Council of the Arts and he is the Victorian Government repre sentative on the Australian Children's Television Foundation. He's a member of the national council of the Australian Opera but he hasn't been to any meetings because they have them on Sundays, a deadly serious working day for P. Adams. He is on the Federal Government's expo committee for expos in Vancouver, Brisbane and elsewhere. He is on the Australian-Italian committee for cultural and business exchanges between Australia and Italy. Last year he was the senior Anzac fellow. He says: "I'm chairman of the Darling Harbour exhibition committee. That's an enormous project of Wran's to refurbish Darlirtg Harbour around the docks. That's a committee with all sorts of people like David Williamson and Mike Willesee. It's our job to come up with ideas. I am, too, on the Brisbane Olympics committee, run by the Lord Mayor of Brisbane." He is president of the Australian Academy of Broadcast Arts and Sciences and they give out awards called Pater awards, radio awards for Australia, New Zealand and Asia. He did the Matilda awards a few weeks back in Canberra. "That was for a nice little scurrilous magazine on a wild bacchanalian night in Canberra. We gave, one Matilda to David Combe for. being the most masticated public figure of the year. Spike Milligan's mother was mother of the year and we gave an award to the photographer who took shots of Sydney politicians on a boat with hookers in Sydney." I started to run out of space with his activities; patron of Skeptics, Com missioner of the Anti Football League, trustee of Montsalvat and how about director of Monahan Dayman Adams? He concedes things have changed. "It's not like it used to be.. Until six years ago it was totally focused on me. So I made a series of deliberate changes. Now there are 22 separate offices, here, in Asia and in New Zealand. I have a role with all those offices. I wander around and see how they are going. It is now a large decentralised business, less demanding than it used to be. Thank heavens." As for the so-called verbal diahorrea, he does a column for The Bulletin and two for. The Australian. He loves quotations, so he does a quote column for Australian Business. He does a weekly column for Radio Australia and a book every two years. Nelson has just produced Adams Versus God. He gives up everything that normal people do. There's no golf, no tennis, no bar drinking, no jogging, no visiting aunty, no bridge and absolutely no dinner parties in the Adams's life. He confesses that he did go out once several weeks ago but that was the first time in two years. His wife, Rosemary, has plenty of visitors but not Phillip. . And there's no sleeping. He works until 2 am daily and he works at home during the weekend. He takes Saturday morning off to go out and visit a few mates but all the rest is work. On Sunday he dictates his columns into a recorder and his patient secretary, Sandra, translates those on to a word processor. Sleep? "I don't," he said. "I'd love to be able to but I can't. Oh, I sleep a couple of hours a night but it is a bad sleep. The only time I sleep is at board meetings. Honestly! There is a couch in the board room. I go to sleep on that and they wake me up to pass resolutions. I sleep in aeroplanes, I sleep in taxis and I sleep through meetings but I can't sleep in bed." The theme that runs through Adams's writing is not so much fear of God but fear of lack of God and a pre-occupation with death. "No, I'm not frightened of death," he said. "I just don't want to die. I find life so extraordinary, so much fun. I find it a great shock that you have 200,000 hours left to live. You get 600,000 hours in a normal life expectancy." He lives in Hawthorn, Melbourne, in a house loaded with $2 million worth of Egyptian, Roman and Greek antiquities; allegedly 8,000 catalogued items. The whole house is a personification of death. There are mummies all over the place; it is downright eerie. What's it like, sitting there, brooding at 2 am? "I havd a mate called Graham Geddes. He's the best antique dealer in Australia. Graham got hooked on antiquities through me and now he's phenomenal. He actually sleeps upstairs in his antique shop. The other night he had in the room a mummy, a body. Now he's a tough little cookie; he used to be a bouncer at a disco. He wakes up with this smell of perfume in the air. Incense. There's the mummy, standing, sort of looking down smiling on him. "I have never had that experience. Rosemary and the kids regularly see a ghost in the house. I have never been lucky enough. "I don't feel that way about them. In the room in which I work, for instance, there are three tombs of Roman children. There is the sarcophagus of an Egyptian child. There are a couple of dozen sarcophagus fragments, there's a room full of death. But to me it's not like that. I find them very cheering and soothing ... my little stone and wooden friends. Makes up for a lack of social life." ■■ Adams always has been brilliant writing on his unhappy childhood. But he never fully explained how he came to sack his father by letter. He told the story with a faint air of melancholy: "I don't know whether my father is alive or not. I haven't seen him since I wrote that letter, 20 yeafs ago. The circumstances were complex. I never lived with my father. I was born in 1939. He went to the war as a Gongregation alist chaplain, so I never saw him. He was someone who arrived in a taxi on leave occasionally. I remember him being bright yellow (anti-malaria tablets), or he would send me coconuts through the mail, name chiselled on the shell plus a stamp. "So Dad was a stranger to me. When he got back after the war, I was living with my mother. She is now with her third husband. I must emphasise that. My trouble was with the second husband, now dead. He was a monster. "I was living in hell, no other word for it. It was an appalling nightmare [> Phillip Adams for many years. He wasn't around. He never intervened. Finally he turned up again. I remember he wrote me a letter complaining I hadn't sent him a Christmas card. That was it! Christ Almighty! All those years I was being beaten up, being savaged and monstered, now you have the audacity to complain I didn't send you a f..... Christmas card. "So I wrote a long letter to him, listing his sins of omission and commission and saying on balance I thought it was-a good idea if we didn't see each other again. "I sometimes regret that. He was Congregationalist. He got chucked out of the church after the war and he worked in the Taxation Office, reversing the Biblical parable. I haven't seen him since. That's a bit sad. I think I would have heard had he died. He never attempted to resolve it and nor have I. Now I know I never will." Curiously enough Australians have a passion for being spoken at and if you want Phillip Adams to talk the fee is $1,000. He has a special mission: John Embling. "Did you ever read Tom? Embling came into this office 10 years ago. He was a young school teacher from Williamstown Tech. He brought in a manuscript. It was a record of his attempt to salvage one appallingly-disturbed kid. I thought it was a magnificent manuscript. Penguin published it and we made it into a film. "Embling got sacked from the Education Department because they wouldn't wear what he was doing with these kids, so we set up the Families in Distress Foundation, which he runs with a woman called Heather Pilcher, and that's privately . funded. The money I get from speeches I give to them. Then I write a couple of columns for them a year and they always bring in 20 or 30 grand in donations. Embling usually has about a dozen kids living in a beaten-up old weatherboard in Geelong Road, Footscray. These are kids so psychotic, so dangerous, they have been dumped by the official welfare system. John and Heather just turn them round, turn them into bonzer kids." What else about Phillip Adams? Several years ago he had his stomach stapled. It was a desperate attempt to improve his health and figure. He does not recommend it. It wasn't a great success. He still eats. He is still large and yet his figure does not have the majestic proportions of old. Migraines? Oh yes, he still suffers from those and he never drinks more than an occasional sip of wine for fear of triggering them off but what else does he do for them? Nothing. "Technically mine are called cluster headaches. Packer once sent me to his doctor who got out a feather and a pin. He ran them down the side of my head. 'Do you feel any difference?' 'I feel it sharply on one side and I don't feel it much on the other.' He said, 'That's because one of your lobes, one of your hemispheres, is quite bright, the other is almost moronic. You are not getting enough blood flow, the two are uneven.' "So that's why I am innumerate. I can't remember numbers, figures. I can't remember telephone numbers. Nor can I remember people's names; hopeless. I have a good memory for incidents. I can't remember dialogue. I am astonished by David Williamson. He can remember what people say for years. Ideas I can remember. And arguments, but not how they were couched." And who does Adams admire? Barry Humphries. "I think Barry is the most brilliant human being I ever met, bar none; dazzling. I am troubled that he has become increasingly right wing but where would we be without him. Humphries made it possible to have a local theatre, a local film industry. If you took Barry out of the scene I don't think you would have a David Williamson, a Jack Hibberd, a Romerol. Jesus, he was a great catalyst. I admire Jack Clarke enormously. I love what David Williamson does, except for his last play. The cleverest man in the country is Patrick Cook. What he does on the back page of The National Times is the most inventive, the most wonderfully cynical diatribe anyone has ever written in this country. "Heroes? I still have a great affection for Gough Whitlam. I don't think there has been anyone of comparable scale in my life. Barry Jones is a missionary zealot, and I am one of the few remaining who has great respect for: Hawke. He has his great shortcomings and problems but, my God, so have most of us." For a man who has just taken on God in open battle it is interesting how often Phillip invokes the Almighty in his conversation. Mr Adams laughed merrily over: that one. He even mentioned a certain Catholic archbishop who thanked him for what he had done for religion. After all, he is always talking about it. □
