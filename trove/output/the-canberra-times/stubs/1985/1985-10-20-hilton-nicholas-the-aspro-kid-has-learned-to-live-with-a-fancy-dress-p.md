KEITH DUNSTAN found that the man behind the Melbourne Cup is just a little sensitive about some of the fun and frivolity. mm VOU CAN take all our m national days. Australia Day is nonsense. Anzac Day is in an ^■advanced state of decay. The Grand Final business is sliding faster than the dollar. Un questionably the only deeply-felt, passionate, religious holy day is the first Tuesday in November. The Melbourne Cup is much more than a horse race. It is a national release of internal pressure, a community outburst from the psychiatrist's couch, the true National Day. The gentleman-figure in charge of all this is Hilton Nicholas. Back in a dim Boltean age he would have been Sir Hilton. Instead, he is just Hilton John Nicholas, OBE. For his morning suit he prefers matching grey: grey topper, grey tails and grey trousers. Splendid. 'ce One wondered just how many citizens g would gather at Flemington this first g Tuesday in November? 5 Hilton Nicholas scratched his head: "I x am terrified to think. We have virtually R everything happening at once. We will [>, Nicholas have here the Prince and Princess of Wales. The Cup is being broadcast around the world. It will be seen live by at least 60 million people." What's more, the Melbourne Cup had a stake of $1,025,000. There were other sponsored horse races in the world with million-dollar stake money but only one or two and certainly this was the only handicap race worth more than $1 million. He engaged two sensitive points in one hit. One had to learn to live with the Foster's Melbourne Cup. Here was sacrilege of a high order. It was like having High Mass at St Paul's sponsored by a detergent. And wasn't a handicap race just a bit Non-U? It was not designed for the best horse in the land. The Melbourne Cup was really for the glory of the bookies and the TAB. Hilton Nicholas was ready to defend the barriers on both points. There was nothing new in having a sponsor, he said. The VRC had been pioneers. . "We have had sponsorship of races since the early 1960s — the brewery, Black & White Whisky, Craven A, George Adams Tattersalls — these were the pioneers. Foster's sponsorship of the Melbourne and Caulfield Cups will give a tremendous fillip to racing." He was patient about the slur on the Cup as a handicap, explaining that the great races in Europe were set weight, or weight-for age events. "But don't you see, they get the competition. We don't get the numbers of international horses competing. For a race such as the Arc de Triomphe in France, the horses come from the US, Canada, England, Ireland, all over Europe. They can't come here because of our quarantine restrictions and, besides, they would have to stay in training too long. "The Melbourne Cup was a handicap from the beginning. It caught on because anyone could win it. It was a common man's event, not something designed for the exclusive racing set." If the Melbourne Cup has become a fancy-dress party he is prepared to live with it. He said: "I have a personal abhorrence of some of the things you see at the Cup but provided there is no violence, no hooliganism, we can live with it. We had to fence off the Members' Car Park and almost turn it into a prison. That was a cause for regret. We had to ban people bringing in Eskies full of liquor." Hilton Nicholas was born into racing. His father George, founder of the Aspro empire, was a VRC committeeman for 25 years. Hilton won his father's seat after his death in 1960. George Nicholas launched Shirley Park Stud in the early 1930s. "My father won nearly every race in the book except one, the one he really wanted, the Melbourne Cup. He should have won it with Morse Code in 1951. Morse Code was loping down the straight towards victory, he pulled out to pass another horse, clipped its heels and down he came. Racing is one great big 'if* but the chances of winning that year were greater than in any other." Shirley Park is unchanged. Hilton hasn't had the same success but after his father's death he bought the English stallion Court Sentence and in his first season he produced Begonia Belle, which had a series of great wins. The Nicholas boys grew up at Homeden in Lansell Road, Toorak. I remember the sale of the property back in 1961. Mr G. R. Nicholas left an estate of 2,120,719 pounds. Mr A. A. Lord, a financier, bought Homeden for 91,000 pounds. Homeden was set in two acres, had 22 rooms, a six-car garage with a tiled floor, an indoor heated swimming pool with fernery and a tennis court. Nicholas said living there was really superb. The Army took it over during the war . . . "The old man had to come out of retirement to run the family business. As the Army had Homeden he commuted every day from his place at Macedon. He had an old Buick Century complete with gas producer. Has anyone ever preserved a gas producer?" One remembers the Homeden auction in August 1961. The crowds of sightseers, stickybeaks, were beyond belief. The classic remark was made regarding the antique 180-piece silver cutlery canteen: "It wouldn't be any use to us, dear, they've 'N' on everything. You'd have to get that wiped off." Homeden was maintained for little more than a year. Hilton Nicholas remembers one evening he was invited in for drinks by the local managing director of Ford Australia. He lived in flats directly overlooking Homeden. "I'll never forget that night. The wreckers had moved in. Homeden was half pulled down. It was a dreadful sight." It was one of the last of the great Toorak estates to become residential. George Nicholas was a great public benefactor. He made huge bequests to Wesley College — so many that every little Wesley boy was called an Aspro. It wasn't easy being at school there while your father was re-building the college. "Yes, they called me all sorts of things. Kids being what they are they tended to take it out on you. But it had its compensations. We had a magnificent fellow who taught swimming and boxing, called Ron Grant. I used to take a few of his swimming lessons for him and in return he taught me boxing." Nicholas joined the RAAF in early 1943, went to England and flew Hurricane fighters but, fortunately for him, the war was virtually over by the time he was ready to go into action. By the time of his discharge he wasn't even 21 but his interest in flying went on. He and his brother, Lindsay, still have the air charter business, Nicholas Skyways. He flies his own twin-engined Cessna 402 and he has a strip on his property Howlong, half-way between Albury and Corowa. Howlong became famous back in the 1940s. It was the property where the Pyjama Girl was found dead in a culvert. After discharge from the RAAF, Hilton Nicholas went into the family business. For years there was a running controversy as to who actually discover ed the formula for an acceptable common aspirin, George Nicholas or his chemist partner Harry Woolf Shmith. Hilton Nicholas said he never regarded it as a controversy. He said Shmith was helpful in the synthesisation of acetylsalicyclic acid but he sold out his share to Nicholas •& Co in 1916. "There's a well known story about my father at this time. The bank was about to foreclose on him and he needed money desperately. He went to a famous businessman of this town. He offered him a half-interest in Nicholas for 500 pounds. He made the offer on Albury station but the businessman declined." Hilton Nicholas remembers it was a tradition with the Nicholas company that always, wherever you went, you had to carry an Aspro. You could be challenged by another member of the company to see whether you had it. "One time," he said, "I was travelling in the south of France, I was at Nice or some place like that. I swam to a raft offshore and our European repre sentative was there. He thought he had me, so he challenged. But I had an Aspro in the little pocket of my bathing togs." Since then Nicholas has merged with Kiwi. Nicholas Kiwi was taken over by Consolidated Foods in the US, although in the takeover Nicholas Kiwi has not suffered the blood-letting of other famous companies that have been swallowed. It still operates as before as a separate enterprise and Hilton Nicholas remains as deputy chairman. ■He was chairman of the Victoria TAB from 1963-84, he is chairman of the National Trustees Executors & Agency Co, director of Nicholas Skyways, proprietor of Shirley Park Stud. When we left him he was about to set out for an international racing conference in Paris. We hoped racing was in better shape than the nuclear business. He thought it was. □
