Where the wine is awful but the nudes are lawful YUGOSLAVIA By KEITH DUNSTAN T i' HE TRUTH is that before the grand tour the only city in Yugoslavia I could name with confidence was Ljubljana. Ljubljana, "the city of love," is lovely ancient place, pretty close both to Italy and Austria. They have an annual wine show and, for as long as most of us can remember, Australian wine producers have been sending their wine to Ljubljana. Looks fine on the bottle Gold Medal Winner at Ljubljana Show. One year a "friend of mine forgot to send his wine to the show. It didn't matter really. He won a gold medal just the same. Yet, perhaps they were sincere. After tasting much of the wine there, it would be terribly easy to win a gold medal in Ljubljana. Our original plot was to go to Spain Portugal or maybe even Burgundy. Our travel agent said: "Why not go to Yugoslavia. You've never seen such scenery, and Dubrovnik is a dream." She was almost right: The Adriatic Coast has the type of scenery, so spectacular, it's almost overdone. It could be the backdrop for an improbable operetta, or maybe the subject of one of those exotic paintings you see in Italian cafes. There are snow-capped mountains with sheer cliffs that drop straight down to the Reckitt's blue sea. There are long lines of Grimm's fairytale cottages made of stone and on every high spot a church tower with bells. There are dark pencil pines pointing up to God and neither billboard nor neon for hundreds of kilometres. The first person we met stepping ashore at Dubrovnik was the white coated health inspector. He looked at our passports. "I see you have been in Thailand recently. Mmm, a lot of disease there." And he turned up his nose at this non-socialist, royalist nation. "When you get typhoid or cholera report immediately to the nearest hospital and we will treat you at the expense of the State." "Oh, thank you very much!" Yes, they are doing everything they can to win the tourist dollar in Yugoslavia. They are busy building Dubrovtiik harbour is a dream t> Sheer cliffs drop down to a Reckitt's blue sea The bridge over the river Neretva at Mostar Yugoslavia unchanged: haymaking in the Julian Alps Awful wine hotels all along the Adriatic Coast, which they believe fit the international dream. They are in trendy patterned concrete, big atrium foyers, open-air bars, tennis courts and all.. Now in our hotel in Dubrovnik the lavatory was tied down daily with a paper bandage which said: Sanitised for your protection. That soothed the Americans. There was a bidet — that soothed the French. At breakfast there were lots of hideous sausages, cheese and above-average bread — that soothed the Germans and the Dutch. Downstairs there was the most marvellous indoor swimming pool you ever saw — that soothed the Australians. The hotel food sometimes' was like boarding school, the waitresses dressed like warders in Prisoner and were about as friendly. And I was shocked when my Yugoslav wine came in a bottle with a crown seal. But wait, just across the water there was Lokrum, Island of Love. This was famous for its nudist bathing. The Yugoslavs have been in the nudist business for a good eight years. Our guide Borut told us all about that. Borut was a great character. He was a Slovenian: big dark romantic • eyes and dressed in a brilliant waistcoat, burgundy velvet decorated with little flowers and 50 silver buttons. "We were the first," said Borut. "The Greeks and the Spaniards were too prudish." He explained that there were many nudist hotels. Just an hour's drive north there was the little town of Slano, which had a large elegant nudist hotel. The guests could go naked from their hotel rooms straight down to the water. They t> Awful wine didn't have to put up with the inconvenience of wearing a bathrobe. "They even drink in the bar in the nude," said Borut, his brown eyes glowing with pleasure. "The Germans, particularly — you know with their cold climate — they love to get their clothes off. They have booked out that hotel during July and August for the next five years." Just one thing worried me. The beaches did not have sand, all pebbles. Sun bathing must be very hard on this and that. Like all States desperately short of cash, Tasmania, Nevada, Monacoj Alice Springs — there are casinos. The casino in Dubrovnik is at the Hotel Libertas, perched on a cliff, on the edge of the Adriatic. If you lose your money there's the compensation of one of the loveliest views on earth and a most convenient precipice for jumping. Yugoslavia wants tourists because it is suffering 60 per cent inflation . . . 200 dinars (fluctuating) to the dollar; so you spend your life with a calculator in your hand. Their big claim is "the cheapest holidays in all Europe" ... cheaper than Spain, Italy or Greece. That fancy hotel mentioned-earlier is $47 a night for two, breakfast thrown in .. . $70 at peak season. But heavens, we were buying meals at intriguing little restaurants, $7 for two of us, including wine. Good buys in Yugoslavia are shoes, good shirts which you can get for $7 to $10, and in places like Ljubljana and Zagreb, marvellous high fashion leather — jackets, trousers, skirts. A high fashion skirt and, jacket to match cost $170 Australian. You would pay $600 plus for it in Sydney. Germans, according to Borut, top the Yugoslav tourist invasion, then French', English, American, Italian, Scan dinavian. He doesn't like mixing his tour groups. It is best if you have ail-German, all-French, The Americans don't mix well with the English. The English are withdrawn and find the Americans too noisy and out-going. The other day, he said, they had an American who hired a Mercedes Benz, which he drove all the way from Ljubljana to Zagreb. When he arrived, the American said that it was a fine car, but "kinda noisy." He drove it all the way in second gear. He had never been in a car which had plain old-fashioned gears. The unfortunate old-fashioned Mercedes now needed a new gearbox. The star attraction, of course, is the old city of Dubrovnik. In 1976 it won a gold medal for being the best preserved ancient town. It is almost the last of the great medieval walled cities. On a castle scale of one to 10, Dubrovnik would have to be 10. The Tower of London would score seven, Kronborg Castle at Elsinore in Denmark would make six. That's where Hamlet's ghost strutted his stuff on the misty battlements. Admittedly' the Tower of London is a better place for torturing and beheading, but Dubrovnik too has some very nice airy dungeons where once evil-doers were put on display. Dubrovnik needed a walled city because all the world rejoiced in invading their shores — Venetians, Byzantians, Turks, French, Germans . . . It took 500 years to build it, from the 12th to 17th century. In the old days the drawbridge went up at nine o'clock every night and no foreigners were allowed inside, only Dubrovnikkers. Over 5,000 people still live within the walls. Their houses are made of stone with "terracotta roofs and few of them are under 300 years old. You can believe in their age because every so often you get a whiff of the noble antiquity of their sanitation system. At every turn of a corner there is an ancient "church, a carving, a fountain, a clock tower and ' in June, during the Dubrovnik Festival, the town is alive with baroque music. The wall is immense; in many places 25 metres high and three metres wide at the top. It is an athletic achievement to march the entire circuit, climbing a thousand steps. It is an interesting feat of the imagination to stand in one of the towers, look out to sea and imagine what it was like on guard duty 500 years ago. It could have been a chill winter's night, snow falling. "My God! There are those bloody Venetians again. Get out the boiling pitch."
