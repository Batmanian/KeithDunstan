MIRK A MORA HAS A WALL She's so excited she can't sleep By KEITH DUNSTAN DUNQUESTIONABLY one of Melbourne's most enchanting creatures is Mirka Mora. God has passed a wand over her. She has never grown a day older since she was. 14.-Yes, at 57 there's a grey hair here and there is another over there but she ; still has brown eyes as big as cricket balls. Charles Blackman eyes really. She has an almost gleeful smile and she still wears little girl dresses and little girl shoes. They are Alice shoes. Some call her the eternal gypsy but to me she is Alice Through the Looking Glass. She has that eternal air of innocence and wonder. The novelist Joyce Cary once created an artist named Gully Jimson. Inside Gully Jimson there was an irresistible force. He went berserk whenever he saw a bare wall. Walls had to be adorned, beautified, painted upon. Mirka Mora has a wall right now. She is so excited she cannot sleep at night. One thing calms her down. She adores expensive perfume; this makes her breathe deeply and helps her to doze. But as she lies in bed she is working images. Perhaps Melbourne's most bizarre building is Flinders Street Railway Station.; Robert Morley once said - it looked as if they were setting out to design St Paul's and suddenly they, got confused. Actually it is a cross between an Islamic temple and the Brighton Pavilion. However, millions are being spent. The barriers have been swept away and now it has a Fab squeaky-clean look. Right on Flinders Street,-just before you cross Princes Bridge, utterly prime position, there is a wall, nine metres by six. That's for Mirka. She is filling it with fantastic figures.' There are animals, fish with legs, a bunyip, a train . .. oh yes, there has to be a lovely train . .. There 'are faces taken live out of the street. BLF workers in their yellow helmets, a pink punk with her hair standing on end. At the mural's lower level there are sculpted pictures. Children can feel them. There is one image of a child all bound up. "The child Is strapped," says Mirka,, "because children are not set free. They are a bit strangled. We do not listen to them enough." The mural is in mosaic and her palette is a million tiny pieces of glass. Glass is a very unforgiving medium. So she has had to study geometry. She has studied the great mosaics of Italy dating back to the fifth and sixth centuries. She has sketch plans where every piece has to go. The great murals were always done in plaster. Not here. There is too much train vibration at Flinders Street Station so every piece has to be stuck on. She has the help of Bruno Fortuna, an Italian: Bruno has olive skin, a big smile and a big. black moustache. "Isn't he beautiful,!' says Mirka. "He knows what to do. His family has been making mosaics for three generations. It is a joy to see him cut a mosaic. He caresses every piece." Mirka is French and she rolls out her accent even better than Edith Piaf. She was born in Paris. Her mother was a Rumanian and her father Lithuanian.' It is better to let her tell the story: "They met in Paris. My mother was an artist of life. She was not well educated, she had a lot of imagination, tremendous spirit. My father loved books. He was an antique dealer. All my childhood I saw wondrous things. He f used to collect knives. We had one wall that was a panoply of swords, all shapes, some curved. He loved to collect German armour, antique stuff which soldiers wore. "When the Germans came to Paris, they went from house to house demanding all metals. My father wouldn't let them have it. As soon as it was night he threw all his armour, his ■v swords, his priceless collection into the Seine. It is still there, somewhere near Notre Dame. Isn't that sad? "We; had beautiful furniture. My father loved Napoleon furniture, with a lot of encrustation. Very ornate, not e unlike mosaics. Sometimes we would be very wealthy, sometimes everything was lost. The war years we did not do well. '"My father was Jewish. You had to i; wear a yellow star. I remember the first day. My mother didn't know what to do. ; When I went off to school she decorated my yellow star all around with lace. A policeman stopped me. Sent me home. 'What are you doing?' he said. I could have been arrested. "The worst day was July 6, 1942. A policeman came to collect us at 11.30 am. He was a French policeman, very nice, he could see we were not ready ■ and he announced he would come back at 1.30. My mother said to my father 'I will go with the children, you hide.' "So my mother, with her three daughters went off to concentration camp. They put us in a train where they put all the animals. My mother had an envelope, paper and a pencil. She said to me 'Madeleine'.. . you see, my name really is Madeleine. I prefer Mirka. It is the name of the yeti, the snowman; also a Tibetan name and it means maker of images. Isn't that lovely? My grand mother had that name ... ■?. "In the train, mother said, 'Madeleine, look through the wood and tell me which stations we pass and I'll write it down. Then you will know where we are.' That's what we did. When we finally slowed down she threw the envelope out of the train. It was addressed to my : father, no stamp. A dog could have carried it off, anything. But some wonderful person picked it up and posted it. I think of that person every day. - "Anyway my father got the letter, he knew in which camp we were and after three weeks he got us out. I don't know how but he, used some influence. Of the 25,000 people who went into that camp only 18 survived. I was 14 then. We were put. in a horse cart and taken out. I saw all the,people on the fence looking at; us and I felt so ashamed that we left everybody behind." A member of the Resistance who worked on a railway station got them false passports. "Now Mirka was neither Mirka nor Madeleine. She was Mahdi, born in Tunisia. Individually, and alone, they all went to a secret' hiding place in the country and they remained there until the war was over. What happened then? She worked with orphans who had lost their parents , during the war. And she went to a theatre school. The great Jean Louis Barault was there. So too was Marcel Marceau. "Marcel Marceau was my teacher". He knew me when I was 17, that's why we always see each other when he comes to Melbourne." Then she met Georges Mora, who was to be her husband. "Georges used to deal with inventors and sold patents. Inventors are very like painters. So we married and came to Australia in 1951. We had a studio in 9 Collins Street. People such as Charles Blackman and Arthur Boyd used to come there. This was the very studio once occupied by Tom Roberts, a fabulous place. Streeton, Longstaff, they all came. We lived there for 15 years." Mirka was always a painter. She took her paints when she went on ,her honeymoon and she did a painting from the first floor of their hotel in Annecy, a French village near Switzerland. She still Mirka Mora: it is natural for adults to have toys has the painting. It depicts a church. But it wasn't easy. At 23 when she arrived in Melbourne she felt she had to start all over again. "I couldn't draw properly. Yes, I could doodle but only straight lines. Melbourne was starting' to have buildings. I'd go to my dentist, see the ICI building. I did lots of structural paintings, very abstract. Nobody would think they were mine. "When I was 27 I was getting nowhere. So I decided to work every day, eight to nine hours drawing. John Reed used to say to me, 'Mirka, what are you doing? You should paint, paint. Don't draw.' But I have to draw because drawing is the structure of painting." Very early Mirka started to collect dolls. All her sons had dolls and teddy bears. She said: "Philippe, my son the film maker, started making films at 11. He used dolls in all his sets and I even painted some of them. He wanted his first movie camera at 11. Oh, he didn't make home movies, he did proper stories. One of the first movies was in the eFitzroy Gardens. Many times my second son, William, had to be pushed in the lake by my third son, Tiriel, for the action and there is one scene where s William comes out of the water very quickly because of the yabbies." Mirka says they are all very talented. Philippe has just finished a movie on the Leonski murders and has flown to California. William, like his father, is an art dealer and he has gone to London to work on a film. Tiriel is an actor and also a writer. But to get back to dolls. They have had an enormous influence in Mirka's life. In 1970 Georges Mora, art dealer and restaurateur, and Mirka Mora, artist, separated. "I was very sad. I couldn't work at all. When you paint you have to be very clear in the mind. But you see I am a compulsive worker. I started to cut out my drawings and they all looked like dolls. I saw Max Harris at Mary Martin's in Adelaide and I got some painted dolls on cloth from him." The next move was' to start making dolls, not baby cuddly little dolls to go in a pram, but huge dolls, sculptures. She had two exhibitions, which received extraordinary publicity. "To me it was distressing. I could have made a fortune with those dolls. But I had to stop. It was a mystery, strange. I had to find out why they excited me so much. I never did find out and I didn't have, another exhibition for 10 years." Her return.to the real world was the painting of a tram. The Victorian Department of the Arts commissioned a dozen artists to turn classic old Victorian trams into works of art. Mirka Mora was the first to be commissioned. "It was just like being asked to paint a church and a tram is really like a church, complete with congregation inside. It always used to appear at the beginning on the ABC's Weekend Magazine . Wherever I was — Perth, Brisbane, Adelaide — I would see it: 'Hello, there's my. lovely tram'." Mirka gets around congested St Kilda doing her shopping on an old-fashioned child's scooter. She loves it. She has a rocking horse. She loves that too. "Remember Shere Hite, who wrote the report on women. When she came out here she said that women liked to ride on rocking horses because it made them very erotic. Oh dear, I lost my innocence all over again. I love anything with wheels — I would love to own a little boy's go-kart." Mirka Mora believes it is natural for adults to have toys. They have a soothing, calming effect. How stupid to be so inhibited that it stops you doing what you want to do. Recently she was travelling in an aeroplane and she had a large teddy bear. Mirka said: "There was a woman sitting next to me and she loved my teddy bear. She wished she had one too but she explained she would be too terrifed to carry it in public for fear what people would think." Mirka pointed out what people thought had never worried her. "The lady in the aeroplane said she would go off and get her own teddy bear." □
