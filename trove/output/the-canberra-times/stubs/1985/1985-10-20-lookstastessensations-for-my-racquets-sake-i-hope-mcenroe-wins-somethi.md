THE BEST I By KEITH DUNSTAN GOOD grief. 1 have just bought a new tennis racquet, the first in 10 years. A man had to sit down and drink a glass of water — $160. One thought $55, maybe even $65, would be OK for something really fancy. But they were all up around $160 and beyond. "Where have you been?" asked the young salesman. But it wasn't only the price, it was the technical mumbo jumbo. It was hard to discern whether one was buying a tennis racquet . or a 10-phase dish washer. The racquet I chose was the Dunlop (mid-size) Max 200G, a beautiful thing in green and black, as used by John McEnroe. It was "computer designed," made of "pure graphite with specially blended nylon filament, vibration free," put together by a "revo lutionary hollow-in jection moulding process.for an exceptionally light, tough frame" . . : "laboratory tested to a load of over 60 kilograms, 10,000 times." It appeared to have every convenience except a microphone. The blurb spoke of "awesome power, incredible aggression." I thought back to the days when I took my first tennis lessons. Jack Crawford and Gerald Patterson were still around. There were grass tennis courts scattered all through the suburbs. There were memories of hot days, fresh cut grass and the bliss of 54 playing in b&re feet with grass between the toes. The game was very polite.' One always apologised for a net cord and it was practically compulsory at the end of the match for the victor to jump the net to shake hands with his opponent. Racquets were all made of a beautifully crafted timber, mainly spruce, quite unsuitable for throwing or knocking over the soft drink box. The stringing was gut and down at the bottom was a pretty, piece of coloured lacing. I had a Donald Budge racquet and after the war I graduated to a Pancho Gonzales. Now with my new Max 200G I have to get acquainted with another era. It was about 1950 that developers discovered a tennis court was precisely the right size for a couple df home units. So the private court almost disappeared. I understand that nobody has apologised on a tennis court since 1940 and nobody has jumped the net since 1938. The correct form on winning is to put your fist in the air, drop a hand over the net, shake without looking at your opponent and give a slight sulk. Wooden racquets seem to have gone almost entirely and they are all made of mysterious substances ranging from highly-refined alu minium through to extraordinary fibres,, carbons, alloy? and such, discovered only recently on the planet Uranus or some such place. They don't put the fancy coloured lacing on the bottom any more. There was a further complication. Racquets came in various sizes. "That's cheating," said my wife. "You only want an unfair advantage over me." This was exactly what I had in mind, so I consulted Alan Trengove, editor of Tennis magazine. He said 10 years ago Prince racquets brought in the jumbo or oversize racquets. Paul McNamee used a Prince worth about $500. Jumbos, he said, were par ticularly popular with veterans; for those who didn't see the ball with the accuracy they had 10 years ago. Most champions, he said, used the mid-size racquet-on the theory that you get better control and the sweet spot,is actually larger. That's another thing with which you must become acquainted in the 1980s. All the racquet salesmen talk about the sweet spot and it has nothing to do with mixed doubles. Alan Trengove said unques-. tionably racquet makers using new materials and new construction methods had made huge advances. "It's like the car industry," he ' said. "They keep bringing out new models. In the old days you would hang on to a racquet. But the real enthusiasts buy new racquets every year. All the brand names have changed. There are brands on the market now you've never heard of." Alan was not keen to favour any particular racquet but scouting the stores and using his magazine we came up with some names. One of the most companies is Pro-Kennex, whose racquet is used by John Fitzgerald. The Copper Ace, midsize 50 per cent graphite, 50 per cent fibreglass sells for $75 to $100. The Black Ace 100 per cent graphite goes for $160 to $200. Vitas Gerulaitis uses a Belgian racquet, the Snauwaert Graphite Mid, $175 to $200, which is 80 per cent, graphite and 20 per cent fibreglass. Another racquet worth looking at is the Rossignol F200 — green, carbon fibreglass, midsize — used by Mats Wilander, $160. All racquet manufacturers make great use of their stars. Now Dunlop feature McEnroe, saying their racquet indisputably is the most advanced because it is used by the champion of the world. But wait a minute, McEnroe was beaten by Ivan Lendl in the US Open. Boris Becker won Wimbledon. Now let's see. Lendl uses an Adidas racquet and Becker uses a Puma. Both are not available here. But it is a worry. Do you think I am already out of date? If McEnroe doesn't win a big title pretty damn quick I might have to buy something else. 11
