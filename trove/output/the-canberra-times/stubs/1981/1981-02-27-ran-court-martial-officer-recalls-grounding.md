COURT MARTIAL Officer recalls grounding From KEITH DUNSTAN,. in Long Beach, USA Lieutenant-Commander Michael Harrison, of the Royal Australian Navy, re called yesterday the moment when HMAS Adelaide'went aground on January 8. He said it was just on 2.30am. The ship had been making a broad turn to starboard at high speed when the captain had called, "We are going to hit that .. . island". The captain had grabbed the throttle, pulled it back and almost at the same time Com mander Harrison had given the order for full speed astern. Com mander Harrison said he had seen the outline of trees against the lights of Seattle and seconds later the ship had gone aground. A 22-year-old able seaman, who heard the captain swear, was askqd what he did when they went aground. He replied, "1 got off the bridge as quick as 1 could, sir". Commander Harrison, 31, and the ship's captain, Commander Glen Lamperd, are being court martialled on charges of negli gence. Commander Harrison is the first to be tried. The Judge Advocate, Mr Terence Cole, QC, told him he could give evidence on his own behalf if he wished, but if he did so he would liable to cross-examination. Commander Harrison said January 17 had been a very dif ficult day, beginning at 5.35am. His official role had been ship's torpedo and anti-submarine of ficer. He also had been duty officer and he had been standing in as communications officer. He had had only a few hours inter rupted sleep before he had come on as officer of the middle watch just before midnight. He had been late on watch because he was organising a boat pick-up, because of a breakdown in radio communications, then the ship had been hove-to for 45 minutes while a problem in trac ing equipment was attended to. The ship had been making acoustic runs in Carr Inlet by Puget Sound. The American civilian operators of the acoustic range had said they had to make two more runs at 25 knots. Commander Harrison said, "The captain replied vehemently that he didn't want to do it". They had begun their run at 2.20am and had hit Fox Island while making a 180-degree turn at 25 knots. Commander Harrison was cross-examined by the prose cuting officer, Commander John Bayliss. , When you came on the middle watch did you brief the look outs? — I di4 not. Did you brief the operating room? — I did not. Why didn't you brief the look outs? — I plain and simply forgot. Commander Bayliss put it to him that surely when operating at night Would it not be normal practice to plot the course on the chart, defining all the clearing bearings, all the hazards they were likely to encounter. Com mander Harrison relied: "Yes". When asked then why he had not done so, he said this had been the fourth acoustic watch and he had thought it reasonable to accept the organisation of the three previous watches. Do you accept that an officer of the watch must have a proper navigation plan? — I would have to say,'Yes'. I put.it to you that when you made the starboard turn you were not aware of the position of the ship — You can put it to me. Sir. • 1 find it very hard to answer. At the time 1 felt 1 knew where the ship was. I was reinforced by the captain's authority. But in hind sight it is ludicrous. Had you looked at the chart before ordering the starboard turn? — No sir. Had you done anything to dis cern the position of the ship? — No sir. Had you any doubt about the safety of the maneouvre? — None whatever. , Were you unhappy? — No I wasn't unhappy. Were you in doubt? — No, I wasn't in doubt. . Were you worried? — 1 wasn't worried, but I was uncomfortable from the moment I went up to 25 knots. Why? — Well, sir, 25 knots in a ship is fast. 25 knots in pilotage waters seems even faster. Then 25 knots at night in pilotage waters is disquieting. If the officer of the watch is uncertain of the position of the ship, should he inform his com manding officer? — Yes. When asked how close he had thought he was to Fox Island, ■Commander Harrison said he had thought there was room to ' make the turn twice over. The case for the defence is now complete. Final addresses by counsel will be made and then the board of five will give its verdict. Policemen each fined $3,000 [ MELBOURNE: Two suspended Victorian policemen were- each fined a total of S3,000 in the County Court yesterday. Judge O'Shea fined Mr Brian John Duggan and Mr James Gordon Langskiall $1,000 on each charge of theft, false imprisonment, and attempt ing to pervert justice. Mr Duggan, 27, unemployed, of Rosebud, and Mr Langskiall, 28, labourer, of Mentone, were found guilty by a jury after a mq,nth-long trial. Judge O'Shea said the incident arose on New Year's Eve, 1980, when the two had been rostered on duty in a police divisional van. They had arrested Mr William Henry Stewart at gunpoint in St Kilda after a chase and had placed him in the rear of the van. They had put in the front seat a dozen small bottles of beer from the back seat of Mr Stewart's car. "It was a petty but despicable theft which reflects oh the honesty 'and inte grity of the police force", Judge O'Shea said. The' two men had prepared a false account of how they had arrested Mr Stewart after he complained. A.C.T.
