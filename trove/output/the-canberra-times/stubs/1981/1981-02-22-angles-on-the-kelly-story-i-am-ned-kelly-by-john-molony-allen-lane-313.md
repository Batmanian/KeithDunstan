Cassell. 218 pp. $5.95. NED KELLY IN POPULAR TRADITION. Graham Seal. Hyland House. 192 pp. $14.95. BUSHRANGERS: A Pictorial History. By Harry Nunn. Lansdowne Press. 226 pp. $9.95. Reviewer: GRAEME BARROW PITY poor Ned Kelly. It is a little more than 100 years since he dangled at the end of a rope, lost his head, and was lowered into a pit at Melbourne Gaol, yet still his spirit is not allowed to rest. Instead a media/tourist industry has sprung up, investigating his life and times and cashing in on his awesome deeds. The books to be reviewed here are among many media events which marked the centenary year of Kelly's execution. That they vary con- siderably in quality and content is only to be expected. Most of them are aimed at different markets; some are reprints or new editions of works published decades ago, while one is the product of scholarly research undertaken in recent years. This latter book, John Molony's 'I &nbsp; am Ned Kelly', is a devastating attack on the Victorian police of the Kelly era, an expose made more forceful by the elegance of Molony's cool prose tinged with irony and gentle sarcasm. A tendency to sen- timentality in the early chapters was corrected, but unfortunately this vig- ilance did not extend to the number of literals disfiguring the pages. At least 15 is far too many for what is a major contribution to Australian history. The origins of many of the poor &nbsp; selectors and bush workers who finally settled in north-east Victoria last century lay in Ireland, although at least one, Ned's father, did not leave the green and misty isle volun- tarily. He was transported as a con- vict to Van Diemen's Land — for stealing two pigs. Kellys, Quinns and Lloyds were among those settlers, many of them heavily influenced by family tradi- tions of official persecution and op- pression of their forebears in that unhappy place across the oceans. Voyaging thousands of miles across rough seas did not mean the end of persecution, however. For between 1860 and 1862, alone, as Molony records, 14 offences were alleged against four members of the clan. Only three were sustained, the worst being the illegal use of a horse. Molony outlines the family's in- ability to improve its lot, the continu- ing clashes with police, and the clan's predilection for cattle duffing and horse stealing, offences which were to prove of little social conse- quence when compared with the up- heaval caused by Ned's police kill- ings, bank robberies and the last stand at Glenrowan. Molony is unrelenting in his ex- posure of the police harassment of the clan, their concocting of so- called evidence, and their blundering and ineptitude once the Kelly on- slaught began. He shows that police persecution was not restricted to petty, if annoying, harassment. There was an attempt to murder Ned when he was only 16, and one senior constable apparently slept with one of Ned's sisters while her husband was in gaol. She became pregnant and died within a few hours of giving birth. Another policeman, Constable Alexander Fitzpatrick, later sacked from the Force, was responsible for claiming that Ned had shot and &nbsp; wounded him when he went to arrest Ned's brother, Dan, on a horse- stealing charge. As Molony shows, Ned was many miles away at the time and the minor wound was ac- tually inflicted by a fire shovel wielded by their mother who, among other things, resented the constable's advances to her 15-year-old daugh- ter. Ned's mother, and others said to be involved, was charged with "aid- ing and abetting" Ned who did not discover that he was accused of "intent to murder" until he returned to the district some months later. Molony's telling of the Kellys' longing for land where they could be free, and of the brutality of the Victorian police and their wicked persecution of the clan, is a sombre one. It may, however, help to explain why the activities of a group of outlaws continue to cause such spir- ited debate among Australians a century afterwards. Keith Dunstan's 'Saint Ned' is a breezy romp through the Kelly saga which adds little to the story. With tongue in cheek he makes the mis- chievous suggestion that Ned de- serves sainthood or a posthumous knighthood — presumably for the riches he has brought to tourism and the media. Dunstan gives a good account of the way tourism has taken Ned to its heart with gratifying returns. There is a fine collection of illustra- tions although many carry no at- tribution, an annoying lapse. Some- times the references he cites in his pop journalism approach are inade- quate. 'Ned Kelly: Man and Myth' is one of the better offerings of 1980's contribution to Kellyana. It is a reprint of the 1968 edition of papers and discussions at ai Ned Kelly semi- nar at Wangaratta. Some august luminaries. — Manning Clark, Weston Bate, Louis Waller — grace its pages. Its final chapter is reserved for a police PRO, Sergeant Keith Holden, who seeks to justify the police ac- tions against Kelly. He lists 17 occa- sions on which Ned was "subject to the processes of the law" including his appointment with the hangman, which I thought a bit rich. Holden admits that Kelly had "many re- markable qualities", yet he also had "most of the attributes of a cunning, scheming, vicious criminal equal to the worst of its kind today". Ned had a "low criminal mind" and with his brother Dan pursued Sergeant Kennedy like a hunted animal before shooting him. "If ever there was an act of cold-blooded murder, it was this one", says Holden. He dismisses the Jerilderie Letter as Kelly's "disjointed rav- ings", but I question whether many would agree with him. I thought it an astonishing document in which Kelly mounts a powerful defence and portrays vividly the persecution he and his family suffered at the hands of the police. The various papers from this semi- nar form a comprehensive whole from which those totally ignorant of Kelly may quickly gain an apprecia- tion of the times in which he lived and form their own conclusions of why he acted as he did. Graham Seal's 'Ned Kelly in Pop- ular Tradition' shows that it is pos- sible to trace the continuing public fascination with Kelly and his ex- ploits back to the widespread sup- port he had from his own people — the small selectors, bush workers and itinerants in north-eastern Victoria. An early popular song about the Stringybark massacre, for example, comes down quite firmly on the side of Ned and has the police regarding, their hunting of Kelly as "quite a lark" — if so, it was a frolic with unfortunate repercussions for three of them. Support for Kelly did not end with the efforts of the songwriters. Seal states that the success and survival of the gang for nearly two years "was largely a result of their 'bush telegraph' system, the network of family, friends, and sympathisers who provided the gang with food, ammunition, and information". From the beginning it seems here was a person who was destined to endure in the popular mind, becom- ing, as Seal claims, the country's only national hero, a strange pedes- tal for an outlaw. It is odd that the official and popular views of Kelly should be so violently opposed. Usually some common ground can be found, but here Kelly is either reviled as a murdering thug or re- vered as an Australian Robin Hood. The ballad of Stringybark Creek was written soon after the gunfight and since then songwriters, poets, journalists, authors and film-makers have drawn inspiration from the Kellys, interpreting, reviewing and recording Ned, his times and deeds. Seal reproduces the words and some- times the music of many of the songs and has gathered a varied collection of photographs, posters, drawings and advertisements dealing with Kelly. &nbsp; &nbsp; Harry Nunn devotes a large slice of his 'Bushrangers: A Pictorial His tory' to the Kellys and recounts their activities in mostly easy-to-follow chronological style although the mass of names at the start is in- timidating. Much of Nunn's lack-lustre text traverses familiar ground, although there are some fresh illustrations among the 61 reproduced. He con- cludes that although Kelly described the police as his natural enemies, "perhaps the enemy was within himself and he was unable to rise above his inherited background and the circumstances of his life". Part 2 of Graeme Barrow's survey of Kelly books will be published next Sunday.
