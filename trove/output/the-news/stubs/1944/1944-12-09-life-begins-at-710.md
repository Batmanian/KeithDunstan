Life -. =:~. ' .";-: ins At 7a.'B By Keith Dunstan HETHER a'man is successful or not is often judged by the hour Sat which he travels to work. If that is so, I, like Hitler. am going down steadilyv. Once I used to cling to a strap in the 8.30. but now I sit comfortably and sleepily in the 7.10. When I caught my new bus for the first time, I was glared at by my fellow occupants with all the hostility that women would show towards a man who has inadvertently walked into their powder room. Under their steady scrutiny I blushed, and decided that I mu.st have committed some social crime regarding my appearance. Was there some shaving cream or lipstick still on my cheek? No! As I afterwards discovered, it was none of these things. The 7.10, as opposed to the 830, carries a very select group of a dozen or so souls, who consider that their bus is more select than a city club. One is not elected a member: it is only after the pas sage of time that one is accepted. * * T -HE strange part about these people is that never a word is spoken, yet we knew each other almost intimately. This intimate knowledge has been gained through having noth ing else to do but study one's fel low occupants for half an hour daily. I know nearly every article in their wardrobes, where they live. their taste in literature. and their jobs. There is one man, who. like Dag wood, is always late. As the bus leaves his stop. he chases after it at a speed which would outclass the Stawell Gift, competitors. He leaps aboard the moving bus, still with only half his clothes on; what he is not wear ing he carries over his arm. Everybody else in the bus watches him. fascinated, while he puts on his tie. sweater, and coat; pulls out his comb and does his air. I am waiting for the day when he is really late and has to shave. Travelling in trains is altogether different. No sooner have you found a seat than someone points out to you the state of the weather and what is going to win the first race on Saturday. The trip which I made to Mel bourne recently at first promised to be a peaceful one, for the train was nearly empty. I shared my compartment with an airman, who from the start was all out for com fort. Y S soon as the train stopped at. the first .station he pulled out an empty port bottle and started to sing. I was a little amazed at this, for I could see no reason for such gaiety. but my amazement soon turned to admiration when I saw the "would-be" occupants of the compartment take one look inside and then move off somewhere else, muttering something about the curse of liquor. The train moved on. and the air man, looking sheepishly at me. put the bottle back in his kitbag. "I'm never without it." he said. The ruse worked well until we were invaded "by three schoolgirls, who were probably drawn in by the singing rather than scared away by it. I sat back, lit my pipe, and thought: what lovely girls! Their ages were about 16, nine, and 12 years. We hadn't gone far when the youngest started to cry. I felt an awful heel. After all, it was a non-smoking compart ment. and.my pipe has killed many a budding friendship, I put it out and said, "There, there. No more smells. Pipe's out." "It's not your pipe." she said, sobbing harder. "I forgot to kiss mummy good-bye." * * r A S the train approached Mel bourne, sobbing schoolgirls and empty wine bottles didn't stop our compartment from being filled, because it was either a case of sit in with us or out in the passage. The airman, in order to make room, was forced to hang his kit bad by ropes from hooks on the ceiling, Peace then came to the carriage, and I achieved what I had been trying to do since the journey began. I went to sleep. A kitbag swung acros the com partment by its long cord and hit me full in the face. There were shrieks of girlish laughter, the loudest coming from the small girl who had perpetrated the act. I was thinking of homi cide, but I laughed gaily, pretend ing it was a wonderful joke. This sort of thing continued for some time. until I felt that I.would get out and walk rather than con tinue the ordeal. Just as the small girl was on the point of undoing my shoelaces, with eveiybody else encouraging her by saying, "Isn't she a dear." I hopped out of the carriage at one of the outer suburbs. From there I caught a bus to the city. No one said a word, and that was the way I wanted it.
