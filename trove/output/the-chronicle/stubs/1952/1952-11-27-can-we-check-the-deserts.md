Can We Check The Deserts? from Keith Dunstan LONDON.— The deserts of the world are still creeping inexorably into the rich, good pasture lands, driving farmers to the cities, leaving behind desolation, waste. EVERY day the &nbsp; world's population &nbsp; adds to itself by 55,000 and every day the deserts spread like a ter- rible growth, destroying the earth's blood and tis- &nbsp; sue. Deserts make up more than a quarter of the land &nbsp; surface of the globe. They must be made to produce &nbsp; food. It is a race against starvation. Here in London, experts from nine nations, including Australia, have been meeting to pool information and to find some way to fight the menace. They meet every year and the conference goes &nbsp; under the ponderous title of the United Nations Educa- tional, Scientific and Cul- tural Organisation, Advisory Committee on Arid Zone Re search. &nbsp; The committee has brought out a special publi- cation on deserts. In it Dr. W. C. Lowdermilk, erosion expert, says:— "Mankind has strode across the face of the earth for 7,000 years, reap- ing where he has not planted, destroying where he has not made. In occupying new lands one after the other, one section of man- kind has looted his fellows, skimmed off the cream and left thin milk for generations that follow. He has hastened to destroy the sources of his existence." The United States has its dust bowls, the deserts of Australia are still creeping forward through overstock- ing of cattle, rabbits and de- forestation. Through merci- less exploitation, according &nbsp; to the UN, national catas- trophe is more imminent in South Africa than in any other country. Deserts have eaten up a third of Argentina and the sweep across the northern half of Africa, across almost the entire Middle East, across Asia, deep into Mon- golia. * * * ONLY a major scientific discovery will make the &nbsp; great deserts productive. It is all very well to talk about rain-making, but most deserts are arid because rain clouds don't go there. It is easy, too, to talk about turning salt water into fresh but this is still too expensive to do on a large scale. No, scientists must tackle the problem in other ways and first by going to work on the fringes. They know that under most of the deserts are vast underground lakes of water. In many cases it is deep underground and the only way to get it to the sur- face is by power— power in large quantities, cheap and always available. This could come from the wind or the sun. Britain's expert on wind-power is Dr. H. Golding. He has set up great streamlined wind mills in the Orkneys and on the windiest hilltops of Britain; he has turned the ocean breezes into electric power. He believes, with further development, it may be pos- sible to use his super-wind- mills not only for drilling into the earth and pump- ing water but also for sup- plying light and power to small towns. The French in the Sahara are hoping their power will come from the greatest source of energy of all — the sun. Scientists are using chemical converters and huge mirrors to collect the sun's rays. They have had some success in heating water, running small motors and in India there is talk of putting a 'solar- energy' stove on the mar ket. &nbsp; But new advances in physics will be necessary before the sun will help in the Sahara. * * * THE major theme of the London conference was plant life in the deserts and how plants can stop the spread of the deserts and sustain life. One of the leading experts on this was Australia's Dr. B. T. Dick- son, from the CSIRO. He is studying the types of plants that grow in dry areas and why they exist on so little water. He is trying to find out whether it is possible to breed the various types so as to make tougher, more virile plants to bind the soil and provide feed for cattle and sheep. He told the conference the story of the subterranean clover, a plant to which Australia owes millions and millions of pounds. It came to Australia about 70 years ago. Farmers noticed it growing at Mount Barker (SA); apparently it had come out in the dunnage of some ship. It received a great deal of attention from farmers and scientists and now it is grown all over higher rain- fall parts of Australia, and Australia sells more wool, more mutton because of it. Just a common clover, com- mon in England, the Medi- terranean countries and Israel. "But those countries have never regarded it as anything in particular," says Dr. Dickson. Dickson is looking for other plants like this clover, plants which will endure in the blazing sun in the in- land of Australia. It is slow work of a kind in which 20 years counts for nothing. But it is work on which the future of nations depend.
