Man Who Thinks Cities Should Be 'Vertical' The problem, how to deal with the expanding popu lations of the world's big cities is worrying engineers, architects and town planners all over the world. The French architect, Le Corbusier, one oi the greatest in his profession, has a solution. by Keith Dunstan LONDON.— The incredible French man, Le Corhusier, the architect who loathes little houses, little gar dens, and 'abominable' great cities like London, \ has won the approval of the British. Last month the Royal In stitute of British Architects awarded him the gold medal for 1953. Le Corbusier always has been a believer in the 'ver- tical city,' large, airy, sun absorbing blocks of flats in green parklands. He objects to sprawling cities, the mil lions all living in their cramped, little houses. Cities, he says, should have grass, space and light. As it is now the lives of men are ruined by 'the devouring mile,' the wasted hours they spend travelling, dog-tired between house and office. Le Corbusier sees no need for this. According to his plan the entire population of large cities like Sydney or Melbourne could be pleas antly housed within a circle of two and three-quarter miles diameter. Office workers would have no dis tance to travel from the office yet they would be on the brink of countryside and green fields. ? ? ? TlfANY Australians still x'x think flat-living isn't liv ing at all. And neither it is in many flats, but take a look) at 'Radiant City' which Le Corbusier has built in Mar seilles. For years it has been the subject of a passionate con troversy in France. Le Cor busier followers say it is one of the greatest buildings of the century, a paradise, a style of living the world will adopt within 50 years. But others say it is an eye sore, a hovel, an impossible machine which will drive people mad with too much light, heat and noise. Le Corbusier himself de scribes the building as a 'gigantic bottle-rack de signed to hold prefabricated dwelling units instead of bottles.' It is 17 storeys of glass and concrete, and per haps most astonishing of all. the building is raised on 20-foot concrete pylons, as if it were floating in air. Radiant City houses 1,700 people and stands in eightj acres of gardens. One entire floor is a shopping centre which has everything you would find in your local vil lage — food shops, chemist, laundry, post office, tobac conist, hairdresser, florist and newsagent. On the roof there is a 330 yard running track, a sola rium, gymnasium, swimming pool and community centre. Mothers don't have to worry about baby-sitters, when they svant to go for a swim or take a sprint around the track, they can leave their children at. the creche. Each of the flat units or 'bottles in the rack.' as Le ICorbusier calls them, are 70 feet by 13 feet. They are divided in part horizontally into two floors which give bedrooms 8 ft. 4 in. in height. The remaining half with its great wall of glass at the end is a brilliantly lit living room with a lofty ceiling of 16 ft. 8 in. Then outside is a garden terrace with 'sun filters' de signed to give shade in the summer and sun in the win ter. There are no uooish tins. Chutes take the rubbish direct from kitchen to base ment. There are cupboards which open directly out to the public passages so that tradesmen can leave milk, bread and groceries without bringing the housewife to the door. It is a city in a building. Madame can do her shop ping without going outside and when she feels too tired to cook she has only to call down to the restaurant to have a meal served in the flat. The rent for five rooms is £12 a month. Le Corbusier claims he gives space, the comforts that come with a large unit, an abundance of hot water and aircondition ing in every flat. He gives a dozen things the small householder doesn't have, yet he claims he can build Radiant Cities at a cheaper price than the equivalent number of small houses. Australians would still need some convincing that small houses were inferior to a Radiant City. Radiant City, the building on stilts, Corbusier's answer for comfortable living, which was opened last October.
