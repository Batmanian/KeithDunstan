Storv Behind The Ball-point Pen By Keith Dunstan. The story of the ball-point pen is fascinating — and funny. IN August 1945, Milton Reynolds, a dynamic American business man. who had already made and lost sev- eral fortunes, started a new pen com- pany — a company to manufacture ball-point pens. In October, Gimbels. a New York department store, sold £40,000 worth of the new pens on the first day of delivery. The advertising agencies sank their teeth into the new idea ecstatically. Some, more ambitious than others declared that a "precision built" ball pen would write for three to twelve months, or even two years, without refilling. They claimed that one re- fili would maka several lines around the world, fill two full-length books, write on an alligator's belly under water: that the pen could not smudge or leak, would make numerous car- bon copies, brand cattle and could be used as a dart on a dart board! The "wonder" pen would, in fact, do most of those things, but some of the old pen companies, looking to their diminishing trade, began waging a counter advertising cam- paign, pointing out the ball-point pen's defects. One compNY said that it was "the only pen that would make eight car- bon copies and no original," and others showed pictures which portray- ed the depth and characters of the writing produced by their normal fountain pens, contrasted with the dead flat line produced bv the ball- Point pen. Patented In 1888 ! A patent for a ball-point instead of a nib was taken out by John Lead in 1888. but he never made a hit with it and the Patent expired. Ladislao Biro, a Hungarian refu- gee, who invented and built the mod- ern pen in the Argentine in 1943, has faded into comparative obscurity. In the construction of the pen, manufacturers talk in terms of mic- rons — that is thousandths of an inch There are from two to four microns between the tip, a tiny ball-bearing of chrome steel, and the outer case. The ball revolves as it moves across the paper and it transfers the ink, which is a solid non-fluid block, to the writing surface outside. The ink is pressed hard against the ball. The pen is thus simplicity itself, but a high precision engineering job. School teachers frown upon young pupils who take ball pens to school. They say that the point ruins handwriting, because it is impossible to get a thick down stroke and a thin upstroke. This is true, and all the manu- facturers are experimenting to see if the disadvantage can be overcome. Goose-quill Days Still, the ball pen hasn't stirred up the trade as much as the develop- ment of the ordinary steel nib did in the middle of the last century. In those days. Poland and Russia kept immense flocks of geese just to supply literate Englishmen with quills. In one year St. Petersburg sent 27 million quills to England. That meant a lot of geese, considering that the average yield from each full-grown goose was only about 10 quills, which were plucked from the wings. The first threat to the quill indus- try was in 1822, when J. Hawkins and S. Mordan manufactured pen nibs out of horn and tortoiseshell. The idea was expensive, because they had to be tipped either with diamonds, rubies or sheet gold. Steel nibs became popular in 1840, after James Perrv had discovered that they were pleasant to write with — when the nib was split to a small hole in the centre. Fountain pens were introduced to the public in 1860, but they were messy affairs. They had no auto- matic lever filling device, and the whole pen had to be taken to bits and filled like a cigarette lighter. The feed to the nib was temperamental, and so many fingers were dirtied they didn't become popular until the early 20th century, when the capillary feed was perfected.
