Disney Makes 195U His Filn .Year By KEITH DUNSTAN, in New York. Here is the biggest, happiest and best news to come out of Hollywood since the end of Walt Disney has two hits on his hands, and for the first time in almost 12 years he is really making money. You can chalk 1950 up as "Disney year." Walt Disney deserves to do well. He has probably given more theat rical joy to more people than any other living human. But his art has not been an easy one. Those jolly little cartoons which dance delight fiAlly on the screen cost a fantastic amount of money. Disney employs 750 artists, and for each film foot they make 19 celluloid drawings. For a full length feature they throw away 1 million drawings before the final version is complete. Disney was puttering around with animated cartoons as early as 1920, but it was a mouse that really made him famous. He thought of Mickey Mouse in a railway train in the late '20's, and by 1930 Mickey was as international as coca-cola. In Mex ico they called him Miguel Taton cito, in Italy Topolino, and in Japan Miki Kuchi. To children every wvhere he was exciting as Santa Claus. The next really big hit was "Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs." The little dwarfs were loved the world over, and "Whistle While You Work" hit the tops of a thousand hit parades. Then came troubles: "Fantasia," "Pinochio," "Saludos Amigos" and "Song of the South" did not do well, although all of them had the sure Disney touch. Only "Dumbo," the happy little elephant, was a money maker. B UT THIS YEAR came "Cin derella," not a brunette like SSnow White, but a doe-eyed blonde. Also no little dwarfs, but once again enchanting, fantastic little mice romp through the story. Cinderella as a money maker is tieing just about even with Snow White. Australians will see it around Christmas time. The publicity men had the time of their lives. They toured the coun try with glass slippers, and when they found lovely maidens whose dainty feet would fit the slippers, they made them "Cinderellas for the Day" and packed them off to Hollywood. Now the fun is on again for the latest Disney hit, "Treasure Island;" The publicity men are handing out several million numbered keys. The holders of keys with lucky num bers will win 100,000 .dollars in prizes-handy prizes like refrigera tors and cars. "Treasure Island" is Disney's first film with live characters. It was made in England and opened there six weeks ago. It has cleaned up its cost of production in Eng land. In the first few days there, receipts bettered even thdse of "Best Ydars of Our Lives."' It has only been rinning in New York a few days, but the theatres have been packed, with "standing room only signs" outside. The boy star, Bobby Driscoll, plays the lead in "Treasure Island," and he will soon appear in another "live" Disney feature, "Robin Hood." But don't think for a min ute that full length cartoons are going out. "Alice in Wonderland" and "Peter Pan" are on the way. It is good to see Walt Disney hack in the limelight. As long as he makes films, nobody can truly say that the fun has gone out of motion pictures. -- LIFTON WEBB collapsed on a the set recently. The first Sdiagnosis was mumps, and everybody in Hollywood chuckled. After the films, "Sitting Pretty" and "Cheaper by the Dozen," Webb, a bachelor, has built himself up into such an authority on children that people thought this was poetic jus tice. The all-round genius had the last laugh. It was a virus infection.
