Gloria Swanson “Bee's Knees” From KEITH DUNSTAN In Hollywood. The "Oklahoman" reported in black headlines this week: "Here's Gloria Swanson: At 51 she's still the bee's knees." The "Oklahoman's" colourful statement was almost correct. This glamorous grandmother, who made hearts beat faster 32 years ago, was still wowing the town. Everybody agreed Gloria Swanson was better than ever; and the bet in the industry is that her comeback film, “Sun- set Boulevard," will win her an Academy award. If that’s true, “Sunset Boulevard” will be the best film of her career. Many kind things have been said about Gloria Swanson in days gone by, but few people ever said she could act. Gloria Swanson was one of the last stars who built Hollywood into a fantastic illusion composed of Duesenbergs, swimming pools, 100- room castles, leopard skins, six months' marriages, million-dollar salaries, and dry bathing beauties. She was one of the first "vamps," or celluloid oddities, that put Holly- wood on the map. She is still an extraordinary character. Last week she appeared as a cover girl on the front page of a national magazine. The pic- ture showed her lounging on a leopard skin cushion. She wore a plunging neckline dress with an odd piece of leopard skin tossed over her shoulder. She had brace- lets up to her elbow, and she was thumbing through the complete works of Plato. SUNSET BOULEVARD" is the story of everything she stood for. It begins with a corpse floating in a Bev- erly Hills swimming pool, with the dead man's voice, narrating the record of the few fantastic months preceding his death. The man is a film agent, who became tangled with Norma Desmond (Miss Swan- son), a retired star of the silent screen. The film shows the life this star led in a great Beverly Hills man- sion, cluttered up with shockingly ugly gewgaws and fripperies, such as a gold-plated piano, a private projection booth and personal pipe organ. Norma Desmond tries to make a come-back. She asks her old director, Cecil B. deMille, for a job. DeMille, who plays himself, says, "No, thank You." The film shows the tragic, bor- ing life this woman leads, made up entirely of nostalgic memories. Fin- ally, she kills her boy friend; and in a last dramatic scene, when the police and reporters rush to her house, she goes out of her mind. The film is a courageous one for two reasons. Hollywood takes a frank look at Hollywood as it once was; and Gloria Swanson plays a part which could be an unflattering mirror of hetself. Ac tually, her story is the reverse. She can name her own price on tele vision. In fact, she can look after her self so well she has never had much success with men. She has been married five times. * * * * THE KOREAN war has caused a mild panic in Hollywood. Every major studio is making war films. Battleships, jet fighters, aircraft carriers and submarines are not things which directors can easily call out from the prop de- partment. The studios depend on the armed forces for these things. But now the armed forces will be too busy to worry about Holly- wood. Nevertheless, "Korea," "South Korea," and "Crisis in Korea" have already been registered as titles with the Motion Picture Associa- tion. Shirley Temple has ended her contract with David O. Selznick. There were no hard feelings and no regrets. Now Miss Temple can take any job she likes, but she isn't finding it too easy. She doesn't want any more teen-ager roles, in stead she wants something with a little dramatic quality which will put her back among the top ladies in the industry. She is reading through half a dozen scripts in the hope she will find something to her liking. The future for Miss Temple is none too certain. * * * * * There's a remarkable new trend in Hollywood. Once upon a time, Hollywood used to steal many of its best stars from the Broadway stage, but this week the situation seemed to be in reverse. Basil Rathbone was playing in "Julius Caesar," Jean Arthur and Basil Rathbone were in "Peter Pan," Tyrone Power was signed for the London company of "Mr. Roberts," with Jackie Cooper and Jennifer Jones was due to play in a "Legend for Good Women." Katherine Hepburn several weeks ago made a very beautiful Rosalind in "As You Like It." Sometimes, we are rather cynical about the acting of film stars, but those stars who have the courage to face the Broadway stage usually do extremely well.
