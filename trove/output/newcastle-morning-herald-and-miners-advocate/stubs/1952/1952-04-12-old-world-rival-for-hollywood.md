Old WorldB$ Rivapl For Hollywood From KEITH DUNSTAN, in Hollywood -All you need to make a stack of dollars in New York is a big voice, a big hall, a spotlight and a pile of old books. Ageing film stars and vet eransi?tors are all busy in New York, reading aloud to audiences Shakespeare Shaw, Dickens and most especially the Bible. It's the latest craze. Emlyn Wilhtams, the Eng lish actor, playwright, arrived several weeks ago, fresh from making "Ivanhoe" with Eliza beth Taylor. He announced he would give an exact repeti tion of Charles Dickens trnum phant 1868 tour of the United States. As good a way of making dollars for the old country as ever I saw. He stuck a false beard on his chin, a carnation in his buttonhole, several handsome rings on his fingers, a little make-up and he was the dead ring of Dickens. Every night he stands before a carved Vic torian desk in one of Manhat tans larger theatres and reads extracts from "Tale of Two Cities," "Pickwick Papers," and "Dombey and Son." People rush along in droves to hear him, anxious to pay the equivalent of 45/ a seat. John Carradine, a tall cada verous looking character, who has played the villian in 200 Hollywood films, is reading Shakespeare in a Greenwich Village night club. Those people who go to that part of town to see naked dancing girls and have a whoopee time are finding the change rather as tonishing. Claude Rains and Herbert Marshall are reading, too, but the ~iost extraordinary success is the drama ouartet reading of "Don Juan in Hell.' Charles Laughton, Charles Boyer, Sir Cedric Hardwicke and Agnes Moorhead have read this Ber nard Shaw piece to audiences all over the U.S. and England. HEY use no scenery, they don't even dress up, they simply read from the book. But when the news came they were on their way to New York, Carnegie Hall sold every seat within eight hours of the announcement. There was a terrible to do. The management was so busy hauling in the gold it forgot to reserve seats for the drama critics. So they had to go and try and buy or bribe them back. It was mostly bribe. Some people returned them on con dition they received equally rare seats for the Sadler's Well ballet and one woman handed over hers on condition, on the special condition, she re ceived a back stage interview, alone, with Charles Boyer. This readingcraze all start ed with Charles Laughton. During the war he wanted to do something to entertain ser vicemen, but he couldn't think what. He didn't dance, sing, juggle, whistle or stand on hIis head. And even worse, he was unlovely of appearance with a figure so rotund that it made even a new suit look like a sack of mallee roots. So he took up reading the Bible and extracts from Shakespeare. To the amaze ment of the generals he was a bigger hit than the song and dance acts; FTER the war . he carried on o 'his . act from town to 'town across America, and even now, although he can make 100,000 dollars a film. he believes this is his life work and what he likes best. H e said: "I found people everywhere hungry for cul ture. People want the reassur ance of good things that have stood the test of time. I plead for more reading aloud. It is a friendly, quiet and thor oughly refreshing thing to do." So you see in New York in 1952 we have turned the full cycle and Hollywood has a deadly rival. New Yorkers, if they wish, can twiddle the plastic knobs of their television sets, see bands, beauties, dome dians, exotics shows, sometimes being turned on at a cost of 120,000 dollars an hour to some cigarette or soap firm. New Yorkers can also go to their theatres and see "Quo Vadis" with Rome being burnt down at a cost of 12 million dollars. They can go to the largest theatre in the world at Rockefeller Plaza and see 100 lanky girls kicking their un clad elegant legs in perfect time. But no, New Yorkers by the thousand are ignoring all these things to hear middle aged film stars read from the classics. The New York Times remarked: "Could we perchance be getting just a wee bit tired of mechanical wonders? Are things getting a little too mir aculous for comfort and do we long to return to the dozy impgfections of the Victor ian age?" I'm sure some of us wouldn't mind a scrap.
