Gershwin Music Booming From KEITH DUNSTAN in Hollywood It is 14 years since George Gershwin collapsed and died from a tumour on the brain, aged 38. It was like the sudden eclipse of Rudolph Valen tino. Gershwin was so very alive, so much a spokesman for young people. His death seemed unbelievable. This year's open air Gersh win concert at Lewiston Sta dium attracted 20,000, which is a record here for any type of musical programme. The New York Philhar monic also gave a Gershwin concert at Carnegie Hall, and this was a sellout. The record companies are churning out all the Gershwin songs, and there is an ex tremely ambitious rendering of the folk-opera, "Porgy and Bess," complete on . long playing records. .r~ HERE'S no doubt that Gershwin is in America's blood. Every radio' station has a Gershwin pro gramme and it is difficult to turn the dial without picking up "I've Got Rhythm," or "Strike Up the Band." For this reason it was hardly surpgrising that "An American in Paris," with music* by Gershwin, was America's top money-making film for October. It has been such a success in city after city that it will certainly go down on the record as one of the best musicals ever to come out of Hollywood. It is an old story. The British have the actors, the Russians the dancers, the French can make love, and Americans turn out the musi cals. And it is true they have refined the musical to a su perb streamlined product. I know there have been some appalling, musicals from Hollywood. We all know the musical about the mid-west ern college that is going broke, so the students decide to put on a show, which, after a few'hitches, is a huge suc cess. They save the college, but by a curious coincidence a Broadway producer happens to be sitting in the front row and little Betty Grable or Alice Faye, whoever it is playing a cute co-ed, suddenly rockets to romance and star dom. But every now and then we have a musical that is differ ent and "An American in Paris" is different. It is warm, alive and original. It has three big advantages--Gene Kelly, beautiful colour and Gershwin. Kelly is an energetic, amus ing, rather determined Ameri can-Irishman. If you spotted him on Sunset Boulevard he would be wearing probably blue jeans, T; shirt, white cot ton socks and moccasins. T HIS is the very latest style. Once a Holly 'wood star had to drive a Jaguar or a Mercedes, wear sun glasses, a super sports coat with reindeer shoulders, a broad-brimmed white hat, fancy silk tie, pastel shade slacks and maybe two-tone shoes. But now he has to be all rugged up with a short haircut in the Kirk Douglas, Mont gomery Clift, Macdonald Carey tradition For years Kelly had only run-of-the-mill parts, but last year he created a record- for the longest queue ever outside New York's Radio City Music Hall-nine blocks long. He has become one of the top stars in the business, and he earns an incredible 2500 dollars a week. Kelly is a dancer and he rivals Astaire as the greatest ever to come to Hollywood. His dancing differs from As taire in that it has always been of a more serious kind and more closely related to ballet. That is why, at 39, it took him so long to reach the very top. The Press agents say he was always loved by the wo men, in fact, a man was never loved more. But a female fol lowing is not enough. No male star has ever hit the big time in Hollywood unless both men and women were crazy about him. Kelly, apparently, now has this double following. "Embraceable You," "'S Won derful," and a remarkable bal let rendering of Gershwin's fine musical suite "An Ameri can In Paris." The film is actually worth seeing for its color alone. It is an example -of how much more intelligent film producers on both sides of the Atlantic .have become in their use of it. S".ORGY and Bess" was still a great success on Broad way, and the "Herald Tri bune". said it was impossible to visualise America without George Gershwin. But America 'isn't really without* Gershwin. His music is as popular now-no, it's more popular than it ever was. He was the first com poser to write music that was all American. He took jazz out of "Tin pan Alley" and made the world realise that here was a new style of music. He put America' with all its fuss and breezy "let's go to town" spirit into sound, As a fantastically, success ful young man he was a spokesman for the crazy 20's. and he portrayed the spirit of the drug stores and the honky tonks on Third Avenue. He brought a freshness and vitality to music. He spoke the popular language, and the people loved him for it. Not only does it show some beautiful views of Paris, but the producer, Vincent Minnelli, makes wide use of single dom inant colours, and in the great ballet sequence he has used sets inspired by the paintings of Dufy, Rousseau, Utrillo, Toulouse Lautrec, Renoir and Van Gogh. It's all a far cry from the early days of Technicolor. Do you remember how they used to spray on colour as if it came out of fire hoses? Every girl had to be a red head or a canary yellow blonde. So in this film there is a combination of new and old techniques. It is refreshing to go back to the 80's with Gershwin, a time when a rich tiow of melody was consider ed better than a groan.
