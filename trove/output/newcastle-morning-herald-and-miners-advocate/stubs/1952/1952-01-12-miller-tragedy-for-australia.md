Miller Tragedy For Australia From KEITH DUNSTAN in Hollywood Arthur 'Miller is the great hope of the American theatre, and possibly the finest American playwright since Eugene O'Neill. Miller, who is 36, has a big name here and in Europe. In 1949 his now famous tragedy, "Death of a Sales man," came to Broadway. It won the Drama Critics' Award and the Pulitzer Prize, the two highest awards a play could win. The critics said it was the best play for many, many years. It made for Miller the best part of two million dollars. The people who control the large theatre chains in Aus tralia read the script, decided that it had no message for Australians and that they wouldn't understand it, any way. That is why you may not have heard about Arthur Miller. Yet "Death of a Salesman" in the last two years has play ed in London, Stockholm, Prague, Paris, Rome, Budapest, Mexico City, Buenos Aires, Vienna, Israel, Athens and Zurich. ALES of the play in book form ran into the hundreds of thousands in the United States alone. It was translated into 28 lan guages. No other play can equal this record, except pos sibly "Streetcar Named De sire," which gained an extra notoriety through controversy. Now at this late date Aus tralians will have their chance to see "Death of a Salesman" on film. Mr. Miller, a most uncompromising gentleman, has nothing but contempt for Hollywood. He believes Hollywood has turned the stage into a great stud farm fo'r its big Wild West machine, a machine which eats up talent and ruins authors who could be writing something worth while. These men could be writing the na tion's books, plays and verse. Instead, they scribble scripts for films which have no con nection with life and reality. He sold the rights of "Death of a Salesman" to Stanley Kramer. The result was a big success. Most critics have placed it high on their lists of 1951's best films. Kramer barely altered the script. He put Fredric March in the leading role. "Death of a Salesman" as a play is tragic, moving. At the same time, it has some thing to say, and so it is ex hilarating. It ghattered a num ber of American illusions. HE play was the story of Willy Lohman, a self important, blustering little travelling salesman. He lie lieved he could grab success easily with a big smile, a firm handshake, and by being "well liked." He woke up at 63 to discover his life was a sham. He had offered nothing to society and received nothing in return, except disillusionment and finally suicide. In the past, surveys have shown Hollywood producers that the average age of the filmgoer is 17 or 18, There fore they have directed every thing at the teen-age market. But recent films like "Death of a Salesman," "Streetcar Named Desire," and "The Red Badge of Courage" are signs of a new awakening in Hollywood, signs of better and more intelligent films to come. Stanley Kramer is one of the big hopes of Hollywood. He was one of the first to prove that serious films, speak ing to an adult audience, could make money and a great deal of it. He was also on of the first to prove the fal lacy of the star system. He showed that people would pay to see a great story, even when it didn't sport on the billboards any of the fabul ous Hollywood names. He made his reputation with "Champion," "Home of the Brave" and "Cyrano de Ber gerac." Before he was an in dependent. Now, he has made a deal with Columbia. "Death of a Salesman" is the first of 30 he will make over the next five years, with a capital outlay of 25 million dollars. T HIS does not mean you will see scads of superb pictures, but there may be a good one just every so often. The outlook is promising. Apart from Kramer, there are a number of directors who are making films considerably bet ter than the fare we used to get several years ago. The best of these are Joseph Mankiewicz, Elia Kazan, Wal ter Huston and William Wyler. People are starting to look for these names; that in itself is a sign that the public is be coming educated to better films. The director more than anyone else puts the stamp of his personality on a film, not the Gables and the Grables they do as they're told. Because of the star system, and perhaps the poor quality of films, all but a few direc tors, famous for their exhibi tionism, have been ignored. The Europeans with their ex cellent art films treated their directors more kindly.
