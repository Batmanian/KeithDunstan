Hollywood0& Beams About Its Future From KEITH DUNSTAN, in Hollywood Hollywood is beaming with satisfaction and confidence for the future. Everything has gone off splendidly, and the studio chiefs are patting each other on the back. SJanuary is the month' when the experts pull the past year to pieces and exa mine the tiny fragments through their often cloudy test tubes. Well,' 1951 was the great turning year, the year when Hollywood lifted itself out of a depression and won back the favour of the public. Movie folk, for the first time in years, were able to fill their swim ming pools and pay the back instalments on their Cadillacs. The studios chose two ways to improve their box office receipts. One group decided the way to make nloney was to turn on a whopping spec tacle, so big, so outlandish, so vulgar, that no one could afford to miss it, with no end of lions, elephants, gladiators, fights, wars, and girls, girls, girls and lots of girls. They followed the old adage: "One lion is good, but 50 lions are 50 times as rgood." The second group made films which featured well told, in telligent stories, screened on a low budget. Both groups made money and they scorned the B, that second-rate picture which you mnay have to suffer before see ing the main feature. The B, which stands for "Boredom," is now going out. The studios realise they must make pic tures goodt enough or extra ordinary enough to make people leave their television sets. Films of the B quality are on television by the dray load. HE film which made more money than any othiler in 1951 was "David and Bathsheba,"' a story from the Bible, pepped up and made gorgeous by 20th Century Fox with Gregory Peck and Susan Hayward as the star exponents of Biblical love. Isn't that curious? For two years now, gaudy versions of Biblical tales have been the top money-makers. In 1950 "Samson andt Delilah" was the big success. The New Yorker comment ed on this with a cartoon show ing a window display of Bibles and a great sign: "You've seec the movie-NOW read the book!" Second and third on 1951's list were two good musicals, "Showboat" and "An Ameri can in Paris." Fourth was "The Great Caruso," with Mario Lanza. Yet the pictures which re ceived top billing from thae New York film critics were "Streetcar Named Desire," with Vivian Leigh; "Bright Victory," with Arthur Ken nedy; and "A Place in the Sun," with Montgomery Clift and Elizabeth Taylor. P ERHAPS more than anything else 1951 was a year for new stars. The old greats were still making a picture every so often, but they weren't stir ring up the passions' and cre ating little dream worlds in the hearts of the filmgoers as they used to. Barbara Stanwyck was a little more grey, Gary Cooper looked tired about the eyes and even more languid than usual. Bette Davis was a little haggard; Joan Crawford, Loretta Young, Irene Dunne, Claudette Colbert, MvIarlene Dietrich, Joel McCrae, Ray Milland, Fred MacMurrayand MIelvyn Douglas were little more than food for the columnists. Now there is a strong group of yonuger stars in their place. "Variety," the trade paper of the film world, lists these names as the top stars of the moment: Gregory Peck, Burt Lancaster, Gene Kelly, Kirk Douglas, Dean Martin and Jerry Lewis, Clif ton Webb, Red Skelton, Rob ert Mitchum, Montgomery Clift, Jane Russell, Esther Wil liams, Susan Hayward, Jeanne Crain, Virginia Mayo, Doris Day, Shelley Winters and Elizabeth Taylor. The newcomers to the game are: Steve Cochran, Howard Keel, Gene Nelson, Gary Mer. rill, Jose Ferrer, Jeff Chand ler, Louis Jourdan, Tony Cur tis, Judy Holliday, Debra Pag et, Jean Peters, Sally Forrest and Nancy Olsen. The studios are enthusiastic about their new talent. There is none of the pessimism of three years ago when the only promising star they could think of was Elizabeth Taylor. JANUARY is also the month for talking .of the future. One soothsayer said tihe people of Anlerica didn't alp preciate the magnificent work that was being done in Holly wood. But, he said, Shake speare and Ben Jonson were treated the same way by the Elizabethans. In 300 years this would be looked upon as a great age. Studios would be museums, scripts and original films would be of priceless value and the names of stars and producers would be great in American history with streets and towns named in their hon our. However, soothsayers coln cerned with the more imme diate future think Hollywood is in for a period of great prosperity, and that good stor ies coupled with old-fashioned Hollywood showmanship will beat the television bogy. HEATRE tele vision, probably in full colour, will certainly spread all over the nation. A great many theatres al ready have it in black and white. Theatre TV is quite simple. If you had it at your theatre in Newcastle, it would work this way: Instead of a second feature, the theatre would project on the screen from a television set the fight at Newcastle Sta dium, perhaps the current show at a theatre; or, if it were a matinee, maybe the tennis or cricket. More than 30 theatres have theatre TV here in the eastern States of the United States. The big theatre chains buy the TV rights to fights, foot ball matches and shows. It is the film industry's challenge to television, and, in the long run, it has only one difference-people see the fights and football games at the theatre, instead of in the comfort of their own houses. This gives more power to HIollywood. It is still the world's greatest entertainment factory, and 60 million Ame ricans still go to the movies every week. So you see neither television, the race tracks, the pubs, baseball, 25 cent. best sellers-no, not even the comics-can compete with this colossus.
