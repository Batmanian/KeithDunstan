All Hollywood Not Crazy From KEITH DUNSTAN, In Hollywood Most people think Hollywood is a great, dazzling, neon-lit metropolis, somewhere in Southern California, and inhabited mostly by lunatics, heretics, beautiful women in bathing costumes and eccentric millionaires. Actually, Hollywood is a minor suburb of Los Angeles, inhabited by studio employees who work for union-scale wages not vastly different from those in any other industry. The proportion of beautiful women, lunatics, heretics and mil lionaires is only slighltly higher than in other American suburbs. Yet after Washington and New York, Hollywood is the world's largest source of news. Nearly 400 correspondents, working on nothing else but film news, ser vice newspapers all over the world. Hovering around these news papermen is a vast .army of Press agents, employed almost in com panies and platoons by the major studios and independently by the stass. Their object is to turn out a constant supply of extraordin ary news stories. When the going gets tough, they are prepared to cook up fake law suits, weird stunts and unusual functions. They have done more than any other group to give Holywood its leg endary fame. The crazy publicity dodges come quickly when an expensive film is beginning to die at the box of fice, or when a young starlet is ready for fame. The classic case was "Duel in the Sun," a film which was savagely and unanimously "panned" all the way from New York to Melbourne. Even David Selznick, the pro ducer, had few illusions about it. But he had to do something, for the darn thing had cost six mil lion dollars, So a Press agent quietly wrote a letter to a Texas newspaper, stating that although the story was about Texas, all the scenes were photographed in Ari zona. The reaction was splendid. Texas was furious. The newspapers de manded that the picture be ban ned-it was an outrage against the honour of Texas. Of course, Arizona didn't like it either. The citizens were so offended, that several members of the State leg islature introduced. a bill in an attempt to stop Texans from coss. ting the border into Arizona. From there, "Duel in the Sun" really got going. Balloons an lolln.:ing "Duel Day" soared over the cities, 20,000 Duel-T shirts were distributed along with candy bars, ash trays, pencils, card packs anC light bulbs. The cities were lathered with stickers. Even signs were pasted on circus elephants. "Duel in the Sun" grossed one of the greatest sums in film history -10,750,000 dollars. It was a very bad film. THE stunts aren't difficult to arrange. Back in the days when they were trying to build up Jimmy Cag ney as a tough man, a shockingly beat-up man staggered into a New Jersey police station and collapsed. The police sergeant carefully called a reporter and said: "Say, you sure look like that film star fella Jimmy. Cagney." The poor devil moaned. "Yeah, that's the trouble. Every time I go into a bar, just for a quiet drink, mind you, some tough has to come up and say, 'O.K., Cagney; come on outside-I want to see if you're as tough as you look." But the people who need the publicity are the young starlets. Perhaps you haven't heard of Piper Laurie. She is a pretty young lady of 18. Universal is putting her in starring roles for the first time. A Press agent pushed her into the limelight by announcing that here was a girl who liked nothing better than to sit down to a meal of salted gardenias and camellias. The story made an agreeable sen sation. There are dozens of these stories every year. Actually, they are good fun. They don't really do anyone any harm, except that they give the world the impression that Holly wood is the most cock-eyed place on earth. As long as Hollywood dloesn't mind, nobody else does. THE latest cycle is, naturally enough, atom bombs. One is called "Five," which deals with the five people left on earth after an atomic war. Another, called "To the Centre of the Earth," is about a search for uranium. A third is titled "The Day the Atom Bomb Fell"-a story of national hysteria and confusion. It seems the British got in first with "Seven Days to Noon."
