Bullfighting . New Film Craze From KEITH DUNSTAN, in Hollywood. Of the world's savage, brutal, individual sports' we have cockfighting, bear-baiting and bullfighting, and of these the meanest and most dramatic is bullfighting. It is a straight conflict be tween man and animal.' One of the two must be blooded--either the animal must die or the man must be killed or gored. It can not end in a draw. Bullfighting is popular in the Americas, particularly in Mexico. There it is a huge industry. Bulls are trained more carefully than racehorses. Their blood pedigrees go back to the fighting bulls of Spain. The bulls are bred to be killed. They are bred for one day only, a glorious day in the arena. They cannot be used twice, for after one day they know too many-tricks of the matador. Mexicans say foreigners criti cise bullfighting because they don't understand it. They say bullfight ing for them is a ritual which goes back deep into their history and is symbolic of their way of life. Bullfighting began as a hunt for meat mn the mountains of Spain, and later became a sporting pas time through the centuries, acquir ing extraordinary grace and skill. Mexicans believe bullfighting is not a sport, but a form of drama, an art, a great spectacle. They say it is the only art in which the artist deals death and risks death. It is designed to show the glory of courage. That is Tom Lea's explanation, which he gave in his novel "The Brave Bulls." And now bullfighting is fascin ating Hollywood: and we have moved into another great cycle. There are currently three bull fighting films being released here. Robert Rossen's adaptation of Lea's book with the same title, "The Brave Bulls." Albert Lewin's "Pan dora and the Flying Dutchman," and Oscar Boetticher's "Death in the Sands." \ 'r HE MOST im Spressive of these is "The Brave Bulls," a film which the "New York Times" described as not only the best film on bull fighting so far, but the best we are likely to see in our. time. The star is Mel Ferrer, 33, no relation to Jose Ferrer. He is a serious and extremely talented actor. To play the role of bull fighter he lived the life of a" pro fessional fighter in Mexico for many months. He wsorked with the toreros and followed their exact training schedule. Ferrer says it is largely by his work with the heavy cape that a fighter is judged. "Every sweep of the cape is im portant," he says. An experienced fighter can stop the bull or turn him around in his own length. The tradition that red infuriates the bull is fiction. It is the movement only that is important. The animal must be made to rush at the cape, not at the man. "Practically every boy and every young.man in Mexico City hopes to become a bullfighter," says Fer rer. "The small part of one of the fighters in the picture was filled by an elevator boy at our hotel. He had been gored, and he was proud of a big scar he had across his face." Apart from a rather stupid love theme, Robert Rossen, the producer of "All the King's Men," has turned this film into another master piece. Rossen gives it everything n strange drama, the stark, brassy, arrogant music which accompanies the bull fight, the screaming, fickle crowd, "hungry for food and hungry for excitement." The object of all the cheering, yet terribly alone in the centre of the arena, is the matador. Mr. Ferrer shows very" well how the matador, outwardly courageous, can be so utterly terrified. Terrified in the knowledge that the sharp horns of the bull can so easily rip his groin or toss him in the air. The Production Code has not permitted the actual killing of the bulls to be shown, but Mr. Rossen has got around this cleverly enough. Fven so, it is not exactly a pleasant picture. Bull fighting is not pleas ant. SEQUELS, parti cularly film se quels, are nearly always stale and dull. But now we have an exception. Last yeqr, probably the year's most successful film, was "Father of the Bride." It was the story of the hell a father goes through when his daughter gets married. The great sufferer was Spencer Tracy, the mother Joan Bennett, and the daughter Elizabeth Taylor Now we have the sequel, "Father's .ittle Dividend." The theme is ohvious. Spencer Tracy, who thought his troubles wrercr over now goes through the hell of becomine a grandfather. The critics all agree that it is as good as the original, if not better. Just now it is attracting bigger houses and making more money than any other film in the United States. It just shows you-people like families and babies best. T HERE is another slump in the film industry, all brought about by the General Mac Arthur controversy. People are staying at home te watch the great debates and the many parades on television rather than go to the pictures. Attendances are down 10 to 35 per cent. So far 20th Century Fox is win ning the race on the life of Mac Arthur, with a film to be titled either "The MacArthur Story" or "Old Soldiers Never Die." They have a character for the lead named Robert Barrat, 60, who is apparently the "spittin' image" of MacArthur.
