Welles' "Macbeth SIDMay Flop From KEITH DUNSTAN, in Hollywood. Orson Welles, now a rather jaded genius of 35 years, has tackled New York with a start ling screen version of "Macbeth." A Macbeth, acted by Orson Welles, produced by Orson Welles, directed by Orson Welles, and all but written by Orson Welles. The film came here last year, but preview audiences said they could not make head nor tail of his unintelligible Scotch ac cents; so it went back to him for revision. Mr. Welles then had to un derake the laborious and costly job of remaking the dialogue in "English." Welles's version of Macbeth has nothing of the rare insight and in spiration Sir Laurence Olivier gave "Henry V." and "Hamlet," but it has certain qualities. He gives a picture of the incredible cruelty and savagery of the time without any great interpretation of Shakespeare. He uses all thle tricks of _hoto gmraphy, which made "Citizen Kane" such a curiosity. The audience never looks' Macbeth straight in the face. It is either a mouse on the floor or a fly on the ceiling. These angles make Welles' shaggy head look the size of that of a lion. The actors always seem half in the dark. The castle of Dunsinane seems not so much a castle, but a damp terrifying version of the Jeno lan Caves. There is no let up. The film is filled with insane screams, clanking of chains, shadows of figures hanging by the neck and the croaks of harpies. I T IS unlikely to make much money here. Americans have now rejected Mr. Welles. They are inclined to raise one eye brow at his exploits. A different story from his early days when frort two years on he was regarded as a coming Tost canini, Paderewski, Valasquez, Tol stoy, Moliere or Garrick. At 15 he was a sensation as a portrait painter, at 16 as an actor, and at 23 the extraordinary radio nnouncer who announced the ar rival of the Martians. Now he is more welcome mn Paris than Los Angeles, where they claim him as a penniless but lovable genius who renounced the "iniqui ties" of Hollywood. But Welles may conquer yet. His version of "Othello" will arrive here soon. There is talk that he is work ing on the film adaptations of Homer's "IUiad" and "Odyssey." STEWART GRANGER and Jean Simmons spent their honey moon motoring through the sunny south of the United States. They moved into their all-electric bridal house on New Year's Eve. It cost £36,000. A little later, a reporter saw them having a ineal at a Beverly Hills drugstore. "None of our pots and pans have arrived," said Stewart. "Neither have the sheets from England," said Jean. "So we'll be sleeping on the floor," said Stewart. "And eating out," concluded Jean. First American film for Jean Simmons may be "Young Bess," a novel by Margaret Irwin about Queen Elizabeth's struggle for sur vival in the scramble for power after the death of Henry VIII. M.G.M. has the screen rights to the novel: J. Arthur Rank is anxious that she should get the part. BILLY WILDER, the Hollywood director who enticed Sir Laur ence Olivier to Hollywood for one film, has done the same with Maurice Chevalier. He flew to Paris last month and offered Chev alier the lead in a musical entitled "A New Kind of Love." To every one's surprise, Chevalier accepted. It is 15 years since he left Hollywood, with the words: "I no longer get a kick out of making films here." -JERE is the complete list of the stars Americans paid most money to see in 1950 in the order 'of their popularity: John Wayne, Bob Hope, Bing Crosby, Betty Grable, James Stewart,'Abbott and Costello, Clifton Webb, Esther Williams, Spencer Tracy, Randolph Scott, Gary Cooper, Gregory Peck, Clark Gable, June Allyson, Betty Hutton, Burt Lancaster, Red Skel ton, Van Johnson, Roy Rogers, and Cary Grant. Bob Hope and Bing Crosby both slid down a place to make way for John Wayne. Spencer Tracy miss ed the top 20. altogether last year to come up to 9th place. James Stewart rose from Ilth to 5th. Clark Gable, for the first time, was not among the first 10. His posi tion was unhappily 13. SOMERSET MAUGHAM'S posi tion as a film script writer now seems reasonably secure. Both "Trio" and "Quartet" were such a money-making success here that Cusick International Films has bought the rights to the Maugham short story, "His Excellency."
