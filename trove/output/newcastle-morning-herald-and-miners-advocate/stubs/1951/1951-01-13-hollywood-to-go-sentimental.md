Hollywood To Go . Sentimental From KEITH DUNSTAN, in Hollywood. Hollywood, making less and less money as the weeks go'by, has an idea that maybe a few tears, a few memories and a few old songs might attract people back to picture theatres. The studio chiefs believe the average human being is a very sentimental fellow. All he needs is a few old songs, such as "Star Dust" and "Polka Dots and Moonbeans," to let loose a flood of nostalgic memories. The past is always better than the present, so a few old songs is all that is necessary to provide a perfect evening. It all started several months back with Betty Grable and Dan. Dailey, in "My Blue Heaven." This had a selection of old songs. It was a hit. Then 20th Century Fox let loose "I'll Get By," with June Haver, Gloria De Haven and William Lundigan. It had 16 old songs, including "The Yankee Doodle Blues," "McNamara's Band" and "Deep in the Heart of Texas." Now, another long, painful cycle is on its way. Studios are mak ing films which feature these songs: "I'm in the Mood for Love," "I Don't Want to Walk Without You," "Just One of Those Things," "Tea For Two," "I Want to be Happy," "Sonmebody Loves Me," "Lullaby of Broadway," "I Only Have Eyes For You," and many others. EV?EN the deadly serious screen version of "Streetcar Named Desire" will toss in one old song, "Paper Moon," to get he right mood for one scene. The studios are following the unwritten law that the old fav ourites must be at least 10 years old. As Sam Goldwyn once said: "Nostalgia was not built in a day." The "Wall-street Journal" pub lish:-i these figures on film at tendances. In 1946, 85 million Americans went to the pictures every week. This declined stead ily till by 1949, when the figure had dropped to 62 million a week. In 1950, it fell again to 57 mi! lion. Theatre chains throughout the country reported drops in at tendance in 1950 ranging from 15 to 40 per cent. R OBERTO ROSSELLINI is in trouble again. The film cen sors have banned his latest film, "The Miracle," from every theatre in town. "The Miracle" began extremely vell at the P'aris Theatre. The critics praised it highly and voted it tihe best foreign language film for 1950. Then New York's Commissioner of Licences (Mr. Edward McCaf frey) saw it. Although he had re ceived no complaints, he decided the film was blasphemous and banned it. A battle is taking place. Furious members of the motion picture in dustry are writing letters to news papers. The PI'aris Theatre is giving free passes to every patron so they can return after the ban is lifted. King Vidor, a veteran motion picture director, said in the "New York Times:" "It is too bad the movies cannot approach life as people know it. Like everyone in the motion picture industry, I'm opposed to censorship."
