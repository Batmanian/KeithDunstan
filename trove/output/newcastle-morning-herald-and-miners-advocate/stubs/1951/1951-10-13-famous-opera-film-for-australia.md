Famous Opera Film For Australia From KEITH DUNSTAN in Hollywood At last Australians will soon be able to hear Anna Maria Alberghetti and the works of the brilliant Italian-American composer Gian Carlo Menotti. Menotti's film version of his opera "The Medium" has had its world premiere in New York, and among those present were Sarah Churchill. and Margaret Truman. It is now settling down for a long run. Copies will soon be on their way to Australia. "The Medium" introduces on film for the first time Ann Maria Alber ghetti, 15. She is beautiful with that deep kind of beauty so common amohg Italian women. She is a'Pier Angell with a voice, and a truly superb voice at that. She was just another little girl, quite unknown, when she came to the United States last year. She re mained unknown till she sang at Carnegie Hall. . Then she became one of the most sought-after singers in the world. After her performance, the audience stood up and cheered; crowds of people rushed forward to congratu late her. One man jumped up and down and shouted with tears in his eyes: "Un angelo del paradiso!" Even the not-too-easily-moved "New York Times" crtiic admitted: "It's true. She did sing like an angel." From there, she went on to sing to 13,000 people at the great Lew isohn Stadium. She gave concerts at 750 to 1500 dollars a perform ance. ANNA has poise, extraordinary ability, and a great under standing of music. She comes from a musical family. Her brother, Paolo, aged 7, is a prodigy, and re cently conducted a concert in Phila delphia. Ann complained recently: "Every time we go on the stage to gether, he takes the show away." Menotti decided that Anna was exactly what he needed for his film version of "The Medium." Menotti is without doubt a great composer. Many .American .critics say he has made the only genuine contribution to opera in the past 50 years. This is rather cavalier treat ment of the works of Benjamin Britten, Stravinsky and Prokofiev. But Menotti has done this: To the millions of Americans who consider opera an antique art, unutterably boring, he has shown them that modern opera; sung in English, can be powerful, dramatic and deeply moving. Now 39, he has written six operas. The best of these are "The Medium" and "The Consul." He has won the Pulitzer Prize for music and most of the highest awards for musical drama. "The Medium" opened here in 1947 and ran for seven months, a record for any kind of opera in New York City. It is the story of Madame Flora, an aged medium who feels the touch of an ice-cold hand about her throat at a phony seance. Gradu ally she goes out of her wits with fear, believing that she really has spiritualistic powers. Victims of her madness are her two assistants, a daughter aged 14, and a deaf mute. FEW people have succeeded in reproducing opera on film but Menotti did it well with "The Me dium." Except for Maria Alber ghetti, he used his New York cast. Marie Powers, a contralto, played the part of the medium, and Leo 'loleman the deaf mute. There is so much of Rome, pov erty and suffering in this film, that it is an out-and-out Italian produc tion. And one thing is clear from Italian films-Italians, perhaps more than any other people, excel at creating real characters, real emotions and heart-moving situa tions. Menotti said he had no trouble in finding colourful extras. One can see them in "The Medium." Extraordinary Italians, faces full of character, no stereotyped people such as you see in Hollywood. These people don't have to act they live. Menotti was born near Lake Lugano, the ninth child of a father who made a fortune in the export import business, and a musical mother. He started composing songs at the age of six. He was a first-rate pianist, and he became a favourite at the salons of Italy's most distinguished families. In Menotti's own words, he be came an insufferable brat. His mother decided that the' only cure was to send him to America, where he would find a new atmosphere and new friends. At 17, Menotti went to study in Philadelphia. He arrived lonely, shy and unable to speak English. It was a wise decision. It taught him humility and, for the first time in his life, he applied himself to music with concentration and energy. Later he came uinder the eye of Arturo Toscanini. One night at a rehearsal of "The Consul," with no sets or costumes, Toscanini sat in the stalls. At the finish, Tosca nini walked up to Menotti with tears in his eyes, pulled him into an embrace, and sat down again without a word.
