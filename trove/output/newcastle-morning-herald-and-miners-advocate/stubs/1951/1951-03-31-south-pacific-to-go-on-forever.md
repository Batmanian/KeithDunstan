"South Pacific" To Go On "Forever" From KEITH DUNSTAN, in Hollywood. "South Pacific," the most successful show New York has seen, goes on for days, months, years--in fact, it seems likely to last with Broadway for all eternity. This musical comedy has be come part of New York, like the Empire State Building, the Statue of Liberty, and the Wash ington Square Arch. Already actors have grown tired and weary, and like life it self passed on their parts to younger and more vigorous players. England will see it maybe around the end of this year. Australia's turn will come in 1952. Then, no doubt, it will become oart of the Australian scene, like 6 o'clock closing, and no gas after 7. It is almost two years since "South Pacific" opened here, yet mail orders for seats are still com ing in at the rate of 3000 daily. The theatre is booked out for six months to all those who cannot afford to pay 50 dollars to the ticket scalpers. "South Pacific" has become the rage, the fashion, a cult; and any one who has not seen It is some thing of a pariah. Brooks AtKinson in his "New York Times" review bluntly ordered: "Everyone in America must see 'South Pacific' to-morrow night." And th.-t apparently is what America tried to do. People went to outrageous lengths to try and bribe the box office. One man offered unlimited supplies of lobsters, another several tolls of flea powder. Edgar F. Luckenbach, jun., heir to a shipping fortune, claimed, without a blush, that he got tickets by swopping them for /a "couple of ships." Indeed, "South Pacific" is a phenomenon. Take, for example, the fact that New Yorkers consider "Annie Get Your Gun," "Oklahoma!" and "Kiss Me, Kate" mere trifles by comparison, and there you have it. S OUTI-H Pacific" was put toget her by the old and much exalted team of Richard Rodgers, Oscar Hammerstein 2nd and JoshuauLogan. They took tn, story from James Michener's "Tales of thie South Pacific," and created a musical drama. Yet with all its comedy and hilarity, they put over, so the critics say, some of the misery, boredom and slaugh ter of war. But most remarkable of all are the songs and lyrics-they have dug themselves into American folk lore. The radio stations, even after all this time, play "I'm in Love with a Wonderful Guy," and "I'Tn Gonna Wash that Man Right Outa My Hair," as if they had nothing else. The records are a muilion dol lar business by themselves. The show started with Ezio Pinza (the ladies love to pronounce it Eeeeczio Peeeeenza), the great Italian who walked across from the Metropolitan Opera House t aladd lustre to Broadway. He proved clearly that men of 58 still make superb lovers, and a whole flock of elderly, society bobbysoxers started to run after him. But a year or so of romance, being thought a wonderful guy by the same women, five nights a week plus two matinees, began to wea on him, so he left Broadway for Hollywood, and Lana Turner. George Sanders was to take hi place, but Mr. Sanders's doctor has warned him that his bad back would not stand it, and Roy Middle ton stepped in. Mary Martin is the leading lady. She is no little girl either, for she has a son of 19. She wore her hair cut short almost to the bone, and the women of New York thought this so ravishing that for months they wore th ir hair cut short, almost to the bone. It has changed since, for a Paris hairdresser has told them it is more feminine to wear it long. Miss Martin will leave South Pacific on June 1 and Martin Wright will take her place. That is not right. Miss Martin won't leave "South Pacific" for a long, long time. She will play the lead in London and probably further afield-per haps even Australia. T IE "archaic and dying" medium, the legitimate theatre, is having more and more fascination for Hollywood. On the stage here we have had Rex Harrison (the tabloids insist on calling him Sexy Rexy), Lilli Palmer, Gloria Swanson, Jean Arthur, Louis Calhern, Clive Brook, Claude Raines, Katherine Hepburn, Boris Karloff and Basil Rathbone. Tyrone Power went to London to play in "Mr. Roberts." Now even the tempestuous Shelley Winters and Ginger Rogers are expressing a yearning for the stage. We need only Errol Flynn to play Richard III. and Hollywood will have arrived. Most of these stars are accept ing stage parts at a fraction of their normal film salaries. The most interesting case is that of Olivia de Havilland. She is. with Bette Davis, perhaps the most suc cessful dramatic actress Hollywood has. produced in the past 10 years. She has been nominated for an academy award eight times and she has won the best actress award twice. Her last three films grossed 15 million dollars and she can ask 200,000 dollars a picture. She can take .ny picture she wants, yet she has given up films for two years. Furthermore, she paid 100,000 dollars of her own money to get out of a ;ontract all so that she can play Juliet in "Romeo and Juliet" on Btoadway at one-fifth of her normal salary. It is a big risk. The mere name of de Havilland on Bioadway is not enough. Only 12 American actresses have tried Juliet in the past 50 years, and many of them flopped.
