Josephine Baker From KEITH DUNSTAN, in Hollywood. Josephine "La Bakhair" came to Broadway this week for the first time in 15 years. Her brown ankles flashed high in the spotlight, her shoulders rocked in perpetual motion and her. hips revolved with a bewitch ing speed that set the crowds yel ling for more. Josephine in raptures opened her arms wide and in a voice as warm as a Mallee breeze, she said: "You like me huh! You loooove me a leetle bit, too?" It has taken, more than 30 years to do. it, but it seems at last that 1951 will be Josephine Baker's American year. She left St. Louis as just another chorus girl soon after the first world war. In a few months she was the top star of the Paris "Folies Begeres." She made periodical trips to America, but somehow the Ame ricani public treated her with no thing but a great yawn of indiffer ence. Paris remained her home. As the years went by she became more and more of an exotic and legend ary figure. Now, as one newspaper put it, "she has stormed the Bastille of P.roadway." She arrived with 43 gowns and 46 pieces of luggage gowns by such people as Christian Dior, Jean Desses, Jaques Griffe, Marcel Rochas and Balenciaga. Quaintly, she refers to them as her scenery and wears a different piece for almost every number. Josephine has now gone all Euro pean. Her dancing and singing have a subtlety seldom seen in this country. She is now 45,' and as ravishing and as volatile as ever. Once again, we have an example of how the old generation of the 'twenties still has an artistry of its own that the young people of the fifties cannot touch. Miss Baker is giving the Strand Theatre on Broadway some of the best houses in its history. Already Hollywood producers are asking her to come their way. She sings in French, Italian and Spanish. Al though" audiences here have little idea what she sings about, they don't care very much, it just sounds wonderful to them. She gained much of her fame in the old days through a shrewd sense of publicity. In the 'twenties she used to travel round Paris with a young leopard on a leash. N ONE occasion when she was in her box at the theatre, this leopard jumped over the railing into the audience, tore one woman's dress to shreds and threw the crowd into a panic. Josephine is reported to have said: "Come back to me honey. Where, you all think you goin', may Ahb ask. Oh, ma dear, what shall Ah; do." The leopard was more terri fied than anybody, and finally hid under a seat. In 1928, she went on tour in Europe. In Vienna, the churches were horrified at her .brief dancing attire, and carried on church ser vices for three. days "to atone for these outrages against morality." Outside her theatre a young danc er, according to the "New York Times," shot himself all for love of Josephine. Much the same thing happened in Zagreb, Yugoslavia, where a drafts man, aged 21, who had been send ing her love letters and bouquets stabbed himself as she left a night club, and fell at her feet. But her real success was in Paris, where she danced the Charleston and the Black Bottom. She was the embodiment of the Parisian's idea of the negro of the jazz era. In her memoirs she wrote that in her first nine years in Paris she re ceived 40,000 love letters and 1912 offers of marriage. (She accepted three. First an Italian, then a Frenchman, and now she is mar nied to another Frenchman, Joe Bouillon, her music director.) She; received such gifts as two limous-j ines, 35 strings of pearls nine sets of panther cubs, one live bear, seven, Persian cats, furs (she doesn't re member how many), sets of ruby and emerald bracelets for her arms, wrists and ankles, and finally sev eral rings with diamonds as big- as eggs. Through out the war she sang for Altied soldiers in North Africa, and be came a lieutenant in the Air Aux iItary of the Free French Forces.
