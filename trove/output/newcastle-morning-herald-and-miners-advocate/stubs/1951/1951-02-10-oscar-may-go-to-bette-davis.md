Oscar Mayr Go To Bette Davis From KEITH DUNSTAN in Hollywood Bette Davis, the remarkable lady, who at 42 still provides as many headlines as Errol Flynn and Princess Ali Khan, has reached the peak of her career. In 1950, she was Holly wood's highest paid actress, with an income of 364,000 dollars. The New York film critics who are more often right than anyone else with their Academy Award predictions, gave her their vote as 1950's best actress for her part in "All About Eve." Dark horses sometimes come along, but her only obvious rival is Gloria Swanson, who made a comeback in "Sunset Boulevard." Some people are betting that sentimental Hollywood will give her the award. It is rather curious, even a com pliment to Hollywood, that Bette Davis should be the top actress. One executive complained: "All she has is her intelligence. Her smile is crooked, her cranium is the wrong shape, her mouth's too small, her eyes too large and neck too long. As for sex appeal: it just doesn't exist." The story goes, when she first arrived in Hollywood 21 years ago, the studio's representative could not find her on the railway station be cause she looked just like anybody else you would see on a railway station. Almost Failed Universal put her to work, and sent in requests for pictures of what they called her "gams," suggested her names be changed to Bettina Dawes (they thought that was a natural), and she be test-kissed by 15 men in one afternoon. After more than a year, she had done little else, but make one poor film. She was about to return to New York as another Hollywood failure, when George Arliss asked for her to play his leading lady in "The Man Who Played God." She was so good in that she went straight to stardom, and has been playing wicked, bad-tempered ladies ever since. In 1950, she stdrmily left her studio, Warner Brothers, and equal ly stormily left her third husband William Grant Sherry. She married her fourth husband, Gary Merrill, immediately after the divorce. According to her latest bio graphy, "she likes attention, hates cooking, is vital, easily bored, has an alert mind and is unpredictable." The film got bad re views, boycotts, and a very poor run throughout America. NNA ALBERGHETTI, a shy 13-year-old girl. from Italy, will make her Ame rican film debut opposite the great Bing Crosby. She will sing "Cara Nome" from Verdi's "Rigoletto" in Bing's next film "Here Comes the Groom." Anna Maria, five feet tall and weighing 95 pounds, arrived in New York almost unannounced last April. She sang at Carnegie Hall and won unanimous praise from the hard boiled New York critics. She can send her coloratura notes three and one-half notes to G over high C. Her father is limiting her appear ances so as not to strain her voice. In "Here Comes the Groom" she plays the role of a blind war or phan brought to the United States by Mr. Crosby, a war correspond ent. She sings the aria at a tryout arranged by Crosby. No one realises she is blind till she stumbles against a table. When the sequence was filmed Frank Capra described it as one of the most effective scenes he had ever made. Even cameramen and studio hands who had been on the job for many years were moved. THE fact that Peter Lawford is in Australia does not worry the columnists here one bit. They still have plenty of stories about him. One eminent gossip writer said that instead of Sharman Doug las, he had a new girl friend in Australia. He was taking her out in temperatures of 109 by day and 105 by night. There is some doubt on whether Rita Hayworth will make a film while she is here. According to a columnist, Erskine Johnson, she wants to make another film like "Gilda," with Glenn Ford as her leading man. SEVERAL stars have been in strife.
