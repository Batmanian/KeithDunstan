Film Enchanted The Censors From KEITH DUNSTAN in New York. England once again has made a great sac rifice for dollars and prestige in the United States. "Tales of Hoffmann," the most bally hooed English film since "Hamlet," had its world premiere, not in London, but here in New York. Americans are usually rather blase about English films, and give them little else, but a polite nod and a stuffy out-of the-way theatre on upper Man hattan. But "Tales of Hoffmann" had half-page advertisements jn the newspapers months before it arrived and it had its pre miere not in a common little theatre, but in the distinguished Metropolitan Opera House. Tickets were 100 dollars each for the American Red Cross. After this honour. it went to the expen sive Bijou Theatre by Times Square. It is not the custom here to buy picture tickets in advance, but at the Bijou every seat for every night and every matinee is booked out for the next six weeks. Even "Red Shoes." which ran for two years two months, didn't open as well as this. Perhaps what startled people here so much was the way Emeric Press burger and Michael Powell, its pro ducers, spent so much money and talent on this film. After all, Americans expect the British to. be conservative, and never, never to attempt either the Sigantic or the colossal. But "Tales of Hoffmann," a fantastic opera which took Offen bach 30 years to write, takes up two hours 20 minutes of film, about the same time that it does on thle stage. The film has 300 singers, actors, ballet dancers and musicians, in cluding Moira Shearer, Ludmilla Teherina, Robert Helpmann, Fred erick Ashton, Pamela Brown (one of the world's greatest stage ac tresses), Leonide Massine, Robert Rounseville, Ann Ayars and Sir Thomas Beecham conducting the Royal Philharmonic Orchestra. E MERIC PRESS SBURGER call ed a Press con ference here to tell reporters all about it. He wouldn't say how much it cost, but he did say it was cheaper than "Red Shoes." "This is really a bigger produc tion," he said. "But we saved a lot of money. on what we had earned about movie ballet in 'Red hoes.' We thought we could use ballet dancers the same way as we se movie actors, taking one shot fter another. "Moira Shearer fell to the floor in exhaustion and could not stop sobbing hysterically. We then ound that we had to have long waits while dancers recovered their strength between takes. That was expensive. Now we use the set and cameras for other scenes while the dancers rest." Mr. Pressburger explained it was probably the only film ever made around a sound track. Sir Thomas and his singers first recorded the entire opera. Then a fresh cast of actors and singers went through their parts, moving their lips in time to the music. You will live a long, long time before you see a more remarkable array of colour and action as is packed into "Tales of Hioffmann." It is no straight screening of opera. but in parts almost Disney-gone vild with weird, crazy scenery. Moira Shearer dances on a vine into the sky; later, as a broken ol, she loses her head and red prings sprout from her lovely hair. And the scenery ranges from the modernisms of Dali and Cocteau to the ancient columns of Greece. In fact, this film is so packed with good things it is like going into a cake shop and eating 200 chocolate buns. eclairs and tarts. After all, watching Moira Shearer is splendid any time. Her dancing is beautiful. But while doing some of her remarkable pirouettes, she is also singing an Offenbach aria, and in the background she has one of the most dazzling technicolor sets you ever saw. You can't take it all in at once. and you don't-know whether to go for the buns, eclairs or tarts. Anyhow, the supreme compliment came from the British censor. -e sent a note to Mr. Pressburger. saying he and his three assistants were so enchanted they forgot all about their jobs. Censoring was something they didn't think about. V ETERAN. film producer Sam Goldwyn said regretfully this week that the Ame rican film industry was ruining itself with its over-dramatic, stupid advertising. He said: "Hollywood as a whole has failed to realise there is a point long since passed when words like 'sensational,' 'colossal,' 'epic,' 'magnificent,' 'tremendous' and the like lose their power to persuade and have no meaning whatever. "The public to-day is infinitely more sophisticated than it has ever been in the past. Misleading ad vertising has reached the point where it is self-defeating. All of the people simply refuse to believe motion picture advertising, so that even honest advertisements are look ed at with scepticism by the fed up public." He added: "Our advertising is beginning to give our customers indigestion, and we should do some thing about it before the indigestion turns to ulcers."
