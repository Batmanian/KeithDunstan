"Billy's" Niche In St. Paul's By KEITH DUNSTAN, from London So "King Billy" is to go into St.. Paul's. So William Morris Hughes is to be remembered with England's great John Donne, Nelson, Wellington, Jellicoe. Lawrence. It is an immense thought. St. Paul's is London, as St. Peter's is Rome, as the Krem lin is Moscow. All the strength of England seems to be in that mighty dome, and without it London would be like Trafal gar Square without Nelson empty. It is fascinating to think that the thousands-no, tmil lions, of people--who will tramp through St. Paul's this century, the next, and the one after that, will see the plaque to "Billy's" memory, along with the one to Florence Nightingale, Anthony Van Dyck, Sir Arthur Sullivan, William Blake and Hubert Parry. He will be the first Austra lian to be so honoured. A verger took me down the stone, foot-worn steps, deep into the crypt. He was dress ed in a black cassock with a rope around his ample sto mach. He was like a genial monk, a modern Fra Lippo Lippl. "Mr. Hughes's plaque'll go down there," he said. "They don't put plaques or statues upstairs. Stopped all that. You can imagine what the place would be like in 1000 years or so; wouldn't be able to move for, marble." The crypt, dimly lit wit:. fat columns to support the mass above, seemed to extend for 100 yards either way, into darkness. We hurried past the grace Iul black casket, which Henry VIII., in his meanness, stole from Cardinal Wolsey. It lay unused at Windsor for 300 years till the body of Lord Nelson was brought back from Gibraltar in a keg of spirits. The victor at Trafalgar camn tp the Thames, his funeral a magnificent procession of barges. He now rests in this casket. I WAS thinking of England's better days, when expense seemed to be no object, and suddenly I saw the most hideous, the most extra ordinary, thing in London. It was the'almost-forgotten "triumphal car" used to carry the body of the Duke of We! lington, 100 years ago, in Lon don's greatest funeral. It is difficult to describe this thing. It has six iron wheels. and the cast-iron frame is gorgeously decorated with leaf-work, lion heads, spears, and noble-look ing women. It weighs 18 tons, the size of a large tram. How they got it into the crypt is a mys tery. When the Duke died, the Government decided: What could be more fitting than to lmelt down the guns of Water 1oo and make a triumphal car? It took shifts of 100 men 18 days to get it ready. They draped it in velvet, decorated it with guns, swords, spikes, and the whole astonishing affair was drawn by 12 black-plumed black horses. They only cost a couple of hundred quid. "Well, people haven't the money any more. What would a statue cost now?" The verger wiped his forehead. "Golly, I hate to think."
