As far as this city is con cerned, he has the stage by the ears. His play, "The Love of Four Colonels," is being pro duced in 14 European coun tries, and it has juit opened in New York. "The Love of Four Col onels" is a world-wide hit, almost that is, but there is no suggestion of it or any other Peter Ustinov play going to Australia. Australia will have to wait, a long time probably. Your only chance of ac tually seeing Mr. Ustinov is on films. Do you remember him as Nero in "Quo Vadis," slobbering, crying, gloating madly, moaning over his fiddle, his contorted face crim son in the reflection of burn ing Rome? His brilliance there stole the show: HE IS LIKE a small boy who suddenly has dis covered two remarkable talents -he can whistle and he can snap his fingers. So he does both at once, and the racket is terrible. Peter Ustinov's bag of tal, ents is so impressive a critic remarked: "It wouldn't sur prise me a bit if he suddenly took the baton from Sit Thomas Beecham one night and started to conduct the Royal Philharmonic." He has written a dozen plays and acted in most of them. He paints, broadcasts, lectures, appears on television and, at the moment, he Is working on two plays and two novels. He is the sort of man who has 20 ideas in the bath in the morning and spends the rest of the day bursting to put them into effect His bust is now in the Royal Academy, London's symbol of solid achievement. His zest for satire Is both his brilliance and handicap, Critics complain he loads his plays with witty satirical por traits and the overall effect makes for a wonderful even ing's entertainment, but there is no cohesion and it is not great writing. But Peter Ustinov is con temptuous of the critics and when the reviews are unkind he says: "Anyway, I'm not even English. So at 16 he was translating Maxim Gorky for the Lon. don stage, at 20 his plays were being performed privately, at 21 he had his first, big Lon don opening. His only other career was the Army, he says "I just wasn't any good at it. I rose, after many struggles,, to the rank of private, third-class smoking." Now at 32 he's a veteran and building a fortune, a man who can write keeping a wea ther eye on all possibilities of revenue, fiction, stage, radio, television and films. He lives in an 18th century house in Chelsea, filled with gilt sofas, elaborate ornaments and lamp brackets.
