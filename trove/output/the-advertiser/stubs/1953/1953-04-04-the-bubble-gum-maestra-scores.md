TheBubbleGum Maestra Scores Bv KEITH DUNSTAN LONDON. — / have just been watching the mighty London Philhar monic Orchestra being put in its place by a snippet of a girl aged eight (Sig norina Giannella de Marco of Pescara, Italy), named by some as (tthe bubble gum maestra' or 'the doll genius.' In an even match, Giannella versus the LPO, Giannella eventu ally won easily. They don't like child pro iigies in this town. Musi cians who have slaved 30 year's or more to be a part of a top world orchestra be come downright annoyed when they are told to take orders from an infant of eight. The orchestra leader (An drew Cooper) was as de pressed as anybody. He thought of his own eight year-old daughter taking over the orchestra and he didn't like the thought. But in Giannella's first London concert the au dience of 7,000 shouted and screamed their approval. At the finish they brought her back 12 times. Now Giannella was prov ing she was no publicity stunt. She didn't like the way the 'cello was playing an opening passage in 'In- vitation to the Waltz.' Six times he tried and still she wasn't satisfied., 'Lente, Lente,''she shril ed and putting her hands on her hips, threw back her lead in disgust and stamped her foot. The 'cello played it slower and it did sound better. In the great darkness of the Royal Albert Hall little Giannella jumped up and down on the podium like a puppet on a string. She con ducted not only with her bands but her whole body and when she didn't get what she wanted she brought the orchestra to a screeching halt. During Haydn she sucked bubble gum, and all through Beethoven there was an omi nous bulge in her cheek. An elderly harpist said, 'I've seen Beecham do some funny things and I've seen Toscanini go berserk but that's the first time I ever saw a conductor chew toffee through all four movements of the 5th sym phony.' Her Story During a rehearsal break a promoter told Giannella's story. Four years ago her mother and father came home one night to find Gian aella conducting an imagin ary orchestra to music com ing from the radio. At first they thought it was just a childish game but Madame de Marco wasn't so sure. She took Giannella to a teacher and they soon found it was no childish game.* She made her debut at four and a half, and now just four years later she has conducted 123 concerts in Italy, Spain, South America and Paris. It was nearly lunch time and the rehearsal was over. Giannella, bustling, impa tient, was itching to get back to the hotel. She had bought a baby pram and a big pink doll. Mamma had refused to let her bring the doll to the rehearsal. Hence the impa tience. As I left the Albert Hall my last glimpse was of a very little girl, making faces at a photographer and sur rounded by a bevy of admir ing women, who were edging competitively to get into the picture. I heard one of them say, 'You must meet her. She adores violinists — they're her favorites.' Giannella de Marco, weaving baton and with a rapt expression, rehearses the London Philharmonic Orchestra.
