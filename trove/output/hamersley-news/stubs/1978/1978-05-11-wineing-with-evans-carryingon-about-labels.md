WINEING with Evans CARRYING-ON ABOUT LABELS It was Keith Dunstan, the famous Melb- ourne writer, who once pointed out how long-winded Australian wine labels were becoming. He made reference to such things as 'grapes grown in sun-enriched soils fan- ned in the late afternoon by the cool breezes of. the Indian Ocean'....'picked by happy non-union members....', as against the simple 'Chateau Latour 1962'. I tended to agree with him, though I did point out that many Australian labels were honestly informative and did much to guide the genuine wine lover. Wine nomenclature is a difficult thing, and Australia hasn't come to grips with the problem yet. We still use the words Champ- agne, Chablis, Sauternes, Graves, and even recently I saw the introduction of Beaujol- ais. Now all these are areas of France, and I for one would be most upset if they started using the Hunter and Barossa names over there. Perhaps our lack of control and the exuber- ance of our information has led to too much talk of wine, particularly the wrong sort of talk. Some of the words we use have, after all, nothing to do with wine. I use the word 'elegant' a lot. Do you know what I mean? Top hat, white tie and tails? Does 'sturdy' mean corduroy and boots? And 'honest', chopping down a cherry tree? There's one famous story of a man in Melbourne who said a wine was intimate. 'What the hell do you mean by that? Do you you mean it's going to jump out of the glass and make love to you?' an irate friend in- quired. Though I'm given to understand he didn't quite use those words. I've always thought a domestic wine is something you use on your tiles to make them whiter and brighter than ever before. Then there's an angular wine. Will itt stain your shirt front in perfect isosceles triangles? The mind boggles at feminine wines, though I suppose one is referring to softness and smoothness. There's even further predica- ment with masculine wine, specially if it falls off at the finish. The greatest story qf them all, and the one that shows us how unaffected we can be, comes from Jim Hardy, the NSW director of Thomas Hardy and Sons, who was once asked at an August gathering his opinion of a great vintage. He picked up the glass, examined the colour, sniffed it for a long — time, sipped some, ran it round his mouth, sucked some air through it, and finally, swallowed it, allowing it to linger off the back of his tongue. 'Well', he proclaimed, 'it's very easy on the gums'. I heard about a lady who found a bee in a wine bottle (was it beefuddled?), and I once found the end of a bottling nozzle. However, these are rare examples. Most Australian wine-making is conducted with the very best international equipment and standards of hygiene and care are extremely high. Yet matter which does appear in bottles from time to time does upset the consumer. The point is, it shouldn't. Most Australian white wines are brilliantly clean, because that's how the customer wants them. Yet occasionally one sees a white crystal in the bottom of the bottle, one which has a snow-storm appearance when agitated. This is the cream of tartar crystal, and it doesn't harm the wine one bit. Most makers remove it before bottling by a process called cold stabilisation. By lower- ing the temperature of the wine for a period the cream of tartar (potassium bi-tartrate) is thrown out of suspension and crystal ises. If they don't do this it can happen when you store the bottle in the refrigerator. Simply, pour the wine off the crystal. More complicated is the crusting of a red. Again, often after some years, the cream of tartar crystal ises. When this happens it can take some pigments from the colour with it, and also, sometimes, a little protein. All these things make up the crust of the red wine, which is quite common in old vintages. Before a dinner party, either stand the bottle up for a couple of days, and gently pour off the crust, or leave it lying down and decant. This is not complicated. Carefully raise one end slightly, remove the cork, hold fc |g over a lit candle, and pour into another bottle or decanter, until the crust appears over the flame. In fact, this little ritual is one of the pleasures of wine.
